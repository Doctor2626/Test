Start here

Welcome

Renovating a brownstone or rowhouse is a big project, and most of the stress comes from not knowing what happens next. This guide walks through a full renovation the way Emory Street Construction runs one: the order of the work, the approvals, the choices you'll make and when, what we build with, and how we finish and hand the house back to you.

It describes how we normally work. Your contract, your architect's drawings and the decisions we make together on your job govern your project. Where something here differs from those, they win, and your project manager will tell you.

Three things matter most for a smooth job:

  • Decide early. Windows, cabinets, steel and some tile take months to arrive. A choice made late stops the job.
  • Expect what's behind the walls. Old houses hide rot, past repairs and old wiring. We plan for it and price the common repairs up front.
  • Let the inspections run. Nothing gets covered until it passes. That's what protects you for as long as you own the house.
02

The big picture

How a renovation runs

A full gut renovation of a three- or four-story rowhouse usually runs about ten to eleven months from signed contract to move-in. The first two months are planning, approvals and ordering. Building starts once the permit is issued. Underpinning, a facade rebuild or a long approval can add one to three months.

The phases

  1. Planning, approvals and ordering. Your architect finishes the drawings and files for approvals and the building permit. We finalize the schedule and order the items that take longest to arrive.
  2. Protection and setup. We protect the sidewalk, your neighbors' walls and anything in the house that stays, and set up the dumpster, temporary power and site office.
  3. Demolition. Any asbestos or lead is handled first by licensed specialists. Then we take the house down to its structure, top floor first, saving the historic pieces you want to keep.
  4. Foundations and structure. Repairs to the foundation, new footings, steel beams where walls come out, and repairs to the old floor joists. This is where most hidden problems show up.
  5. Roof, windows and masonry. We get the house weathertight as early as we can, then repoint and repair the brick or brownstone.
  6. Plumbing, heating, cooling and electrical. The pipes, ducts and wiring go in while the walls are open. Each trade has its own inspection.
  7. Insulation and walls. After the inspections pass, we insulate, seal the house against drafts and close the walls.
  8. Finishes. Tile, cabinets, stairs, floors, trim and paint, then fixtures, lights and appliances.
  9. Final inspections and handover. Final inspections, your walkthrough and punch list, cleaning, and the paperwork that lets you move back in.

Historic districts

Much of downtown Jersey City is in a historic district. There, the city's Historic Preservation Commission must approve exterior work before it starts. We don't order front windows, doors or facade materials until that approval is in hand, because the Commission can require changes.

Your contract

New Jersey requires home improvement contracts over $500 to be in writing and to carry the contractor's state registration number. You have until midnight of the third business day after receiving your copy to cancel for any reason. Your contract sets out the scope, the price, the payment schedule, the allowances for items you haven't chosen yet, and the prices for common hidden repairs (see Section 07).

Plumbing, electrical and HVAC work on your house is done by New Jersey licensed contractors, and each trade's work is permitted and inspected separately.

04

During the work

Your house, your belongings and your neighbors

Moving out

For a full renovation, you'll need to live somewhere else for the whole job. We don't run full renovations with the family in the house. Dust, open walls, live wiring work and shut-off utilities make it unsafe, and it adds months to the schedule. If moving out isn't possible, talk to us early about a smaller scope or a later start.

What we protect and save

  • Anything that stays (a stair, mantels, original floors, windows) is covered and protected before demolition starts.
  • We walk the house with you and tag the historic pieces you want to keep or reuse: mantels, moldings, doors, newel posts, hardware. They're removed carefully and stored.
  • Older houses are assumed to contain lead paint. All work follows lead-safe rules, and any asbestos is removed by a licensed contractor before general demolition.

Your neighbors

Rowhouses share walls, so your renovation affects the houses next door. Before work starts:

  • We photograph and video the neighbors' shared walls and facades so everyone has a record of their condition.
  • We put small crack monitors on the shared walls and check them regularly.
  • When we underpin foundations, lower a cellar or dig near a neighbor's foundation, we add vibration monitors.
  • Any work that needs access to a neighbor's property waits for a signed access agreement. New Jersey has no law that gives a builder automatic access to the house next door.

We tell neighbors before noisy or disruptive work and keep the sidewalk clean and safe. Debris is bagged and carried out by hand to the dumpster or our trucks, never thrown down a chute or stockpiled on your floors.

06

Quality you can't see later

What we build with, and why

Most of what makes a renovation last ends up behind the walls. These are the methods and materials ESC uses as standard on a brownstone or rowhouse, and the reason for each. Your drawings and specifications may call for something different; if so, we build to them.

Structure

  • Old floor joists are reinforced with full-length new lumber set to a laser line, so floors come out flat. Where too many are rotted or burned, we replace the floor.
  • New floors are engineered joists sized stiffer than code minimum, so tile doesn't crack and floors don't bounce.
  • Steel beams replace removed bearing walls when the span calls for them, designed by a licensed engineer.
  • Foundations are underpinned in small sections, in sequence, so the house and your neighbors' houses stay supported the whole time.

Roof, windows and masonry

  • Flat roofs get a fully adhered single-ply membrane over insulation that's tapered so water runs to the drains. We never use torch-applied roofing on a wood-framed house.
  • Brick and brownstone are repointed with lime mortar matched to the original. Modern hard mortar traps water and cracks old brick, so we don't use it on historic facades.
  • Historic front windows are restored with storms added, or replaced with units that match the original profiles, as the Preservation Commission allows.
  • Cellars get an interior drain, a membrane behind the new walls and a sealed sump pump with battery backup. Every new slab gets a capped radon pipe, so a fan can be added later if a test ever calls for one.

Plumbing

  • Cast iron drain stacks carry waste through the house. They're far quieter than plastic, which matters in a bedroom below a bathroom.
  • Water lines are PEX with a valved manifold at each bathroom behind an access panel, so one bathroom can be shut off without shutting down the house.
  • Hot water comes from a high-efficiency heat-pump water heater sized for the number of baths.
  • Showers in primary baths get thermostatic valves that hold temperature when someone flushes a toilet.

Heating, cooling and fresh air

  • Heat pumps heat and cool the house, with one system per floor or two so each zone is comfortable. In rowhouses we use compact units that fit in closets and soffits.
  • Fresh air comes from a balanced ventilation system that brings in filtered outdoor air and exhausts the baths and laundry, so a well-sealed house still breathes.
  • Original radiators can be kept with a new boiler if you'd like, paired with separate cooling.

Electrical

  • A 200-amp service on every full renovation, larger when an electric car charger and heat pumps call for it.
  • All old knob-and-tube wiring in our scope is removed.
  • Every switch box is wired so you can add smart switches later without opening walls.
  • Lighting is warm white with high color quality, so wood, stone and skin tones look right.

Walls, tile, millwork and floors

  • Walls get a smooth Level 4 finish, upgraded to Level 5 in stair halls and rooms with skylights, where raking light shows every flaw. Mineral wool insulation goes in the walls around bedrooms and baths to keep sound down.
  • Showers are fully waterproofed under the tile and flood-tested for 24 hours before any tile goes on.
  • Tile floors go over a crack-isolating membrane on a stiff subfloor.
  • Trim is solid poplar where it's painted, and never MDF in kitchens, baths or anywhere that gets wet-mopped.
  • Wood floors are solid oak, sanded and finished on site. Original floors are patched with matching boards when they're worth keeping.
  • Original stairs are restored whenever the structure is sound.
07

Built right, checked twice

Inspections and surprises behind the walls

Inspections

The city inspects the work at each stage. Nothing gets covered, poured or closed up until the inspection passes. We also check our own work before every inspection.

  1. Before demolition. Asbestos clearance and utility shut-offs.
  2. Footings and foundations. The excavation and steel before any concrete is poured.
  3. Structure. Steel, beams and framing, with the engineer where required.
  4. Rough plumbing, gas, electrical and fire. Each trade's pipes and wiring while the walls are open.
  5. Insulation. Insulation and air sealing before drywall.
  6. Finals. Each trade's final inspection, an air-tightness test where required, then the final building inspection.

The city schedules inspections within three business days of our written request, so some waiting between stages is normal and is built into the schedule.

Surprises behind the walls

Every old house hides something: joists rotted at the ends, lintels that have rusted, past repairs that were never right, old wiring. Here's how we handle it.

  • Common repairs are priced in your contract. Joist end repairs, reinforcing joists, lintel replacement and repointing carry unit prices, so you know the cost before we find them.
  • Everything else gets a written change order with the price and any effect on the schedule. You sign it before the work is done.
  • Safety comes first. If we find something unsafe, such as a failed beam or a gas leak, we make it safe the same day, tell you and the engineer, and send the change order within two working days.

After demolition

Once the house is open, we walk every floor with you, the architect and the engineer. That's when the real condition of the structure is known and the final structural plan is confirmed.

08

Staying informed

How we keep you informed

  • One point of contact. Your project manager handles your questions, decisions and changes.
  • Weekly update. A short written report each week: what was done, what's next, decisions coming due, and photos.
  • Site visits. You're welcome on site by appointment with your project manager. For your safety, please don't visit alone or bring children.
  • Decisions in writing. Selections and changes are confirmed in writing so nothing depends on memory.
  • Questions for your architect about the design go through a logged question-and-answer process, so the answer reaches the crew in the field.
09

Moving back in

Finishing and handover

  1. Final inspections. Each trade's final, then the final building inspection.
  2. Your walkthrough. We walk the house with you (and your architect) and list anything that needs finishing or fixing: the punch list. We complete it promptly.
  3. Cleaning. Protection comes off and the house is cleaned top to bottom.
  4. Certificate. The city issues a Certificate of Approval for a renovation (or a Certificate of Occupancy for a new house). You can move back in once it's issued.
  5. Turnover package. Manuals and warranties for every appliance and system, a list of every paint color, tile, fixture and finish so repairs can be matched years later, extra tile, flooring and paint, and the keys and codes.

Warranty

ESC warrants its workmanship for the period in your contract, usually one year from substantial completion. Manufacturer warranties on windows, equipment and appliances pass to you; register them after turnover. For a warranty request, email your project manager with photos and the room.

New houses settle in their first year. Small nail pops, hairline cracks at drywall joints and doors that need adjusting are normal, and we come back to take care of them.
