Framing & Structure

How ESC frames floors, walls and roofs, sets beams and posts, and repairs the joists and pockets of old masonry rowhouses, with the different framing systems we use and when each one makes sense. Lead carpenters, framing subs, steel erectors, PMs and owners who want to understand the bones of their house all use this chapter.

4.1 Where framing sits in the job

Framing starts once the foundation can take load (Chapter 03) or, in a gut renovation, once demolition has left a clean, shored shell (Chapter 02). It ends when the structure is plumb, level, sheathed, strapped, fireblocked and has passed the framing inspection. Windows need square openings, tile needs stiff floors, cabinets need flat walls and stairs need correct floor-to-floor heights, so everything after depends on it.

On a new house, framing is one fast campaign: sills, deck, walls, next floor, roof, sheathing. On a rowhouse it runs floor by floor: set steel, rebuild pockets, sister and level joists, frame openings, then partitions, in the order the engineer's shoring plan allows.

Code references are to the IRC 2021 as the model baseline. Local amendments and the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) govern, and where the engineer's drawings ask for more than code, the drawings win. On rowhouses permitted under a local existing-building code, the engineer's repair details govern.

4.2 Before framing starts

The PM confirms all of these before the first lumber delivery.

  • Current stamped structural drawings on site, with beam, header, post and connector schedules.
  • Shop drawings for steel, floor trusses, roof trusses or SIP panels are approved, with delivery dates confirmed (lead times in 4.5 and 4.6).
  • New construction: foundation walls cured and backfilled or braced, anchor bolts and hold-down anchors set per plan and surveyed, foundation inspection passed (Chapter 03).
  • Rowhouse: demolition and abatement complete, shoring signed off by the engineer, party walls exposed and documented (Chapter 02).
  • Existing joists probed and mapped floor by floor: rot, notches, fire damage, sag.
  • Laser benchmark set on every floor, with a reference elevation tied to the finished floor height on the drawings.
  • Mechanical coordination meeting held (Chapters 06, 07, 08): chases, drain runs and joist penetrations agreed.
  • Window rough openings confirmed against the window order (Chapter 05).
  • Staging plan for tight lots: crane or hoist day, sidewalk permits, lumber storage.

4.3 Framing systems compared

Most of what ESC builds is either platform framing on new work or a repair of whatever an old house was built with. The other systems show up when the design, energy goals or schedule ask for them.

Platform framing builds each floor as a deck and stands walls on it, so every floor line is a natural fireblock. Balloon framing runs studs continuously from sill to roof plate with joists hung on let-in ribbons; we only repair it, in old frame houses and some rowhouse rear additions, and its open full-height stud bays are the first thing we fireblock. Advanced framing (OVE) stacks 2x6 studs, joists and rafters at 24 in. o.c. with two-stud corners and right-sized headers. SIPs, light-gauge steel and mass timber show up when the design, the energy target, a noncombustible requirement or the schedule asks for them.

4.4 Rowhouse structure: what we find and how we fix it

A typical brownstone or brick rowhouse has 12 in. to 16 in. brick party walls carrying the floors. Joists span party wall to party wall, usually 16 to 22 ft, and the originals are often rough-sawn, full-dimension 3x8 to 3x12 at 16 in. to 24 in. o.c., set in pockets in the brick. There's often a girder on piers in the cellar and sometimes a bearing partition near the stair. After a century these floors sag 1 in. to 2 in. at midspan and have been notched by every plumber who worked in the house. Our job is to make them level, stiff and sound without taking the house apart.

Options for existing joists

We can sister, level over, replace or supplement the old joists. A sister only carries end shear if it bears on the same supports as the original, which in a rowhouse means a new pocket or a hanger on a bolted ledger. We crown and set sisters to a laser line, not to the sagging original, so the sister does the leveling.

ESC default: full-length sisters set to a laser line, bearing in rebuilt pockets or in hangers on a bolted ledger, with structural screws or through-bolts per the engineer. We switch to full replacement when more than about a third of a floor's joists are rotted or burned, and to a midspan beam when the floor will carry stone tile or a heavy tub and the sistered span still can't meet our deflection target.

Joist pockets in masonry party walls

The IRC requires at least 3 in. of bearing on masonry (R502.6) and decay protection where wood enters masonry: treated or decay-resistant wood, or a 1/2 in. air space at top, sides and end (R317.1). Old pockets are often packed with damp mortar, and the joist end has rotted.

The fire cut bevels the top of the joist end so a burning joist rotates out of the pocket instead of prying the wall outward. We keep it on new joists set in masonry pockets unless the engineer details otherwise. Shims are steel or slate, never wood. Where the neighbor's joists pocket into the same wall, we check the solid brick left between pockets and fill old voids with mortar to keep the fire separation.

Every new floor needs correct bearing, blocking or rim at supports, a glued and fastened subfloor, and a stiffness target set by the finish on top.

ESC default: I-joists for new floors and rowhouse floor rebuilds, sized by the manufacturer's software to our L/480 target. We switch to open-web trusses when the HVAC design (Chapter 07) needs trunk ducts in the floor, and to sawn lumber for sisters, short spans and anything that will be changed in the field.

Stiffness and the finish on top

The IRC sets live-load deflection at L/360 (Table R301.7). Floors that just meet it feel bouncy and crack stone, so ESC designs to L/480 under tile and stiffer where the tile or stone maker requires (Chapter 10). Subfloor is 23/32 in. T&G, glued and screwed or ring-shank nailed, gapped 1/8 in. at panel edges unless the maker says otherwise.

Floor openings and stairs

Cut joists (tails) hang from a header, and the header hangs from full-length trimmers. IRC R502.10 requires doubled headers and trimmers where the header span exceeds 4 ft, hangers at header-to-trimmer connections over 6 ft, and hangers or ledger strips for tails over 12 ft. A rowhouse stair usually runs along a party wall, so the header is long and almost always engineered. Size the opening from the stair layout and confirm 6 ft-8 in. headroom (IRC R311.7.2) with the stair builder before cutting (Chapter 11).

4.6 Beams, headers, posts and the load path

Every load reaches soil through a continuous chain of members and connections. Removing a bearing wall or opening a rear wall reroutes that chain. The engineer designs the members; the crew builds the path exactly, with no gaps under posts, solid blocking through every floor, bearing plates where shown, and nothing relying on drywall or a partition.

ESC default: LVL for headers over 4 ft and for most flush beams, PSL for posts and point-load columns. We use steel W-shapes when the span or headroom puts LVL out of reach, which on rowhouse gut renovations is common at removed center bearing walls and rear wall openings. Flitch plates are a repair tool, used when the engineer details them.

Point loads and posts

Posts stack: third floor over second floor over cellar over footing. Under each post, the floor gets full-depth solid blocking or squash blocks; in I-joist floors, squash blocks are cut 1/16 in. longer than the joist depth so they take the load. Use post caps and post bases per the connector schedule. A new cellar post needs a new footing (Chapter 03); the slab is not a footing.

Rowhouse steel usually bears in new party-wall pockets on a steel plate set in mortar or non-shrink grout, with the brick rebuilt and dry-packed tight to the beam. Shoring stays until the grout reaches the strength the engineer specifies.

4.7 Wall framing

Walls are built flat on the deck and stood up, or in a rowhouse often built in place between floors. Layout drives everything.

  • Studs: 16 in. o.c. from one end so panel edges land on framing. A 92-5/8 in. precut stud with three plates gives a 97-1/8 in. wall for an 8 ft ceiling.
  • Openings: full-height king studs, jacks under each header end, header per IRC Table R602.7(1) or the engineer, flat rough sill, cripples on layout.
  • Double top plate: splices offset at least 24 in. (IRC R602.3.2), with the top plate lapped at corners and bearing-wall intersections to tie walls together.
  • Corners and partitions: three-stud corners, or two-stud corners with drywall clips (OVE); ladder or flat-stud backing at partitions.
  • Blocking: backing for cabinets, grab bars, valves, TV mounts, rails and heavy fixtures goes in now, from the backing plan.

Drilling and notching limits

The lead carpenter checks these limits after every rough-in. Studs in bearing walls may be bored up to 40 percent of the stud width (60 percent if the stud is doubled, with no more than two successive doubled studs bored), with the hole edge at least 5/8 in. from the face (IRC R602.6). Notches in bearing studs are limited to 25 percent of width. For sawn joists, holes are limited to one-third of the depth and kept 2 in. from the top and bottom edges; notches are limited to one-sixth of the depth and not allowed in the middle third of the span (IRC R502.8.1). I-joists, LVLs and trusses follow the manufacturer's hole chart only. If a member is cut past the limit, it gets an engineered repair or replacement, never a field guess.

4.8 Roof framing

A ridge board is only a nailing surface: rafters push the walls outward and ceiling joists or rafter ties resist that thrust (IRC R802.3, R802.5). Remove the ties without an engineered fix and the walls spread. A structural ridge beam carries half the rafter load on posts down to footings, so no ties are needed; it's the method for cathedral ceilings. Trusses span wall to wall, and the permanent bracing on the truss drawings is part of the install. Low-slope rowhouse roofs get slope from tapered sleepers, from joists stepped down toward the rear, or from tapered insulation on a level deck (Chapter 05). Most low-slope membranes need at least 1/4 in. per foot (IRC R905).

4.9 Sheathing, fasteners, hold-downs and fireblocking

Wall sheathing is 7/16 in. OSB or 15/32 in. plywood, edges on framing or blocking, gapped 1/8 in. Low-slope and walkable roofs get 5/8 in. or 3/4 in. sheathing, with panel clips where the span calls for them.

Lateral loads follow a chain too. Sill anchor bolts are 1/2 in. at 6 ft o.c. max., within 12 in. of plate ends, 7 in. embed (IRC R403.1.6), unless the engineer asks for more. Shear wall ends get hold-downs on cast-in anchors or threaded rod, straps tie wall to wall across the rim, and rafters get the ties the design calls for. Every connector gets every specified nail. In rowhouses the floors are diaphragms tying the party walls together, so any straps or anchors the engineer calls for into the masonry go in before the subfloor.

Fireblocking (IRC R302.11) closes concealed spaces at floor and ceiling lines, every 10 ft horizontally, at soffits, at stair stringers and around penetrations, using 2x lumber, 23/32 in. panel, 1/2 in. gypsum or secured mineral wool, with listed sealant at penetrations. Draftstopping (R302.12) limits concealed floor-ceiling spaces, such as open-web trusses, to 1,000 sq ft where there's usable space above and below. Balloon-framed walls and gaps at party walls are where we find the open paths.

4.10 The framing sequence, step by step

New construction

  1. Check the foundation. Shoot top of wall for level and diagonals for square; plan shims where it's more than 1/4 in. off.
  2. Set sills. Sill gasket, treated plate, washers and nuts tight; hold-down anchors protected.
  3. Frame the first deck. Beams, posts, rim and joists on layout, blocking at bearing and point loads, hangers fully nailed, subfloor glued.
  4. Snap and plate walls. Chalk lines from the plans; plates marked for studs, openings, partitions and blocking.
  5. Build and stand walls. Exterior walls first, sheathed flat where practical. Plumb and line them with braces before the next floor goes on.
  6. Repeat per floor. Keep posts over posts and bearing walls over bearing walls.
  7. Roof. Ridge beam or trusses with permanent bracing, sheathing, and underlayment the same day (Chapter 05).
  8. Connectors and straps. Hold-downs, straps, hurricane ties and remaining hangers. Walk every connector against the schedule.
  9. Interior partitions and backing. Frame partitions, soffits and chases; install all blocking from the backing plan.
  10. Punch for inspection. Walk with the plans and fix nailing, straps and plumb before the PM calls the inspection.

Rowhouse gut renovation

  1. Verify shoring. Confirm temporary shoring matches the engineer's shoring plan before any bearing member is removed or cut.
  2. Survey each floor. Laser grid at about 4 ft; record highs and lows to set the target plane.
  3. Set steel and new beams. Cut pockets, grout bearing plates, set steel, bolt nailers, in the engineer's sequence.
  4. Rebuild pockets. Cut out rot; new ends get fire cuts, steel or slate shims and decay protection.
  5. Sister and level. String or laser the target plane, crown sisters up, jack each sister to the line, fasten per the engineer.
  6. Frame openings and stairs. Headers, trimmers and hangers for new stairs, shafts and skylights.
  7. Roof. Sister or replace joists, build slope or a level deck, sheath, roofer in immediately (Chapter 05).
  8. Fireblock old paths. Party wall gaps, chimney chases, balloon-framed bays.
  9. Partitions, backing, punch, inspect. Same as new construction.

ESC standard

No subfloor goes down on a rowhouse floor until the PM has a laser survey showing the framing within tolerance. Planing a joist is cheap; fixing a floor through hardwood is not.

4.12 Inspection holds

Inspection hold

Structural observation before cover. Steel beams, pockets, bearing plates, posts, hold-downs and any engineered repair must be seen by the engineer of record (or their field rep, where the job requires special inspection) before anything covers them. Book it at least three working days ahead and photograph every connection.

Inspection hold

Sheathing nailing. Many AHJs and engineers want to see shear wall nailing before housewrap goes on. Check the permit card. If it's required, no WRB or exterior insulation goes up until it's signed.

Inspection hold

Framing inspection. Called after plumbing, HVAC and electrical rough-ins are in and fireblocking is complete, so the inspector sees the framing as the trades left it. No insulation or drywall until it passes (Chapter 09).

4.13 Common failures and how to avoid them

Common failure

Broken load path. A post lands on a slab with no footing, or blocking is missed under an upper-floor post. Trace every point load on the plans before framing and walk it floor by floor before subfloor.

Common failure

Sisters set to the old sag. The sister is clamped to the old joist and follows its curve, so the floor is stronger but still out of level. Set sisters to a laser line.

Common failure

Over-cut members. A joist notched under a toilet, an I-joist flange cut for a duct. Mark no-cut zones on engineered members and walk the house after every rough-in.

Common failure

Wet lumber closed in. Framing closed in at 22 percent moisture shrinks, pops nails, cracks tile and opens trim joints. Dry in fast and meter before insulation.

Common failure

Missing backing or connector nails. Backing is found missing when the cabinet installer arrives; hangers have short nails or empty holes. Keep a backing plan from the shop drawings and fixture specs, keep the right connector nails on site in labeled boxes, and check both on the pre-inspection walk.

Common failure

Open fire paths in old houses. Balloon-framed bays, party wall gaps and old chimney chases left open. Give fireblocking its own walk-through.

4.14 Framing QC checklist

  • Beams, headers, posts and connectors match the schedule and are photographed with stamps visible.
  • Every point load traced to a footing, blocked solid at every floor.
  • Joist bearing meets the minimums; pockets rebuilt with steel or slate shims, fire cuts and decay protection.
  • Floors surveyed with a laser grid and within tolerance; survey saved to the project file.
  • Walls plumb and straight within tolerance; tile and cabinet walls checked to the tighter number.
  • Rough openings match the window and door schedule, square within 1/8 in. on diagonals.
  • Sheathing nailing per schedule; no overdriven nails through the panel skin; shear wall edge nailing complete.
  • Hold-downs, straps, hurricane ties and hangers installed with the correct nails in every hole.
  • Holes and notches within code or manufacturer limits; any over-cut member repaired per engineer.
  • Fireblocking and draftstopping complete, including penetrations sealed with listed products.
  • Backing installed per plan and photographed with a tape in frame.
  • Framing moisture content at or below 15 percent before insulation.

4.15 Handoff to the next trades

Framing hands the house to the envelope crew (Chapter 05) and to the mechanical trades (Chapters 06, 07 and 08), who often work in parallel. When framing leaves a floor:

  • The structure is complete and inspected, or ready for the framing inspection as soon as rough-ins are in.
  • Roof is sheathed and dried in; window and door openings are sized, square and ready for flashing.
  • Mechanical chases, soffits and duct routes are framed where coordinated, with no-cut zones marked on engineered members.
  • Backing is in for every fixture, cabinet and accessory on the backing plan.
  • The laser benchmark and finished floor elevations are marked on each floor for the trades that follow.
  • The floor is broom clean and stacked materials are off the deck.
  • The PM has the photo set of every beam, connection, pocket, hold-down and backing location.

After rough-ins, the lead carpenter fixes anything the trades cut, closes out fireblocking and the PM calls the framing inspection. Insulation and drywall (Chapter 09) start only after it passes.
