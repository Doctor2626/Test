Division 02 · Existing Conditions

Demolition & Abatement

How ESC surveys, abates, strips, shores and clears a building before new work starts, from a single-room tear-out to a full gut of a masonry rowhouse. Field leads, PMs, demo and abatement subs, and owners who want to know what happens in the first weeks of a job all use this chapter.

2.1 Where demolition sits in the job

Demolition is the first physical work on site and the last chance to learn what the building is really made of before design is locked. On a new house it's short: clear the lot, remove old structures, cap utilities. On a rowhouse it decides the rest of the schedule, because the house finally shows us its rot, old fires and bad framing.

Demo is two jobs: hazard removal (surveys, licensed abatement, lead-safe practice) and controlled removal of finishes, systems and the structure shown for removal on the drawings while the building stays up and the neighbors stay clean.

Permits and schedule are Chapter 01; footings and underpinning, Chapter 03; permanent beams and headers that replace what we shore here, Chapter 04. This chapter ends when the shell is clean, documented and shored.

Codes and regulations cited here are the federal baseline plus the New Jersey rules that sit on top of it. Federal: OSHA 29 CFR 1926 (Subpart T for demolition), EPA 40 CFR Part 745 for lead (the RRP rule), and EPA asbestos NESHAP at 40 CFR Part 61 Subpart M. New Jersey: the Uniform Construction Code (N.J.A.C. 5:23) with the IRC 2024 (NJ edition), the Rehabilitation Subcode (N.J.A.C. 5:23-6) for work in existing buildings, the Asbestos Hazard Abatement Subcode (N.J.A.C. 5:23-8), NJ Department of Labor asbestos licensing (N.J.A.C. 12:120, with DOH rules at N.J.A.C. 8:60), and the NJ lead-safe certificate law for older rentals. Chapter 01 covers code editions and the grace period for applications filed by February 17, 2027.

NJ rules are stricter than the federal baseline for asbestos in houses and for work that touches a neighbor's property. The Jersey City construction official and subcode officials govern in every case.

2.2 Before demolition starts

Nothing gets pulled until these items are closed out. Most come from preconstruction (Chapter 01), but the field lead confirms each one on site.

  • Construction permit placard posted; HPC Certificate of No Effect or Certificate of Appropriateness on site if the house is in a historic district; required neighbor notices served.
  • Neighbor access agreement signed, or court access granted, for any work that needs the neighbor's side (N.J.A.C. 5:23-2.34).
  • Existing-conditions drawings with bearing walls, joist direction and known prior alterations marked.
  • Hazmat survey results in hand, with an abatement plan for any positives.
  • Engineer engaged and shoring direction in writing for any structural change.
  • Utilities disconnected (Section 2.4); temporary power and lighting installed.
  • Adjoining properties surveyed: dated photos and video of neighbors' party-wall faces and existing cracks, with crack monitors where needed.
  • Salvage list agreed, items tagged, storage arranged.
  • Dumpster and sidewalk occupancy permits from the Jersey City Department of Infrastructure (applied for at least 2 weeks ahead), delivery booked, hauler weight limits known.
  • NJ One Call markout in hand if the demo includes any digging (foundation removal on a teardown, yard work, lateral cut-off).

2.3 Hazardous materials surveys

Any house built before 1978 is presumed to have lead paint until testing says otherwise. Any house built before the 1980s may contain asbestos. Old rowhouses usually have both.

Asbestos

A certified asbestos inspector samples suspect materials and sends them for lab analysis, usually polarized light microscopy (PLM). A material is regulated as asbestos-containing when it contains more than 1 percent asbestos. Typical lab turnaround is 24 to 72 hours; plan one to two weeks from site visit to written report. Federal NESHAP rules exempt most houses of four or fewer units, but New Jersey does not leave houses out: removal from a residence must be done by a contractor licensed by the NJ Department of Labor (N.J.A.C. 12:120, with DOH rules at N.J.A.C. 8:60), and the DCA Asbestos Hazard Abatement Subcode (N.J.A.C. 5:23-8) governs the abatement permit and clearance.

ESC crews don't disturb asbestos. An NJ DOL-licensed abatement contractor removes it, an independent consultant monitors air and clears the area, and the abatement permit and any notice period it carries go on the schedule. Confirm the timing with the abatement contractor and the construction official at permit filing.

Lead paint and the EPA RRP rule

The RRP rule applies to paid work that disturbs painted surfaces in pre-1978 housing and child-occupied facilities. It's triggered by disturbing more than 6 sq ft of painted surface per room inside, more than 20 sq ft outside, or any window replacement. The firm must be EPA-certified (or certified by an authorized state), each job needs a certified renovator assigned, and the owner gets the Renovate Right pamphlet before work starts. Paint counts as lead-based at 1.0 mg/cm² or 0.5 percent by weight.

Testing is by XRF (certified inspector or risk assessor) or EPA-recognized test kits (certified renovator). On a full gut of an 1880s house we usually skip testing and treat all paint as lead. Testing pays off when we're keeping a lot of original trim.

RRP prohibits open-flame burning or torching of painted surfaces, heat guns at or above 1,100°F, and power sanding, grinding or planing without a HEPA shroud and vacuum. Interior containment covers the floor at least 6 ft beyond the work area; exterior ground cover extends at least 10 ft. The job closes with cleaning verification using the EPA verification card, and records are kept 3 years. Worker exposure is a separate OSHA rule (1926.62), which drives air monitoring and respirator use for the crew.

NJ lead-safe certificates and abatement

In New Jersey, rental one- and two-family and multiple dwellings built before 1978 also need a lead-safe certificate under P.L.2021, c.182 (amended by P.L.2024, c.74), inspected at tenant turnover. That matters whenever an ESC client rents a unit, such as a garden apartment in a two-family brownstone: the PM flags it at contract so the owner can schedule the inspection after our work and before the next tenant moves in. RRP covers renovation; lead abatement (work meant to permanently remove lead hazards) needs an NJ-certified abatement firm, which ESC brings in as a sub when the owner orders it.

Mold and other materials

There's no federal mold standard. We follow EPA residential mold guidance and IICRC S520 practice: small areas (under about 10 sq ft, the EPA guidance threshold) our crew cleans with PPE and HEPA; larger growth goes to a remediation contractor, after the moisture source is fixed. Also watch for abandoned fuel-oil tanks (licensed tank contractor), mercury thermostats, PCB ballasts, and refrigerant in old AC units, which a certified tech must recover.

Inspection hold

No general demolition in any area that was abated until the abatement consultant's clearance report is in the job file and the PM has released the area. If the survey missed a material and we uncover it during strip-out (a common case is pipe wrap inside a wall cavity), stop, isolate the area, and call the inspector. Do not bag it ourselves.

2.4 Utility disconnects and temporary services

  1. Gas. PSE&G pulls or locks the meter. Our plumber cuts and caps the house piping at the meter location and caps any line we'll cut through. Abandoned branch lines get traced and capped, never left open.
  2. Electric. On a full gut, PSE&G de-energizes the service and our electrician sets a temporary service or a temporary panel fed from the meter. On selective demo, the electrician identifies and locks out circuits feeding the work area and confirms dead with a tester at each box before we open walls. Lockout tags carry the electrician's name and date.
  3. Water. Keep one supply live at a temporary hose bib with a backflow preventer. We need water for wet-cutting and dust control. Everything else is shut and drained, and the JCMUA meter is protected from freezing in winter.
  4. Sewer. Cap the house drain at the house trap or main cleanout once fixtures come out, to stop sewer gas and rodents. Keep one capped connection we can reuse for a temporary toilet if the plumbing subcode official allows.
  5. Heat and fuel. Boiler drained and isolated; oil tank handled as above.
  6. Telecom and alarms. Owner disconnects accounts; we coil and tag the lines at the entry point. Monitored alarm or fire panels are taken offline with the monitoring company notified so the fire department isn't called by dust.

Inspection hold

The field lead signs the utility disconnect sheet (gas, electric, water, sewer, fuel) before demo crews start. On selective jobs, every wall and ceiling we open must be checked dead by the electrician first. A non-contact tester alone is not enough; verify at the device with a two-pole tester.

2.7 The strip-out sequence

We demolish top-down and outside-in. Work starts on the top floor so debris always moves down and no crew works below another. Each floor is stripped to framing before the crew moves down. Structural removals come last, after everything non-structural is out and the engineer has seen the exposed framing.

  1. Protect and set up. Protect retained finishes, stairs and sash. Set the container or truck schedule, protect the carry route, then temporary lighting and one fire extinguisher per floor near the stair.
  2. Salvage. Remove and tag listed items before general demo enters the room (Section 2.9).
  3. Fixtures and loose items. Doors, hardware, cabinets, appliances, plumbing fixtures, radiators. Cast-iron radiators can weigh several hundred pounds; plan the route and rig them down.
  4. Finishes. Trim, then plaster and lath, ceilings before walls in each room so falling ceiling material doesn't land on a crew working a wall. Plaster goes straight into bags and out by hand the same day.
  5. Flooring. Down to subfloor or joists as drawn; pull nails from retained joists.
  6. Systems. After the electrician and plumber confirm everything is dead and capped, remove old piping, wiring, ductwork and boiler components. Knob-and-tube comes out entirely; none is reused or left in cavities.
  7. Non-bearing partitions. Only after the engineer or architect has confirmed in writing which walls are non-bearing. In rowhouses, stair walls and the walls at parlor pocket doors often carry floors above.
  8. Conditions walk. Once the floor is open, the PM, engineer and architect walk it. We photograph every bay and mark findings on the drawings (Section 2.11).
  9. Structural removals. With shoring installed and approved, remove bearing walls, joists, stairs or masonry as drawn, top floor first.
  10. Clean. HEPA vacuum all surfaces, framing and masonry; then the handoff walk (Section 2.16).

Common failure

Piling wet plaster on a floor. Three-coat plaster on wood lath weighs roughly 8 to 10 lb per square foot of wall. A room's worth on the floor can far exceed the 30 to 40 psf live loads houses are designed for (IRC live-load table), and old joists with notches or rot have less capacity than that. Keep debris moving to the chute every shift and never stage it over a span we've already cut.

2.8 Temporary shoring and needling

Before we cut anything that carries load, shoring takes that load. The engineer of record designs it or approves our layout in writing. OSHA 1926.850(a) requires an engineering survey of the structure by a competent person before demolition starts; on any structural removal, that survey is the engineer's walk plus our shoring plan.

Shoring must carry load through every floor to a sound slab, footing or pad. A shore landing mid-span on the floor below just moves the problem down a level.

Needle beams through masonry

For a new or widened masonry opening, we cut small holes above the future lintel, slide a steel or LVL needle through each, shore both ends, and pack the gap to the brick above with drypack or shims. Only then is the opening cut.

Needling a party wall needs access to the neighbor's side, which means a written access agreement and often their engineer. New Jersey has no party-wall statute that gives us a right to enter; if the neighbor refuses, work that needs their side doesn't proceed without a court order (N.J.A.C. 5:23-2.34). Many party-wall openings aren't permitted at all.

Inspection hold

No bearing element is cut until the PM (or the engineer, when the drawings or the construction official require it) has inspected the installed shoring: posts plumb, plates bearing fully, wedges snug, load path continuous to a slab or footing, needles packed tight. Photograph each shoring line with a tape in the shot and file it before the cut.

Common failure

Jacking shores to "take the load" and lifting the floor above. Over-jacking cracks plaster upstairs, pops tile, and on a party wall can lift the neighbor's joists that share the same pockets. Shores go in snug. Any intentional lift is the engineer's call, done in small increments with a laser or level reference.

2.9 Salvage of historic elements

Brownstones carry a lot of material that can't be bought new: carved or marble mantels, pier mirrors, plaster ceiling medallions, pocket doors with original hardware, interior shutters, newel posts and balusters, stained or etched glass, and wide-board flooring. What we keep is the owner's and architect's decision; how we remove it is ours. In a Jersey City historic district, exterior elements such as the stoop, cornice, windows and doors are typically reviewed by the HPC, so none of them comes off until the CoNE or CoA covering that work is in the job file.

  1. Inventory. Walk the house with the owner and architect. Tag each item with number, room and wall, and photograph it.
  2. Test for lead. Painted salvage is treated as lead-painted unless tested. Wrap it before it leaves the containment.
  3. Remove by hand. Score paint lines before prying and pull nails through the back with nippers. Marble mantels come apart as shelf and pilasters, two people per piece, supported as each comes free.
  4. Protect. Wrap in moving blankets or foam, label the wrap with the tag number, and stand flat panels vertically. Keep hardware in labeled bags with its door.
  5. Store. Off site or in a secured, dry, heated room on site that's out of the demolition path.
  6. Document for re-use. Measure and photograph plaster cornices and medallions that can't be removed intact so a plaster shop can run new profiles or cast replicas (Chapter 09).

ESC standard

Salvage comes out before general demo enters the room, not during. Plaster cornices and medallions are usually not salvageable once the ceiling comes down; we take a profile and a sample piece instead. Salvage that returns to the job is reinstalled by finish carpenters (Chapter 11), and the tag log goes with it.

2.10 Dust control and containment

In rowhouses, open joist pockets, old pipe chases and shared flues connect straight into the neighbor's rooms, and plaster dust finds every one.

The tools are containment, negative air (HEPA fans exhausting the dirty zone outdoors) and HEPA cleaning (filters rated 99.97 percent at 0.3 microns; a shop vac with a fine-dust bag isn't HEPA).

  1. Seal the building first. Close and tape HVAC registers and returns, shut down forced-air systems, seal fireplace openings and any party-wall holes (old pipe chases, open joist pockets, mail slots between cellars) with poly and tape or with foam board and sealant.
  2. Build the containment. 6-mil poly on spring-loaded pole systems, taped to floor, walls and ceiling. Airlock with two zipper doors (or overlapping flaps) where crews enter. Floor protection outside the zone: rosin paper or a breathable protection board, taped at seams, with a sticky mat at the airlock.
  3. Set negative air. Size the HEPA air unit for at least 4 air changes per hour in the zone. Example: a 20 ft by 25 ft room with a 10 ft ceiling is 5,000 cu ft; 4 ACH is 20,000 cu ft per hour, or about 335 CFM. Duct the exhaust outdoors through a sealed panel in a window, away from neighbors' windows and air intakes.
  4. Verify pressure. Target -0.02 in. w.c. (about -5 Pa) in the zone relative to the clean side, read on a manometer at the start of each shift. The poly should bow gently inward.
  5. Work wet, clean daily. Mist plaster before it comes down, never dry sweep, HEPA vacuum each day, and wipe bags before they leave the zone.
  6. Tear down last. After final HEPA cleaning and wipe-down, mist and fold the poly inward, bag it, and do a final HEPA pass on the area where it stood.

ESC standard

Poly containment with HEPA negative air for any selective demo in an occupied house or next to rooms we're keeping. On full guts, we seal party-wall openings and flues, run HEPA air scrubbers on each floor during plaster removal, and rely on source control and daily HEPA cleaning, because full negative pressure is hard to hold in an open shell.

2.11 Discovery protocol for hidden conditions

Every old house has surprises. The protocol makes the crew stop the same way every time and gets the right people looking quickly, with documentation for the owner conversation.

What we look for

  • Rotted joist ends at masonry, common under old leaders and at rear walls. Probe with an awl; more than about 1/4 in. of soft wood is a flag. Fixes are designed by the engineer (Chapter 04).
  • Earlier sistering that's short, unbolted or doesn't reach bearing; cut and notched joists from old plumbing runs. Map every one.
  • Undersized or missing headers at openings cut in past renovations. Shore at once if the wall is bearing.
  • Knob-and-tube. Live until the electrician proves it dead; old K&T is sometimes spliced into newer circuits.
  • Fire damage and masonry defects: charred framing, cracked lintels, bulging walls, loose brick at pockets. Masonry repair is Chapter 05; underpinning is Chapter 03.

ESC standard

Any field lead or worker can stop work on a discovered condition. The PM hears about it the same day, with photos in the job file. Structural or life-safety findings get temporary shoring or isolation right away, before anyone discusses cost. Nothing gets covered or removed until the PM releases it in writing.

Inspection hold

Post-strip conditions walk: once the shell is open, the PM, engineer and architect walk every floor. Structural design isn't final, and Chapter 03 and 04 work doesn't start, until findings are logged, answered or listed as open RFIs, and any building subcode inspection the construction official calls for has passed.

2.12 Debris handling, containers and recycling

Rowhouse debris is heavy and dense. Plaster, brick and old mortar fill a container's weight limit long before its volume, so container choice is about weight as much as size. For planning, a full gut of a plaster-interior rowhouse generates on the order of 1 cubic yard of loose debris per 25 to 40 sq ft of floor area, more if we're removing masonry or a cellar slab. Adjust the forecast after the first container comes back weighed.

Use 10- or 15-yd boxes for plaster, brick and concrete, and 20- or 30-yd boxes for lighter framing, flooring and cabinets. Check each hauler's weight limit and overweight rate before booking. In Jersey City, a container on the street needs a dumpster permit, and anything on the sidewalk needs a sidewalk occupancy permit, both from the Department of Infrastructure, Division of Engineering, Traffic and Transportation. Apply at least 2 weeks before delivery. Expect reflective markings and plywood under the container to protect the pavement.

Painted and lead-contaminated debris from RRP work is bagged and can usually go with general construction waste for residential jobs, but confirm local rules. Asbestos waste leaves only with the abatement contractor under its own manifests.

2.14 Common failures and how to avoid them

Common failure

Removing a "partition" that was bearing. Avoid it by getting written confirmation for every wall, checking joist direction from below and above, and assuming stair walls and walls in line with the floor above are bearing until proven otherwise.

Common failure

Dust in the neighbor's house. It usually travels through a shared flue, an open joist pocket or a cellar opening. Seal those before the first ceiling comes down and check the neighbor side if they'll let us.

Common failure

Cutting into the party wall past our half. In rowhouses, the neighbor's joists often pocket into the same wall from the other side, sometimes only 4 in. from ours. No coring, chasing or pocket enlargement in a party wall without engineer direction and a check of what's on the other side.

Common failure

Leaving the building open to weather. When roof, skylight or rear-wall work opens the shell, cover it at the end of every day with secured tarps or temporary framing and membrane. Wet plaster debris and soaked joists set back the schedule by weeks.

2.15 QC checklist

  • Construction permit, HPC approval where applicable, Department of Infrastructure permits, neighbor notices and access agreements, adjoining-property survey, hazmat survey, NJ DOL-licensed abatement contractor's clearance and RRP certifications on file.
  • Utility disconnect sheet signed.
  • Salvage tagged, logged and stored before general demo.
  • Containment built per plan; manometer readings logged each shift; flues, registers and party-wall openings sealed.
  • Debris removed daily; no stockpiles on floors.
  • Non-bearing walls confirmed in writing; shoring inspected, tagged and photographed before any bearing cut.
  • Discovered conditions logged and reported the same day.
  • Shell weather-tight at the end of each day.
  • Final HEPA clean done; RRP cleaning verification passed where applicable.

2.16 Handoff to foundations and framing

When demolition is done, the next trades (Chapter 03 for underpinning, footings and slabs; Chapter 04 for framing) should walk into a building that's open, clean, safe and fully understood. Specifically:

  • Stripped to the scope on the drawings: framing, masonry and subfloor exposed where called for, with no loose plaster, lath, nails or debris left in joist bays or on wall plates.
  • HEPA cleaned, with floors swept and vacuumed so layout lines can be snapped.
  • Temporary shoring in place, tagged and photographed, with a note on each line stating what it supports and who released it. Shoring stays until the permanent member is installed and the engineer releases it.
  • Utilities capped and labeled at the point of entry, temporary power with GFCI protection on each floor, and one live water source.
  • Weather protection secure over any roof, skylight or wall opening.
  • As-found documentation complete: photos of every bay, a joist condition map, and all findings logged with the engineer's responses or open RFIs listed.
