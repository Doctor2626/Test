Division 22 · Plumbing

Plumbing

How ESC plans, roughs, tests and trims plumbing in new houses and gutted masonry rowhouses: water service, drain-waste-vent, supply piping, water heating, showers and gas, with the different ways to do each and when we choose them. Lead carpenters, licensed plumbing subs, PMs and owners reviewing a plumbing layout all use this chapter.

6.1 Where plumbing sits in the job

Plumbing happens in three passes, each with its own inspection.

  1. Underground (under-slab). Building drain, sewer connection, water service and drains below a slab. On a new house this follows the foundation (Chapter 03) and precedes the slab pour. In a rowhouse it follows demolition of the cellar slab (Chapter 02).
  2. Rough-in (top-out). Stacks, branch drains, roof vents, supply piping, valve bodies, shower drains, gas piping and stub-outs. This follows framing (Chapter 04) and leads HVAC (Chapter 07) and electrical (Chapter 08), because drains have fixed slopes and can't bend around ducts.
  3. Trim (finish). Fixtures, trim kits, stops and water heater startup, after tile (Chapter 10), cabinets and counters (Chapter 11) and paint (Chapter 09).

In New Jersey the plumbing subcode is the National Standard Plumbing Code 2024 (NJ edition), adopted under the Uniform Construction Code. The IRC plumbing chapters don't apply to NJ work, so this chapter cites no IRC P-sections. Where a number below is marked "typical; confirm against the NSPC (NJ edition)", it's a common trade value and the plumber checks it against the NSPC before layout. Where the NSPC is known to differ from other model codes, we state the rule in words and point to the NSPC table. Gas piping follows the IFGC 2024 and the IRC fuel gas chapter as adopted in NJ. Framing rules for holes and notches still come from the IRC 2024 (NJ edition) building chapters.

A complete application submitted with all prior approvals by February 17, 2027 may still be reviewed under the 2021 NSPC and 2021 I-codes. Confirm with the PM which edition each job's permit follows.

New Jersey

All plumbing and gas piping is done by an NJ licensed master plumber (N.J.S.A. 45:14C), even where an owner exemption might apply. The permit and the underground, rough and final inspections go through the plumbing subcode of the Jersey City Division of Construction Code. JCMUA is the water and sewer utility (service tap, meter, backflow, lateral), and PSE&G supplies gas. Jersey City lists a JCMUA release as a prior approval before a permit issues for footing and foundation work, so start that early on any job that touches the foundation.

6.2 Before plumbing starts

  • Fixture schedule final, with spec sheets for every toilet, valve, drain, tub and appliance. Rough-in dimensions come from these sheets.
  • Tile layouts for every shower and wet floor (Chapter 10): drain type and location, slope direction, niches and tile thickness.
  • Cabinet shop drawings (Chapter 11) for sink centerlines and drawer stacks under sinks.
  • Framing complete in plumbing areas (Chapter 04): curbless showers recessed, toilet bays headed off, wet walls built 2x6 where the stack or a carrier needs it.
  • Roof deck on and the roofer booked for vent flashings (Chapter 05).
  • Sewer lateral camera-scoped, existing water service size and material known, JCMUA (water and sewer) and PSE&G (gas) applications submitted, and the JCMUA release in hand if the job includes footing or foundation work.
  • Ducts and stacks drawn on one coordinated plan with HVAC (Chapter 07) so nobody claims the same joist bay.
  • Old gas and water lines capped, tagged or removed by demolition (Chapter 02).

6.3 Water service, meter and backflow

The service runs from the utility tap or curb stop to the main shutoff inside the house, usually through the front foundation wall in a rowhouse. Old rowhouse services are often lead or galvanized and undersized. On a gut renovation, plan to replace it and coordinate the street side with JCMUA, which may own part of the line.

Inside, in order: wall sleeve, main shutoff, meter (set by JCMUA), house-side valve, backflow device if JCMUA requires one, pressure-reducing valve (PRV) if needed, then a thermal expansion tank wherever a check, backflow device or PRV closes the system.

6.4 Underground and under-slab work

A wrong drain location under concrete costs a saw-cut and a patch, so this is the least forgiving pass. In a rowhouse cellar the building drain usually runs from the stack to the front wall, where the lateral heads to the street. Many older houses have a house trap and fresh-air inlet at that wall. Ask the plumbing subcode official whether to keep or remove it before demolition, and confirm against the NSPC (NJ edition).

  1. Confirm inverts. Shoot the lateral invert at the front wall and the lowest fixture drain. Fixtures below the lateral need an ejector. Fixtures that could flood from a sewer backup need a backwater valve; the NSPC (NJ edition) sets the trigger, so check it there and ask JCMUA about known surcharge on the street. Jersey City's combined sewers make this a common item.
  2. Lay out from the plan. Snap stack, drain and cleanout locations on the subgrade, tied to the foundation walls.
  3. Trench and bed. Remove rock and debris and bed pipe on 3 to 4 in of compacted sand or fine gravel so it bears along its full length. Never support pipe on blocks.
  4. Set pipe to slope. 1/4 in per ft wherever depth allows. A flatter 1/8 in per ft on 3 in and larger is the usual allowance (typical; confirm against the NSPC (NJ edition)); we use it only when depth forces it and note it on the as-built.
  5. Bring up risers. Stub stacks, drains and cleanouts above the future slab with test caps, and sleeve or wrap pipe where it passes through the slab.
  6. Test and inspect. Water test (6.12), inspect, then photograph every run with a tape in the frame.
  7. Backfill. Bedding material over the pipe first, then fill in lifts. Hand off to concrete (Chapter 03).

ESC standard

We camera-scope every rowhouse lateral before final design. Clay with roots, offset joints or bellies, or heavily scaled cast iron gets priced for replacement or lining to the property line in the base scope.

6.5 Drain, waste and vent (DWV)

Three rules drive every DWV layout: drains slope continuously toward the sewer, every trap is vented within its allowed distance, and the system can be cleaned out.

ESC default: No-hub cast iron for every soil stack and for horizontal drains over finished living space in rowhouses and multifamily work. PVC Schedule 40 for vents, short branches inside bathroom floors and underground where allowed. We go all cast iron where the NSPC (NJ edition) or the plumbing subcode official limits plastic, and wrap any PVC drain over a bedroom in acoustic insulation.

Slope, sizing and framing

Horizontal drains smaller than 3 in need 1/4 in per ft; 3 in and larger may usually run at 1/8 (typical; confirm against the NSPC (NJ edition)). We design to 1/4 throughout. Don't over-slope trap arms: the vent connection has to stay above the trap weir, and a common limit is total arm fall of no more than one pipe diameter (typical; confirm against the NSPC (NJ edition)).

Typical sizes: toilet 3 in, shower 2 in, tub and kitchen sink 1-1/2 in, lav 1-1/4 in trap on a 1-1/2 in drain, washer standpipe 2 in. Rowhouse stacks serving three stacked full baths are usually 4 in.

Rowhouse joists usually span party wall to party wall, so a drain running front to back crosses them. Holes and notches follow IRC R502.8 (Chapter 04): holes no more than 1/3 of joist depth and at least 2 in from either edge; notches no more than 1/6 of depth and never in the middle third of the span. A 3 in drain can't be bored through a 2x8. Route it in the parallel joist bay, a soffit or a dropped ceiling.

Venting methods

Each trap needs a vent within its maximum trap arm length, which depends on trap size. Take those distances from the NSPC (NJ edition) trap-to-vent table, not from IRC tables or memory: the NSPC values differ from the IRC's, and a layout copied from an out-of-state job can fail inspection. Except at toilets, the vent connection can't sit below the trap weir. Vents rise vertically above the flood rim before turning horizontal, so a backed-up drain can't fill them (6 in is typical; confirm against the NSPC (NJ edition)).

Cleanouts

Cleanouts go at the base of each stack, near the building drain to sewer junction, at changes of direction the code requires and at intervals along long horizontal runs. The NSPC (NJ edition) sets the maximum spacing by pipe size and it can be tighter than other model codes, so take it from the NSPC rather than assuming 100 ft. Keep 18 in clear in front of cleanouts 3 in and larger and 12 in for smaller (typical; confirm against the NSPC (NJ edition)). The rowhouse front-wall cleanout is the one the drain cleaner will use, so keep it out of finished closets and never behind a cabinet. Concealed cleanouts get access panels.

Installation rules

  • Steel shield plates at least 0.062 in thick wherever pipe is within 1-1/4 in of a framing face (typical; confirm against the NSPC (NJ edition)).
  • Support spacing per the NSPC (NJ edition) hanger table (typical values in 6.12).
  • No supply piping in exterior masonry walls or uninsulated front and rear bays. Stay on the warm side of the insulation.
  • Don't chase pipe into party walls; build a furred wet wall (Chapter 04). The party wall is shared structure and fire separation.
  • Sleeve through masonry and concrete; firestop rated penetrations with a listed system for that pipe.
  • Drop-ear elbows screwed to blocking at every stub, so stubs can't push back into the wall.

  • Shower and tub valves limit outlet temperature to 120 F (typical; confirm against the NSPC (NJ edition)). Where the tank stores at 140 F, add an ASSE 1017 thermostatic mixing valve at the heater.
  • T&P discharge runs full size, by gravity, unthreaded at the end, to within 6 in of the floor or an approved indirect waste (typical; confirm against the NSPC (NJ edition)).
  • Drain pan under any tank where a leak would damage finished space, which in a rowhouse means every floor above the cellar.
  • Recirculation: a dedicated return with a pump at the heater, or a crossover valve with an under-sink pump at the far fixture where there's no return. Control it by timer, demand button or temperature, and insulate hot and return piping as the energy code requires.

6.8 Rough-in (top-out)

  1. Layout walk. PM, lead plumber and lead carpenter walk each room with spec sheets, tile layouts and cabinet drawings, and paint fixture centerlines on subfloor and studs.
  2. Confirm finished wall thickness. Add up backer, membrane, thinset and tile or plaster, and write it on the stud. A flange set 12 in from studs ends up about 11 in from tile.
  3. Frame for plumbing. Head off joists under toilet bends and shower drains, add blocking for valves, stubs and grab bars, and clear every cut with the lead carpenter (Chapter 04).
  4. Set stacks and DWV. Stack from the cellar up with riser clamps bearing at each floor, then branches, flanges at finished floor height, tub and shower drains, and vents.
  5. Roof penetration. Stack vent through the roof, flashed with the roofer (Chapter 05), clear of operable windows, doors and air intakes, including the neighbor's, by the distance or height the NSPC (NJ edition) requires. On a rowhouse roof the neighbor's skylights and roof hatches count.
  6. Supply. Headers or manifolds, lines and stubs at the 6.9 dimensions. Valve bodies plumb, with plaster guards, at the depth the wall build-up needs.
  7. Gas. Pipe appliance locations per 6.11 and cap every outlet.
  8. Protect and label. Shield plates, capped and tagged stubs, hot and cold marked, manifold ports labeled.
  9. Test, photograph, inspect. Run the 6.12 tests and photograph every wall and floor with a tape in the frame before close-in.

A three-bath rowhouse with kitchen and laundry typically takes a two-person crew two to three weeks to rough, plus a day for testing. Order at contract: thermostatic valves, linear drains, wall-hung carriers and specialty tubs often take 2 to 6 weeks, and a JCMUA meter set or new tap can take 4 to 12.

Curbless showers and drain planning

The finished shower floor slopes 1/4 in to 1/2 in per ft to the drain (typical; confirm against the NSPC (NJ edition)) while its high edge meets the bath floor flush, so the shower subfloor must drop. On new houses we specify dropped or deeper joists in the shower bay. In rowhouses we sister lowered joists or set the subfloor on ledgers between joists, with the engineer's approval where joists are cut (Chapter 04).

The plumber sets the drain from the tile layout, not the framing. The tile setter (Chapter 10) owns slope, membrane and flood test; the plumber owns drain body height, trap and connection. Pick the drain and membrane system together before framing, because each drain fits specific membranes.

6.11 Gas piping

Gas piping follows the IFGC 2024 and the IRC fuel gas chapter as adopted in NJ, is installed by the licensed master plumber and is inspected by the plumbing subcode. Gas runs from the PSE&G meter to each appliance, with a shutoff in the same room and within 6 ft of each appliance, a sediment trap where the appliance needs one, and an accessible connector. The fitter sizes pipe from connected load and longest run using the fuel gas code tables for PSE&G's delivery pressure at the meter. Many of our houses are now all-electric; settle this in preconstruction (Chapter 01) so the PSE&G order is right, including any meter relocation or removal.

6.13 Trim and finish

  1. Flush. Open every stub into a bucket before cartridges and aerators go in. Debris in thermostatic cartridges is our most common trim callback.
  2. Start the water heater. Fill, purge, set the mixing valve, and check T&P discharge, pan drain, condensate drain and expansion tank charge.
  3. Stops, toilets and trim. Quarter-turn stops and tight escutcheons; toilets set after tile and paint, caulked front and sides with the back left open so a leak shows; valve trims with handle limit stops set to 120 F.
  4. Appliances. Dishwasher, refrigerator, washer and gas connections, leak checked.
  5. Recirc. Set controls and time hot water arrival at the farthest fixture.
  6. Test and document. Run every fixture, check below each floor, record static pressure and hot water temperature, tag every shutoff, and put the as-built riser and valve map in the O&M binder.
  7. Finals. Plumbing and gas finals with the plumbing subcode, then PSE&G gas release. JCMUA closes out any meter or backflow paperwork.

6.14 Inspection holds

Inspection hold

Underground. No backfill or slab pour until underground DWV and water service are tested, inspected and photographed, including any house trap, backwater valve or ejector pit. The plumbing subcode inspector signs off.

Inspection hold

Rough plumbing and gas. No insulation, drywall, backer board or shower pan until DWV, water and gas tests pass and the inspector signs off. Gauges stay on and readable.

Inspection hold

Shower pan. No floor tile until the 24-hour flood test passes and, where the plumbing subcode inspects pans, the inspector has seen it full (Chapter 10).

Inspection hold

Final. No occupancy until plumbing and gas finals pass and PSE&G releases gas service.

6.15 Common failures and how to avoid them

Common failure

Dimensions taken from studs. Tile pushes the wall out and the toilet tank hits it, or a valve trim won't reach after the tile changes from porcelain to thick stone. Measure from the finished face, lock tile thickness before valves go in, and keep plaster guards on until tile is done.

Common failure

Over-cut joists. A rowhouse joist notched for a toilet bend sags. Every cut beyond R502.8 goes through the lead carpenter, and the engineer if needed, and gets headed or reinforced.

Common failure

Curbless drain at the wrong height. Water ponds at the threshold or won't clear. Set the drain from the tile layout and a calculated bed thickness, with the tile setter present.

Common failure

Siphoned traps. A fixture moves after rough-in and its arm is now too long, or the vent lands below the weir. Any late fixture move triggers a vent check.

Common failure

Frozen pipes and dripping relief valves. Supply lines in a rowhouse's front or rear masonry wall freeze; a new PRV closes the system and the T&P weeps because no expansion tank was charged. Keep supply out of exterior walls and check tank charge at startup.

Common failure

Screws through lines and unbonded CSST. Missing shield plates and CSST the electrician never heard about. Walk every wall before close-in and put CSST bonding on the electrical rough checklist.

6.16 QC checklist

  • Installed valves, drains and carriers match the spec sheets on site.
  • Toilet flanges within 1/4 in of specified distance from finished wall, at finished floor height.
  • Valves plumb, square, at height and within plaster guard depth.
  • Shower drains on the tile layout and at agreed bed height.
  • Horizontal drains in continuous slope, no bellies or back-pitch.
  • Every trap vented within its NSPC (NJ edition) arm limit; no vent below a weir.
  • Cleanouts at stack bases, the front wall and NSPC spacing on long runs, with clearance and access.
  • Riser clamps at each floor on cast iron stacks.
  • Shield plates wherever pipe is within 1-1/4 in of a framing face.
  • No supply in exterior masonry walls; nothing chased into party walls.
  • Rated penetrations firestopped; roof vents flashed and clear of neighbors' windows.
  • All tests passed with gauges photographed; CSST bonded; stubs and ports labeled.

6.17 Handoff

After rough-in, plumbing hands off to insulation and drywall (Chapter 09) and tile (Chapter 10). The space should look like this:

  • Rough inspections passed and posted; system holding pressure or drained and capped as the PM directs.
  • Stubs capped, protected and projecting correctly past the finished wall plane.
  • Plaster guards on; tub and shower drains plugged and covered.
  • Curbless recesses clean and flat with drains at height, ready for slope and membrane.
  • Blocking for valves, grab bars and accessories in place and photographed.
  • Nothing proud of the framing plane where board or plaster goes; floors broom clean.
  • As-built markups of stacks, cleanouts and shutoffs given to the PM.

At final, the owner gets a labeled, working system, the riser and valve map, recorded pressure and temperature, heater and recirc settings, and warranties in the O&M binder. HVAC (Chapter 07) and electrical (Chapter 08) finals often depend on plumbing for condensate drains, HPWH circuits and boiler fills, so schedule plumbing final first.
