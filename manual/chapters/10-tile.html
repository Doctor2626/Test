Tile & Stone

How ESC preps substrates, waterproofs showers, sets, grouts and protects ceramic, porcelain and natural stone in new houses and gutted masonry rowhouses, with the different systems for each step and when we pick them. Lead carpenters, tile subs, PMs and owners choosing between shower systems all use this chapter.

10.1 Where tile sits in the job

Tile depends on everything under it. A tile floor is only as good as the joists and subfloor it sits on, and a shower is only as dry as the waterproofing behind it. Most tile failures we're called to fix are substrate or waterproofing failures, not bad setting.

Tile work happens in three passes:

  1. Substrate and waterproofing. Flattening, backer, membranes, shower pans, curbs, niches, benches and the flood test. Follows plumbing rough (Chapter 06) and drywall hang in adjacent rooms (Chapter 09).
  2. Setting and grouting. Walls, then floors, in most rooms.
  3. Sealant and protection. Movement joints, stone sealer and floor protection, ideally after paint (Chapter 09) and before cabinets and trim (Chapter 11).

In a rowhouse gut, tile lands in the last third of the schedule, often on two or three stacked baths at once; keep one tile crew on all of them so details match. New construction follows the same sequence with flatter substrates, so prep goes faster.

Codes and standards cited here are the IRC 2024 (NJ edition), the National Standard Plumbing Code 2024 (NSPC, NJ edition) for anything plumbing, NEC 2023 with NJ amendments, the TCNA Handbook and ANSI A108/A118/A137. In New Jersey the codes are the NJ Uniform Construction Code (N.J.A.C. 5:23), and the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) on our Jersey City jobs is the Division of Construction Code. Plumbing rules, including shower pans, come from the plumbing subcode, not the IRC plumbing chapters, which NJ doesn't use. A complete permit application filed by February 17, 2027 may still be reviewed under the prior editions. The AHJ governs; where a manufacturer's written instructions are stricter, follow them, because the warranty depends on it.

10.2 Before tile starts

The tile foreman walks every tiled room with the PM before mobilizing. Anything missing here is a reason to delay.

  • Floor framing meets the deflection target for the tile chosen (Table 10.1); sistering and leveling of old joists signed off (Chapter 04). Subfloor glued and screwed, no soft spots.
  • Drain bodies of the right type (clamping ring for a liner, bonding flange for a sheet membrane) set at the right height. Valve bodies set to finished wall depth, with heads and sprays located from the tile layout. Rough inspection passed (Chapter 06).
  • Blocking for grab bars, glass hardware, niches, benches and wall-hung fixtures.
  • Electrical rough done for heated floors and thermostats (Chapter 08).
  • Envelope closed, building at 50°F or more, party walls dry (Chapter 05).
  • Tile, setting materials and trims on site, checked for lot, shade and quantity. Approved layout drawing for every tiled room.

ESC standard

Order 10% overage for straight lay, 15% for diagonal, herringbone or large-format, 20% for heavily veined stone. Special-order tile and stone run 4 to 12 weeks, so selections lock before framing. Set from three cartons at a time to blend shade.

10.3 Substrates: stiffness and flatness

Deflection

Tile and grout don't bend; a floor that deflects too much cracks grout first, then tile. The TCNA Handbook uses a live-load deflection limit of L/360 for ceramic and porcelain and L/720 for natural stone. L/720 often means deeper joists, closer spacing or a mid-span beam, so check it before stone is promised to the owner.

The subfloor between joists also has to be stiff. Over wood joists at 16 in on center, ceramic typically goes on a single layer of 23/32 in tongue-and-groove plywood (some uncoupling membranes list thinner panels in their tables). Stone needs two layers, with the second layer's joints offset from the first and screwed to it, not to the joists.

Rowhouse floors are the hard case: rough-sawn 3x8 or 3x10 joists pocketed into the party walls, often with 1 to 2 in of sag. Tile can't fix that; Chapter 04 does. A mortar bed or stone floor adds roughly 15 to 20 psf of dead load, so tell the engineer early.

Flatness

Flatness is measured from the required plane, not from level. Large-format tile (any edge 15 in or longer) needs a flatter substrate because it can't follow dips.

10.4 Shower waterproofing

There are two ways to keep a shower dry. A traditional mortar bed lets water pass through the tile and grout, collect on a liner buried under the mortar, and leave through weep holes in a clamping-ring drain. A bonded membrane stops water right under the thinset, so the mortar and backer behind stay dry. Both work when built right. The bonded approach is thinner, lighter and dries faster after each use, which is why most of our showers use it.

ESC default: bonded sheet membrane on a sloped mortar bed or foam tray for floors, with cement board and liquid membrane on the walls where cost matters and a foam board system on party or exterior walls. We build a traditional mortar bed only when it is specified or when matching existing work, and then we follow the plumbing subcode (NSPC, NJ edition) to the letter.

Traditional mortar bed, done right

  1. Pre-slope. Float a sand-cement pre-slope at 1/4 in per foot minimum to the drain flange, about 1/2 in thick at the drain. Let it set overnight.
  2. Liner. Lay a 40 mil (0.040 in) PVC or CPE liner over the pre-slope, fold the corners (never cut them), and turn it up at least 2 in above the finished threshold. That's the long-standing model figure; confirm the plumbing subcode (NSPC, NJ edition) requirement with the plumbing inspector. Fasten only at the top edge. Set it in sealant on the drain flange and clamp the ring.
  3. Curb. Wrap the liner over the curb framing, solvent-weld a preformed dam corner at the ends, and keep fasteners off the top and inside face.
  4. Flood test. Plug the drain and fill to at least 2 in deep at the threshold. Mark the level and hold it for 24 hours. The 24-hour test is ESC practice; the plumbing subcode (NSPC, NJ edition) sets what the inspector requires.
  5. Protect the weeps. Pea gravel or a weep guard keeps mortar out of the weep holes.
  6. Bed. Pack a 4:1 to 5:1 sand-cement dry-pack bed with galvanized wire reinforcing at mid-depth, 1-1/4 in minimum at the drain, sloped 1/4 in per foot. Screed to the strainer height minus the tile and thinset.

Bonded membranes, done right

Sheet membrane: bond it with the mortar and notch the maker lists, embed it with a flat trowel so no air is trapped, lap seams 2 in, use preformed corners and pipe seals, and lap onto the drain's bonding flange.

Liquid membrane: bed the maker's fabric in the first coat at seams and corners, then apply two coats in opposite directions to the maker's thickness (most land near 20 mils dry). Check with a wet-film gauge and log the reading.

Foam board system: use the system's washers, spacing and seam sealant. Never mix brands at seams or drains.

Inspection hold

Every shower pan gets a flood test before tile, whatever the system. Plug the drain, fill to at least 2 in deep at the threshold without overtopping the curb, mark the level, and leave it 24 hours. Photograph the start and end marks, check the ceiling below, and call the plumbing subcode inspector while water is standing where they inspect pans. No tile on a pan that hasn't passed.

10.5 Curbless showers, linear drains, niches and benches

A curbless shower has no step at the entry, so the shower floor has to start below the bathroom subfloor to get its slope. That recess is framing work decided at design time. A linear drain helps: the floor slopes one direction as a single plane, so large-format tile works without the four-way cuts a center drain needs.

Ways to get the recess, in order of preference:

  • Dropped subfloor between joists on sistered cleats, so joists stay whole.
  • Lowered joists framed with hangers, in new construction.
  • Build the rest of the room up when the floor below can't be touched, at the cost of a threshold at the door.
  • Ripping joist tops only with an engineer's detail. Pocketed rowhouse joists are hard to sister to full bearing, so this is last.

On curbless showers, waterproof the whole bathroom floor or at least 3 to 4 ft past the entry, and give the glass a sweep or drip stop.

Niches go on interior walls, never exterior or masonry party walls. Size them to whole tiles, slope the sill into the shower about 1/4 in per foot, and waterproof them continuous with the wall. Benches need solid framing or a foam bench, a sloped seat and waterproofing over top and front; a floating bench needs blocking designed for a person sitting on its front edge.

Common failure

Flat niche sills and bench seats. Water sits, soaks the grout, and finds any pinhole in the membrane. Check every sill and seat with a level before tile. If the bubble is centered, it's wrong.

10.7 Layout

Layout is decided on paper and snapped before mortar is mixed: balanced cuts, no slivers, and full tiles where the eye lands first.

  1. Find the center lines. Snap them both ways. Rowhouse rooms are rarely square, so check diagonals and hide the error away from the door.
  2. Dry-lay or calculate. If an end cut is under half a tile, shift half a tile so a tile, not a joint, is centered on the line.
  3. Check focal points. Door threshold, shower entry, vanity front, niches and valves get full tiles or balanced cuts.
  4. Set control lines. Snap working lines every 3 to 4 ft. On walls, set a level ledger for the first full course; the bottom cut goes in last.

Lippage and offset

Lippage is the height difference between adjacent tile edges. ANSI A108.02 allows 1/32 in for joints under 1/4 in wide and 1/16 in for joints 1/4 in and wider, plus the tile's own allowable warpage. Long tile is slightly crowned, so a half-offset puts the high center of one tile next to the low end of the next.

That's why ANSI A108.02 limits the offset to no more than 1/3 of the tile length for running bond when the tile has any side longer than 18 in, unless the tile maker approves otherwise. Leveling clips help with large-format tile but don't fix a substrate that's out of flat.

ESC standard

Any running bond with tile longer than 18 in goes in at 1/3 offset or less, even if the owner's inspiration photo shows half-offset. Grout joints are 1/16 in minimum per ANSI A108.02; we run 1/8 in on rectified porcelain and 3/16 in on calibrated (non-rectified) tile.

10.8 Setting sequence

  1. Prep. Vacuum, prime where required, and confirm 50°F or warmer for 72 hours.
  2. Mix. Measured water, low-speed paddle, slake 5 to 10 minutes, remix. Never re-temper mortar that has started to set.
  3. Walls first. Ledger up, bottom course last, joint at floor or tub left open for sealant.
  4. Floors far wall to door. Trowel only what you can set while the mortar still transfers to a fingertip.
  5. Clean joints. Rake squeeze-out to two-thirds of tile depth before it hardens.
  6. Cure. Usually 24 hours before grout; longer over impervious membranes or in cold rooms.
  7. Grout and seal. Grout (10.9), leaving movement joints open; sealant after grout cures; stone and grout sealer after 48 to 72 hours.

Movement joints

Tile, mortar and framing move at different rates, and a movement joint gives that movement a planned line instead of a crack. TCNA method EJ171 calls for joints at the perimeter of every tiled surface, at every change of plane (inside corners, floor-to-wall, tub-to-wall), where tile meets a different material, over structural joints in the substrate, and in the field of large areas.

Movement joints are filled with sealant, not grout. Use an ASTM C920 silicone or urethane sealant color-matched to the grout, over backer rod or bond-breaker tape so the sealant sticks to two sides only.

10.10 Heated floors

Electric mats or cable warm bathroom floors for comfort, not primary heat, unless the HVAC design (Chapter 07) counts on them.

  1. Plan. Heat open floor only, never under toilets, vanities or tubs. Size the mat to the net area; cable can't be cut.
  2. Test. Record resistance and insulation resistance on the warranty card before install, after fastening, after embedding and after tile.
  3. Install. Fasten mat or cable to the subfloor or into a compatible uncoupling membrane. Never cross cable over itself.
  4. Sensor. Set the floor sensor between two runs in a conduit so it can be replaced, with a spare beside it.
  5. Embed. Cover with SLC or thinset using a plastic trowel.
  6. Power. The electrician connects a ground-fault-protected circuit per NEC Article 424 (Chapter 08). Don't use the heat to speed cure; wait the mortar maker's time, often 7 to 28 days.

Common failure

A cable nicked during setting and found months later. A continuity alarm clipped to the leads while setting beeps the moment a trowel cuts the wire, while it can still be repaired.

10.11 Natural stone

Stone is softer and more porous than porcelain, and many stones react to acids and to water in the mortar.

  • Stiffness first. L/720 and two layers of plywood (Table 10.1).
  • White mortar for light stone. Gray thinset can shadow through white marble and limestone. Use white thinset and grout that won't stain.
  • Moisture-sensitive stone. Green marble (serpentine) and some resin-backed tiles curl when set in water-based mortar. Use epoxy or a rapid-set mortar the stone supplier approves.
  • Seal before grout. Apply a grout release or the first coat of penetrating sealer before grouting porous stone so grout pigment doesn't lodge in the surface. Seal again after grout cures, and reseal every 1 to 3 years.
  • Cleaning. pH-neutral only on marble, limestone and travertine; note it in the closeout binder.
  • Blend. Dry-lay stone for the owner or architect to approve vein direction before setting.

ESC standard

Every natural stone selection gets a sealed sample board, kept on site, tested with water, oil and lemon juice for 10 minutes each so the owner sees how it stains and etches before it's installed. We note the result and the sealer used in the job file.

10.12 Rowhouse specifics

  • Out-of-level floors. Decide early whether to level the framing (Chapter 04), build up with SLC or mortar, or let tile follow the house. Tile can be flat without being level.
  • Party walls. Never tile on old brick or plaster on brick. Fur out with a gap and use foam board or backer with membrane; brick wicks moisture for years.
  • Stacked baths. A top-floor leak ruins two ceilings, so every pan is flood tested and photographed.
  • Thresholds. Plan saddles or transitions to old wood floors at design time (Chapter 11).

10.15 Common failures and how to avoid them

Common failure

Cracked tile on a bouncy floor. The floor met span tables but missed L/360 or L/720. Check deflection against the tile chosen and fix framing first.

Common failure

Hollow-sounding large tile. Poor coverage from swirled trowel ridges, no back-butter, or skinned mortar. Trowel straight, back-butter, set while the mortar is wet, and pull tiles to check.

Common failure

Leaking shower with a clogged weep system. Mortar packed into the weep holes on a traditional pan, so water backs up under the tile and finds the curb. Protect the weeps, or use a bonded membrane.

Common failure

Tented or popped floor tile. No perimeter or field movement joints, often on a sunny floor or over radiant heat. Follow EJ171 and leave the perimeter open under the base.

Common failure

Screws through the liner or membrane on curbs and the bottom of the wall, from the glass installer or the tile setter. Mark no-fastener zones on the wall and tell the glazing sub where blocking is.

Common failure

Mixed-brand systems. Voided warranties and leaks at the joints. One system per shower.

10.16 QC checklist

  • Straightedge check recorded for every tiled floor and shower wall.
  • One waterproofing system per shower, maker's components and mortars only, with product data in the job file.
  • Flood test photos (start and end marks) and date for every pan.
  • Coverage photos from pulled tiles in each room, 95% or better in wet areas.
  • Layout matches the approved drawing; no visible cut under half a tile; lippage and offset within ANSI limits.
  • Grout joints full, consistent in width and color, with no pinholes or haze.
  • Every inside corner, perimeter and change of plane in sealant, not grout, color-matched.
  • Niche sills and bench seats slope into the shower.
  • Heated floor resistance readings recorded after tile and the warranty card filled in.
  • Stone and cement grout sealed where specified; sealer product noted for the owner.
  • Attic stock labeled with room, product, lot and grout color.

10.17 Handoff to the next trade

Next in are paint touch-up (Chapter 09), plumbing trim (Chapter 06), shower glass, and cabinets and trim (Chapter 11). Leave the room like this:

  • All tile set, grouted, cleaned of haze, sealant installed and cured, sealer applied where specified.
  • Floors under breathable protection board, not plastic, so grout keeps curing. Cuts at valve and spout stubs small enough for escutcheons to cover.
  • Blocking locations for glass and grab bars marked on tape on the tile face, with no-drill zones over membrane upturns marked.
  • Vanity and toilet locations clear of heating cable and marked. Finished tile height at every doorway noted for the flooring and trim crew.
  • Leftover grout and sealant labeled for punch repairs. Saw water and buckets never emptied into new drains.
  • Finished photos in the job file before protection goes on.
