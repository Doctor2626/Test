Building Envelope: Roofing, Windows, Insulation & Facade

How ESC keeps water out, holds heat in and stops air leaks: roofs, water-resistive barriers, windows and doors, insulation, air sealing and brownstone facade work. Lead carpenters, roofing and masonry subs, insulation contractors and PMs use this chapter to pick an assembly and build it in the right order.

5.1 Where the envelope sits in the job

Envelope work happens in two campaigns. Dry-in (roof, WRB, windows, exterior doors) follows framing and sheathing (Chapter 04) so the building stops taking water before the rough trades arrive. Insulation and air sealing follow plumbing, HVAC and electrical rough-in and their inspections (Chapters 06-08) and come right before drywall or plaster (Chapter 09). Cladding and facade masonry run in parallel with interior work once openings are flashed.

A rowhouse is different from a new house. The roof is usually a low-slope membrane behind brick parapets, the front and rear walls are solid brick or brownstone-faced brick, the party walls are shared with occupied neighbors, windows go into masonry openings, and the house may sit in a Jersey City historic district, where facade, stoop, cornice, window and door work needs Historic Preservation Commission approval before it starts. A temporary roof often goes on during demolition (Chapter 02) and the permanent roof only after new steel and top-floor framing.

Code references are to the IRC 2024 (NJ edition) and the 2024 IECC, adopted under the NJ Uniform Construction Code (N.J.A.C. 5:23). Hudson County is IECC climate zone 4A (confirm with the construction official). A complete permit application submitted by February 17, 2027 may still be reviewed under the 2021 editions, so check which edition the permit names. Rather than risk a section number that changed between editions, this chapter cites the code by topic, and any energy value quoted from the 2021 IECC is flagged to confirm against the 2024 IECC table for zone 4A. The Jersey City Division of Construction Code (the construction official) governs, and in a historic district the HPC governs what the facade looks like. Manufacturer instructions are part of the code requirement for most of these products, so keep them on site.

New houses and additions are new construction and meet the IRC and IECC. Work on an existing rowhouse, such as re-roofing, replacement windows, insulating the inside of masonry walls, air sealing and other energy upgrades, falls under the NJ Rehabilitation Subcode (N.J.A.C. 5:23-6), not the IEBC. The project is classified as repair, renovation, alteration or reconstruction, and the new materials and how they're installed have to meet the subcode's materials and methods requirements (N.J.A.C. 5:23-6.8). The existing envelope isn't held to new-construction code as a whole. Confirm with the construction official which energy requirements apply to the new work under the job's classification.

5.2 Before envelope work starts

Before dry-in

  • Sheathing nailing and structural observation signed off where the construction official or engineer requires them before the WRB covers the sheathing (Chapter 04). The full framing inspection comes later, after rough-ins.
  • Roof sheathing nailed off with edges supported; rowhouse roof joists leveled or tapered to the roof plan and parapets repaired.
  • Rough openings checked against approved shop drawings: size, diagonals within 1/8 in., sill level.
  • Windows and doors ordered with confirmed ship dates (custom wood units often 12-20 weeks; stock vinyl or fiberglass 6-12 weeks; see Fig. 1.5).
  • Roofing, flashing metals, WRB and tapes on site, all from compatible product families.
  • Rowhouse: neighbor roofs and party-wall caps photographed; written access agreement signed for any work on an adjoining roof; HPC CoNE or CoA in hand where the house is in a historic district.

Approval hold

Historic district. In a Jersey City historic district (Van Vorst Park, Hamilton Park, Harsimus Cove, Paulus Hook and others), no facade, stoop, cornice, window or door work starts until the owner has a Certificate of No Effect (CoNE) or Certificate of Appropriateness (CoA) from the HPC, whether or not a construction permit is required. Review applies Zoning Ordinance §345-71 (Historic Design Standards) and the Secretary of the Interior's Standards. A CoA is valid for 1 year, so time the application to the work and keep a copy in the job file.

Before insulation

  • Rough-in for Chapters 06-08 complete and inspected. Nothing gets insulated before the rough inspections.
  • Roof and windows watertight; framing at or below 15 percent moisture on a pin meter (ESC target, Chapter 04; 19 percent is the absolute ceiling); masonry dry, not darkened.
  • Every envelope penetration installed and flashed outside, ready for air sealing inside.
  • Blocking for cabinets, grab bars, TV mounts and rails installed per the backing plan (Chapter 04).

5.3 Steep-slope roofing

Steep-slope roofs shed water by gravity and lapping. They show up on new houses, additions, dormers and bays. Most rowhouse main roofs are low-slope (5.4).

ESC default: architectural shingles on synthetic underlayment for new houses and rear additions. We switch to standing seam below 4:12 and on porch and bay roofs, and to natural slate to match or repair a historic slate roof. On a historic-district house, a change of roofing material on a roof seen from the street goes to the HPC first.

Shingle roof process

  1. Deck check. Sheathing flat, nailed off, 1/8 in. gaps, no swollen or delaminated panels.
  2. Eave drip edge. Under the underlayment, lapped 2 in., 1/4 in. below the sheathing, nailed 12 in. o.c. max (IRC drip edge provisions).
  3. Ice barrier. Self-adhered membrane from the eave to at least 24 in. inside the exterior wall line (IRC ice barrier provisions) where the construction official requires it. ESC also runs it in valleys, around chimneys and skylights and along roof-to-wall lines.
  4. Underlayment. Synthetic, shingle-lapped, cap-nailed; then rake drip edge over it.
  5. Shingles. Starter strip, then courses at the specified exposure. Nails 12 ga min with 3/8 in. heads, through the deck or 3/4 in. into it (IRC asphalt shingle fastener provisions), on the nail line, 4 or 6 per shingle per the maker and wind zone.
  6. Flashing. Step flashing, counterflashing and kickouts woven in course by course (Fig. 5.1). Ridge vent only if the attic is vented.

Flashing at walls, chimneys and parapets

Most roof leaks are flashing leaks. Each piece laps over the one below it, metal is never locked down at both ends, and sealant is never the water stop.

  • Step flashing: one L-piece per course, 4 in. min on the roof and up the wall (IRC), lapping the piece below by at least 2 in. The wall WRB laps over the vertical legs.
  • Counterflashing at masonry: let into a mortar joint about 1 in. and lapping 4 in. over the base flashing. Through-wall flashing is better when the masonry is being rebuilt anyway.
  • Cricket on the uphill side of any chimney or penetration wider than 30 in. (IRC cricket provisions).
  • Kickout wherever a roof edge dies into a wall that continues past it (IRC kickout flashing provisions).

5.4 Low-slope roofing (most rowhouse roofs)

Rowhouse roofs typically pitch from the front parapet to a rear gutter or scupper, sit behind parapets, and share party-wall caps with the neighbors. Membranes need at least 1/4 in. per foot of slope (IRC membrane roofing provisions). Ponding that lasts more than 48 hours after rain means the slope or drain is wrong.

Re-roofing an existing rowhouse is rehabilitation work (5.1): the new assembly meets the subcode's materials and methods requirements, and the insulation level for the new roof is confirmed with the construction official against the 2024 IECC for zone 4A before the tapered layout is ordered.

ESC default: fully adhered 60 mil TPO over a cover board and tapered polyiso. We switch to self-adhered 2-ply mod-bit for heavy foot traffic or parapet details too fiddly for welded sheet. ESC does not allow torch-applied roofing on wood-framed rowhouses.

Low-slope roof process

  1. Tear-off and deck. Strip to the deck (rowhouse roofs often hide three or four layers). Replace rotted plank or plywood and confirm the joists follow the planned slope (Chapter 04).
  2. Parapets first. Repoint the inside face and top, rebuild loose top courses, set treated nailers anchored to the masonry.
  3. Drains and scuppers. Set drain bodies, scuppers and the overflow (typically 2 in. above the primary) before insulation.
  4. Insulation and cover board. Vapor retarder on plank decks or over humid rooms, then tapered polyiso with staggered joints and crickets between drains, then 1/2 in. cover board fastened per the uplift design.
  5. Membrane. Adhere, roll, and weld or tape seams. Probe every TPO weld at the end of each day.
  6. Base flashing and coping. Up the parapet 8 in. min above the finished roof and over the top. Coping on a continuous cleat, pitched to the roof, legs lapping 3-4 in. down each face with a drip.
  7. Penetrations. Boots and curbs 8 in. min high. Lay out every curb and vent with Chapter 07 before the roof goes on.
  8. Water test. Flood or hose-test drains and the worst details before finishes go in below.

ESC standard

We photograph both adjoining roofs and party-wall caps before mobilizing, keep debris off them, and tie into a neighbor's membrane only with a written agreement. Otherwise we flash to the wall and counterflash so a future roofer can tie in without cutting ours. NJ gives us no right to go onto a neighbor's roof; if protecting it needs access they refuse, that work waits for a court order (N.J.A.C. 5:23-2.34).

5.5 Water-resistive barriers

Water gets behind every cladding, so the WRB, flashings and drainage gap carry it back out. The IRC requires a WRB over sheathing. Every WRB detail is shingle-lapped: upper layers over lower layers.

Mechanically attached housewrapBoard-sheathed new houses and additionsCheap, familiar, vapor-openTears in wind; only an air barrier if every seam and edge is taped$Fast
Integrated sheathing (ZIP System or similar)New constructionSheathing, WRB and air barrier in one step; easy to inspectTape rolling is the whole system; repairs need the same product family$$Fastest overall
Fluid-applied WRBMasonry backup walls, complex walls, high-performance jobsFully adhered, no laps or fasteners, excellent air barrierDry substrate and minimum temperature required; mil thickness must be checked$$$Moderate

ESC default: taped integrated sheathing on new houses and additions. We switch to fluid-applied on masonry backup walls and complex geometry, and to taped housewrap where the sheathing is already on or budget rules.

  1. Start at the bottom. Lap the WRB over the foundation flashing or weep screed.
  2. Lap up the wall. Housewrap horizontal laps 6 in. min, vertical laps 6-12 in. per the maker. ZIP seams taped and rolled hard with a J-roller.
  3. Integrate flashings. Window heads, deck ledgers and roof-to-wall legs go under the WRB above and over the WRB below.
  4. Drainage gap. 3/8 in. drainage mat or 3/4 in. furring behind siding, vented and screened top and bottom.

5.6 Windows and exterior doors

Window leaks rarely come from the window. They come from flashing out of sequence, missing sill pans and sealant used as the only barrier. ASTM E2112 is the reference installation practice, and the IRC requires flashing at openings per the maker's instructions or with pan flashing.

Historic district. In a Jersey City historic district the owner needs a CoNE or CoA before any window or door work starts, even where no construction permit is required. The HPC applies §345-71 and the Secretary of the Interior's Standards, which favor repairing original features over replacing them, so settle the method with the architect and HPC staff before anything is ordered. If window lead times would push the install past the CoA's 1-year term, plan for renewal.

Replacement windows in an existing house are rehabilitation work (5.1). Confirm with the construction official which U-factor applies to the replacement units, checked against the 2024 IECC table for zone 4A; don't assume either the new-construction number or no requirement.

ESC default: flanged units on new construction. On historic-district rowhouse fronts we restore the sash and add interior storms when frames are sound, and do profile-matched full-frame replacement when they aren't, as the CoA allows. Nothing is ordered for a historic-district elevation until the CoNE or CoA is in hand. Insert replacements are for rear elevations only, after the old frame passes a probe test.

Flanged window installation, WRB first

Fig. 5.3 shows the sequence in a housewrap wall. With taped sheathing or fluid-applied WRB the order is the same, but the opening is wrapped with tape or liquid flashing instead of cut flaps.

  1. Check the RO. Usually 1/2 in. over unit size, sill level, diagonals within 1/8 in.
  2. Cut and open the WRB. Modified I-cut, jamb and sill flaps wrapped in, head flap taped up.
  3. Sill pan. Flexible flashing up each jamb 6 in. min and 2 in. down over the WRB, or a preformed pan with end dams.
  4. Set. Sealant behind the flange at head and jambs only. Shim under jambs and mullions, plumb, level, fasten per the maker.
  5. Jamb, then head flashing. Jamb tapes from below the sill flashing to above the head; drip cap if the cladding needs it; head tape over both.
  6. Close and seal. Fold the head flap down and tape the diagonals only. Inside, low-expansion window foam or backer rod and sealant, never high-expansion foam.

Windows in masonry openings

  1. Survey. Check lintels, arches and sill stones. Replace rusted steel lintels before setting windows, shoring the masonry above.
  2. Pan the opening. Fluid-applied or self-adhered flashing on the masonry, with a sill pan and end dams that drain onto a sill stone pitched outward.
  3. Set. Shim at structural points, anchor per the maker, and keep the frame at the original setback, which HPC reviewers check in a historic district.
  4. Seal. Backer rod and sealant outside, low-expansion foam and an interior air seal to the plaster return.

Historic sash restoration

Restore when frames and sills are sound or repairable with epoxy consolidant and dutchmen. Sash go to the shop to be stripped (EPA RRP lead-safe practices for pre-1978 houses), reglued, reglazed with putty, primed and painted. In the opening, pulleys are freed, cords replaced, weights rebalanced, and weatherstripping added at the meeting rail and stops. A tight interior storm with low-e glass recovers most of the energy gap to a new window.

Insulating solid masonry walls from inside

Rowhouse front and rear walls are two or three wythes of soft brick, often brownstone-faced, with no cavity. They absorb rain and dry in both directions. Insulating the inside cuts off the heat that used to dry them, so the brick runs colder and wetter and freeze-thaw damage and joist-end rot become more likely. Control the rain first, then match the insulation to the brick.

This is rehabilitation work on an existing wall (5.1). The foam, the new stud wall and the thermal barrier meet the subcode's materials and methods requirements. Confirm with the construction official what the job's classification requires for these walls before settling on a thickness; the modest approach below is set by what the brick can tolerate.

  1. Fix rain exposure. Sound coping, working gutters and leaders, pitched sills, flashed lintels, repointed joints (5.9).
  2. Assess risk. North-facing and exposed walls, soft or underfired brick and walls already spalling are high risk. Ask the architect for a hygrothermal review or brick testing before insulating them. Party walls between heated houses don't see freeze-thaw and only need air sealing.
  3. Keep it modest. Around 2-3 in. of closed-cell foam is the usual choice on exposed solid masonry, rather than thick insulation that drives the brick colder.
  4. Protect joist ends. Leave an air space around the joist end in the pocket and seal the foam to the joist at the masonry face. Where joists are being replaced, hangers on a ledger take the wood out of the wall (Chapter 04).
  5. Build the interior wall. Studs set off the brick, drywall over all foam, no polyethylene.

5.8 Air sealing and blower-door testing

On new work the air barrier is a continuous line around the house, usually taped sheathing, the roof deck or top ceiling and the foundation, with every transition detailed. On rowhouses the big leaks are wall-to-floor joints at the front and rear, party walls (gaps connect to the neighbor's joist bays), chimneys, the parapet line and old cornice cavities.

  1. Mark the air barrier on the drawings and walk it with the lead before insulation.
  2. Big holes first. Chases, flue surrounds (fire-rated sealant and metal), soffits, tub and stair openings.
  3. Penetrations and joints. Wires, pipes, ducts, sill plates, rim joists, top plates and window gaps.
  4. Pre-drywall blower door. Depressurize to 50 Pa, find leaks with smoke or infrared, and fix them while exposed.
  5. Final test. New dwellings must be blower-door tested under the energy code (IRC Chapter 11 and the 2024 IECC). The 2021 IECC limit in climate zones 3-8 was 3.0 ACH50; confirm the limit against the 2024 IECC for zone 4A. On rowhouse rehab work, confirm with the construction official whether a test is required for the job's classification.

ESC standard

Every new house and addition gets a pre-drywall blower door, with a target of 2.0 ACH50 or better. On rowhouse gut renovations we test before and after, aim for 5.0 ACH50 or better, and report both numbers to the client, knowing that leakage through party walls counts against us.

5.9 Brownstone and masonry facade

Facade work needs above-freezing weather, mock-up approvals and, in a historic district, an HPC CoNE or CoA before any work starts. Scaffolding or staging on the sidewalk needs a sidewalk occupancy permit from the Division of Engineering, Traffic and Transportation, applied for at least 2 weeks ahead. NYC-style mandatory sidewalk sheds don't apply in Jersey City; pedestrian protection follows IBC Chapter 33 as adopted and the permit's conditions. Start approvals in preconstruction (Chapter 01).

Repointing

Repointing mortar must be softer and more vapor-permeable than the masonry, so the joint, not the brick, takes the stress and moisture. Hard Portland mortar in soft historic brick is the most common cause of spalled faces on rowhouses.

ESC default: NHL 3.5 with sand and color matched to an analyzed sample, approved by mock-up. We switch to lime putty where the HPC or architect requires it, and to Type N or O only on harder brick the mason confirms. Type S and M never go on historic facades.

Historic district. Repointing, brownstone patching, dutchmen, refacing and stoop work on a historic-district facade are reviewed by the HPC under §345-71 and the Secretary of the Interior's Standards. Every method in this section stays available, but the owner's CoNE or CoA comes before the mock-up goes up, and the mock-up is where the architect, the owner and HPC staff (where they ask to see it) confirm mortar, color and profile. Track the CoA's 1-year term on long facade jobs.

  1. Match and mock up. Sample the mortar, match sand and color, and build an approval panel.
  2. Rake out. To 2 to 2-1/2 times the joint width (1/2 in. min), square at the back, without widening joints or chipping brick edges.
  3. Fill. Pre-wet, then pack in lifts about 1/4 in. thick, each thumbprint hard before the next. Tool to the original profile.
  4. Cure. Mist and cover for at least 3 days. No work below 40°F or when frost is forecast during the cure.

Brownstone patching and refacing

Brownstone is a soft sandstone that was often face-bedded, so it delaminates in sheets.

  • Patching: cut back to sound stone, undercut the edges, pin deep patches with stainless steel and wire armature, and build up mineral-based restoration mortar in lifts to the original profile and an approved color.
  • Dutchman: cut out and replace a section with new stone pinned and set in lime mortar. Best for sills, lintels and projecting moldings.
  • Refacing: full-face recarving in restoration mortar over stainless lath where the face has failed widely; needs architect details and, in a historic district, an HPC CoA.
  • Avoid Portland stucco skim coats and non-breathable coatings. They trap water and pop off in sheets with the stone face.

Stoop restoration

  1. Survey the structure under the stoop (brick piers, iron or concrete), any vault, the railings and treads. In a historic district, confirm the HPC approval covers the stoop before demolition of any part of it.
  2. Rebuild the substrate per the engineer, with waterproofing and areaway drainage.
  3. Treads and risers. New stone or patch over stainless armature; pitch treads forward about 1/8 in. per foot; keep riser heights uniform within 3/8 in.
  4. Railings. Restore ironwork, set posts in stainless sleeves or with lead or anchoring cement, prime with rust-inhibiting primer.

5.11 Inspection holds

Inspection hold

Roof flashing. Ice barrier, underlayment, valleys and wall flashings photographed before shingles cover them. On low-slope roofs, base flashing, drains and the weld probe log reviewed before coping goes on. The construction official may also inspect; check the permit card.

Inspection hold

WRB and windows. No cladding until the PM has walked every elevation and photographed each opening's pan, jamb and head flashing. On new jobs one window is hose-tested before cladding.

Inspection hold

Insulation and air sealing. The building subcode's insulation inspection and ESC's pre-drywall blower door both pass before any board is hung. Foam thickness is gauged and the foam certificate posted.

Inspection hold

Facade mock-up. Mortar, patch color and sash paint approved on a mock-up by the architect, owner and HPC staff where they review it before production work. In a historic district, the CoNE or CoA is on file before the mock-up goes up.

5.12 Common failures and how to avoid them

Common failure

Reverse laps. Tape or WRB at a window head tucked under the layer below, sending water behind the WRB. Teach the shingle rule at every opening and photograph each one.

Common failure

Sealed sill flanges. A continuous bead under the sill flange traps water in the pan. Leave the sill open or gapped.

Common failure

Missing kickouts and short base flashing. Water runs into the wall below a roof edge, or over base flashing under 8 in. when snow drifts. Make kickouts part of the roofing scope.

Common failure

Batts and poly on brick. Fiberglass between furring on a solid brick wall with poly behind the drywall. Room air leaks around the poly, condenses on cold brick and rots furring and joist ends. Use closed-cell foam directly on the brick.

Common failure

Portland mortar on soft brick, and insulation over wet materials. Both trap water. Specify lime mortar, and meter framing and masonry before insulating.

5.13 QC checklist

  • Drip edge, ice barrier, underlayment, valleys, step and counterflashing, kickouts and crickets installed and photographed.
  • Low-slope roof hose-tested with no ponding; welds probed; base flashing 8 in. min; coping pitched to the roof; overflow in place.
  • WRB lapped shingle-fashion, seams taped and rolled, tears repaired.
  • Every opening: sill pan with end dams, sill left to drain, tapes in order, head flap closed, interior air seal.
  • Masonry openings: lintels sound, sills pitched out, perimeter sealant on backer rod.
  • Insulation full and uncompressed, density or thickness verified, vapor retarder per code, thermal barrier over all foam.
  • Pre-drywall blower-door result recorded and leaks fixed.
  • HPC CoNE or CoA on file and within its 1-year term for any historic-district facade, stoop, cornice, window or door work.
  • Facade mock-up approved, joints raked to depth, mortar cured, patches matched, stoop treads pitched.

5.14 Handoff to the next trade

After dry-in the building is watertight for the rough trades: roof complete or under a tight temporary membrane, every window and door set and flashed or the opening taped closed, and the WRB intact. Crews from Chapters 06-08 clear any new envelope hole with the lead, and ESC or the roofer flashes it.

After insulation, the space handed to drywall and plaster (Chapter 09) looks like this:

  • Insulation inspection passed and posted; pre-drywall blower door passed.
  • Cavities full, foam trimmed flush with the framing, nothing proud of the studs.
  • Vapor retarder taped where required; no polyethylene on masonry walls.
  • Window and door gaps air-sealed, ready for returns and casings (Chapter 11).
  • Foam waste removed, floors swept, and indoor humidity below about 60 percent before board or plaster goes on.
