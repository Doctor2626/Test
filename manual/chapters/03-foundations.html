Divisions 03 & 31 · Concrete and Earthwork

Excavation, Footings & Foundations

How ESC lays out, digs, forms, reinforces, pours and waterproofs the part of the house that sits in the ground, from a new spread footing on open ground to underpinning a 120-year-old party wall. Field leads, PMs, excavation and concrete subs, and owners who want to understand the most expensive work they'll never see all use this chapter.

3.1 Where foundations sit in the job

Foundation work starts once the site is cleared and shored (Chapter 02) and ends when framing can set a sill plate on a straight, level, dry wall (Chapter 04). It is the least forgiving trade in the house. A footing poured an inch out of position or on soft soil can't be fixed with shims later.

On a new house or addition: layout, excavation, footing inspection and pour, walls, waterproofing and drainage, under-slab rough-ins, slab, backfill. On a rowhouse gut, the foundation exists and is usually shared: we underpin or bench the party walls to lower the cellar, add pads for new steel posts, and pour a new slab with the drainage and vapor barrier the house never had.

Code references are to the IRC 2024 (NJ edition), adopted under the NJ Uniform Construction Code (N.J.A.C. 5:23), and to ACI 318, 117 and 306 unless noted. Rather than risk a section number that changed between editions, this chapter cites the IRC by topic. Plumbing under and through the foundation follows the National Standard Plumbing Code (NSPC 2024, NJ edition), not the IRC plumbing chapters, and electrical follows the NEC 2023 with NJ amendments. A complete permit application submitted by February 17, 2027 may still be reviewed under the previous editions, so check which edition the permit names. The Jersey City Division of Construction Code (the construction official, our authority having jurisdiction) governs, and the engineer's drawings govern over this chapter. Underpinning is always engineered work.

New houses and additions are new construction and meet the IRC. Work in an existing house, including underpinning, lowering a cellar, new post pads and a new cellar slab, falls under the NJ Rehabilitation Subcode (N.J.A.C. 5:23-6), not the IEBC. The project is classified as repair, renovation, alteration or reconstruction (a rowhouse gut is usually reconstruction), and the new work has to meet the subcode's materials and methods requirements (N.J.A.C. 5:23-6.8). New pins, footings and slabs are designed and built to current standards; the existing foundation isn't held to new-construction code as a whole. The engineer and the construction official settle what the classification requires on each job.

3.2 Before we dig: prerequisites

Nothing gets excavated until the following is in hand. Most of it comes out of preconstruction (Chapter 01) and demolition (Chapter 02).

  • Permit issued, with stamped foundation drawings and, for underpinning, the engineer's sequence and special inspection agreement. Jersey City issues the permit only after the prior approvals are in hand, including zoning, Historic Preservation where it applies, and a JCMUA release for footing and foundation work. Start the JCMUA release early; it gates the whole chapter.
  • Boundary and topographic survey with a benchmark elevation; for additions, the existing finished floor tied to that benchmark.
  • Geotechnical report or, for small additions where the construction official allows it, the presumptive soil values from the IRC (NJ edition) load-bearing table confirmed by the engineer.
  • NJ One Call (811) ticket called in at least 3 full business days before digging (weekends and NJ holidays don't count). Digging must start within 10 business days of the call, and the markout is valid for 45 business days, so call again if the job slips past that. Private lines (sewer lateral, old oil tanks, yard drains) located by hand.
  • For attached houses: neighbor notices, written access agreements, a dated photo condition survey of both neighbors, and crack monitors or survey targets where the engineer calls for them. NJ has no party-wall statute giving us a right to enter. If protecting the neighbor's property means going onto it and they refuse, that work doesn't proceed unless a court grants access (N.J.A.C. 5:23-2.34).
  • Demo complete to the depth needed; existing slab broken out and hauled; temporary shoring from Chapter 02 inspected and signed off.
  • Sewer invert, under-slab drain layout and sump location confirmed with the plumber (Chapter 06); mix designs, pump and rebar shop drawings approved.

3.3 Layout

Layout turns the drawings into string lines on the ground. We set batter boards outside the dig so the lines can be pulled again after the excavator has torn out every stake inside the hole.

  1. Establish control. Find the surveyor's pins and benchmark. Shoot the benchmark with a builder's level or rotary laser and mark a reference elevation (usually top of foundation wall or top of slab) on a fixed object off site of the dig.
  2. Rough-stake the corners. Measure from the property line or existing building per the site plan. Set hubs with tacks at each outside corner of the foundation wall.
  3. Square it. Check one corner with a 3-4-5 triangle (or 6-8-10, 9-12-15 for longer legs), then check both diagonals. Adjust until diagonals match within 1/4 in on a typical house footprint.
  4. Set batter boards. At each corner, drive three 2x4 stakes 4 to 8 ft outside the excavation line and screw two 2x ledgers in an L. Set every ledger top to the same elevation with the laser, usually top of wall.
  5. Transfer the lines. Pull mason's line over the ledgers so the lines cross directly over the hubs. Mark each ledger with a saw kerf or a screw so the line can be removed and reset exactly.
  6. Mark the dig. Drop a plumb bob or laser at each string intersection, then paint the excavation line on the ground: footing outside edge plus 2 ft of working room, more where we'll form, waterproof or set drainage.
  7. Recheck after the dig. Re-pull lines, re-check diagonals, and mark footing edges in the hole before forming.

On a rowhouse, layout is mostly elevations: footing bottoms shot at test pits, then pin bays painted and tagged along each wall.

Without a geotech report the IRC allows presumptive values. A report overrides them, and we want one wherever there's fill, old cisterns or a high water table, which in Jersey City covers a lot of sites.

Frost depth

Footings bear below the frost line set by the construction official and at least 12 in below undisturbed grade (IRC footing depth and frost protection provisions). In NJ the usual figure is 36 in below finished grade. Treat that as typical and confirm it with the Jersey City construction official at design: Jersey City fill and the high water table often push footings deeper. Interior footings in a heated basement don't need frost depth, but footings under porches, stoops, garages and unheated additions do. The exception is a frost-protected shallow foundation designed to the IRC FPSF provisions.

Bottom of excavation

  1. Dig to grade with a smooth bucket. The last 2 to 4 in come off with a toothless cleanout bucket or by hand so the bearing soil isn't churned up.
  2. Probe it. A 1/2 in probe rod or hand penetrometer every few feet. Mark soft spots, wet pockets, old foundations and debris.
  3. Over-excavate soft spots. Dig to firm soil, then backfill to footing bottom with lean concrete or with 3/4 in crushed stone compacted in 6 in lifts, as the engineer directs. Never with loose native soil.
  4. Step the footing where grade changes (IRC stepped footing provisions): tops level, bottom slope no steeper than 1 in 10.

Common failure

Pouring on mud or frozen ground, which settles when it thaws or dries. If the bottom is a puddle the morning of the pour, cancel the truck. A one-day slip costs far less than a cracked foundation.

Excavation safety follows OSHA 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P: cuts 5 ft or deeper get sloping, benching, a trench box or shoring set by a competent person.

3.5 Footing types compared

The footing spreads the load of the house onto soil the soil can carry. The IRC footing-size provisions set the minimums for prescriptive work: 12 in wide and 6 in thick, with the projection beyond the wall face at least 2 in and no more than the footing thickness. Most of our engineered footings are wider and thicker than that.

Pouring a spread footing

  1. Form. 2x10 or 2x12 side forms (or steel) on the edge lines, staked every 4 ft, tops shot within 1/2 in. Earth-formed trench footings only if the engineer allows.
  2. Reinforce and embed. Bars on dobies for 3 in cover, laps and corner bars tied, dowels or keyway set, drain and sewer sleeves placed, and the concrete-encased electrode stub (NEC 2023, 250.52(A)(3)). NEC 250.50 requires it to be bonded wherever a new footing has qualifying steel, so the NJ licensed electrical contractor sets the stub-up location before the pour.
  3. Inspect, pour, cure. Inspection per 3.13, then place, rod or vibrate, strike off, leave the top rough or keyed. Keep moist; strip after about 24 hours in mild weather.

Wall forming and pour

  1. Snap and set. Snap both wall faces on the footing and recheck diagonals. Outside forms, then steel, then inside forms with ties.
  2. Bucks and blockouts for windows, beam pockets and every utility sleeve, coordinated with Chapters 06 and 08 before the inside form closes.
  3. Pour in lifts of no more than 2 to 4 ft, vibrating each lift into the one below. Don't drop concrete more than the forming sub's limit without a tremie or elephant trunk.
  4. Set anchor bolts right after strike-off: 1/2 in minimum, 7 in embedment, 6 ft max spacing, within 12 in of each plate end, at least two per plate (IRC foundation anchorage provisions), unless the engineer specifies straps or larger bolts.
  5. Strip at 24 to 48 hours. Patch tie holes and honeycombing before waterproofing.

3.7 Underpinning existing rowhouse walls

Lowering a cellar in an attached house means the new floor ends up below the bottom of the existing party-wall footings, which are often a few courses of corbelled brick or rubble stone sitting 12 to 24 in below the old floor. Underpinning extends those walls down in short, alternating sections so the wall is never unsupported over more than a small length.

Underpinning is designed by a licensed engineer, almost always requires special inspection, and on an occupied neighbor's wall needs the agreements and monitoring listed in 3.2. The engineer sets the pin width, depth, sequence and the waiting time between sequences. The steps below are how ESC runs the work inside that design.

Two NJ rules sit on top of the engineering. Under N.J.S.A. 46:10-1, an owner excavating 8 ft or deeper next to a party wall or adjoining wall has to protect that wall at their own expense. On our jobs that owner is our client, and the protection is the engineer's underpinning or shoring design; confirm the statute's current text with the owner's attorney on any deep dig. Second, NJ gives us no party-wall right of entry. Any step that needs the neighbor's side, such as monitors in their cellar or shoring from their property, needs a written agreement first, and if they refuse it waits for a court order (N.J.A.C. 5:23-2.34). In code terms, underpinning and lowering are rehabilitation work: the new pins, dry-pack and slab meet the materials and methods requirements of N.J.A.C. 5:23-6.8 and the engineer's design.

  1. Test pits and baseline. Dig two or three pits per wall to find footing bottom, width and soil for the engineer. Read crack gauges and survey targets before the first bay opens.
  2. Mark bays. Paint and tag bays along the wall, typically 3 to 4 ft wide, numbered by sequence. No two adjacent bays share a sequence number.
  3. Excavate sequence 1. Dig the approach pit on our side, then hand-dig under the existing footing to the new bearing elevation. Shore the pit faces as needed. Clean the underside of the old footing of loose soil and mortar.
  4. Inspect the bearing. The special inspector checks the bottom soil and dimensions before any concrete.
  5. Form and pour the pin. Set the form on our side, add rebar dowels into adjacent bays if the engineer calls for them, and pour the pin to within 2 to 3 in of the underside of the old footing.
  6. Dry-pack. After the pin has cured the specified time (often 24 to 48 hours), ram a stiff, low-water sand-cement mortar into the gap with a hardwood packing stick and hammer, in layers, until it's packed tight across the full width. Some engineers allow non-shrink grout in place of dry-pack. Follow the drawings.
  7. Next sequence. Open the next numbered bays only after the previous ones are dry-packed and have reached the strength or age the engineer specifies. Read the monitors daily and stop work if movement exceeds the engineer's trigger values.

Bench footings and basement lowering

Where the cellar only needs to drop a modest amount, or the neighbor won't agree to underpinning, a bench can lower the middle of the floor while leaving the soil wedge under the existing footing undisturbed. The new floor steps down at a line set back from the footing toe, typically no closer than a 1:1 (45 degree) line drawn down from the bottom edge of the existing footing on the excavation side. The engineer sets the actual line by soil type. A short retaining curb or concrete bench holds the step and gives a useful ledge for mechanical runs or a built-in seat.

The cost is floor area: at 1:1, each foot of drop below the footing costs about a foot of width along each wall. On a 16 to 20 ft wide rowhouse, deep lowering usually means underpinning.

Common failure

Opening adjacent bays to "catch up" on schedule, or digging the pin before the dry-pack in the next bay over has cured. That is how party walls crack and neighbors' floors drop. The sequence on the drawing is a hard rule, and the field lead stops work if anyone breaks it.

3.9 Concrete: mix, placing and curing

Ordering

We order by mix design from the supplier, not by "bag count." The IRC (NJ edition) minimum concrete strength table sets strength by weathering exposure: 2,500 psi for basement walls and footings not exposed to weather, 3,000 psi for exposed walls in moderate and severe weathering regions, and 3,500 psi for porches, steps and garage slabs in severe regions, with air entrainment for exposed concrete in moderate and severe weathering areas. We order higher than those minimums as a matter of course.

Placing

  1. Check the ticket. Mix, time batched and water held back. ASTM C94 calls for discharge within 90 minutes or 300 drum revolutions unless the purchaser waives it. Reject old loads.
  2. Water control. Add water only up to the amount withheld on the ticket; use plasticizer for more flow. Extra water costs strength and adds shrinkage cracks.
  3. Test where required. When special inspection or the engineer requires it, the testing agency takes slump, air, temperature and cylinders (ASTM C31) at the truck.
  4. Place and consolidate. Pump or chute close to final position and vibrate each lift.
  5. Finish slabs in order: screed, bull float, let bleed water leave, then edge, joint and trowel.

Curing and weather

  • Curing. Keep concrete moist or covered for at least 7 days: wet curing blankets, poly sheeting, or a curing compound compatible with any later flooring adhesive (check Chapter 11 and Chapter 10 before using one on a slab that gets tile or wood).
  • Cold weather (ACI 306, air at or forecast below 40°F during the protection period). Never place on frozen ground or against frozen forms. Use heated water and accelerator from the plant (non-chloride where there's steel), cover with insulated blankets, and keep concrete at 55°F or above for the protection period in sections under 12 in thick (most footings and walls), 50°F for thicker sections. Concrete must not freeze before it reaches about 500 psi.
  • Hot weather (ACI 305). Pour early, wet the subgrade, use retarder, cure immediately.
  • Loading. Wall forms stripped at 24 to 48 hours; no backfill until the wall is braced or the floor framing and slab are in (IRC backfill provisions); full design strength at 28 days.

3.10 Slabs on grade

A new rowhouse cellar slab is our chance to fix water, radon and cold floors at once.

  1. Subgrade. Trim and compact. Under-slab plumbing (Chapter 06) roughed, tested to the NSPC and inspected by the plumbing subcode; trenches backfilled with stone and compacted.
  2. Base. 4 in min clean 3/4 in stone, screeded. It doubles as the radon gas-permeable layer, with a 3 or 4 in vent stub-up (see Radon below).
  3. Insulation. Under-slab rated XPS or EPS. Hudson County is IECC climate zone 4A (confirm with the construction official), so take the required slab R-value from the 2024 IECC table for zone 4A. ESC standard is R-10 full under conditioned basement slabs; confirm it meets the 2024 IECC requirement on each job.
  4. Vapor retarder. The IRC slab provisions require 6-mil polyethylene minimum. We use a 15-mil ASTM E1745 Class A retarder directly under the slab, seams lapped 6 in and taped, turned up the walls to slab top, pipe penetrations booted.
  5. Isolation and perimeter. Isolation joint material around columns and at walls. Perimeter interior drain, if any, set per 3.11.
  6. Reinforcing. Wire or #3/#4 bars on chairs. Wire "hooked up" off the poly during the pour doesn't count.
  7. Pour, finish, joint, cure. See tolerances below and Fig. 3.5.

Radon

NJ requires radon-resistant construction in Tier 1 municipalities (N.J.A.C. 5:23-10). Confirm Jersey City's tier with the construction official at permit; don't assume it either way. The stone base, sealed vapor retarder and sealed sump lid already in our slab are most of a passive system, and a vent stub costs very little while the slab is open.

ESC default: every new cellar or basement slab gets a passive sub-slab radon stub: a 3 or 4 in pipe from the stone layer, sealed through the retarder, capped and labeled, with a route to the roof noted on the drawings so a fan can be added if a later test comes back high. Where the municipality is Tier 1, the full radon-resistant system is built to N.J.A.C. 5:23-10.

3.11 Waterproofing, drainage and sump

Dampproofing (IRC foundation dampproofing provisions) is a thin bituminous coating that slows vapor and capillary moisture. It does not stop water under pressure. Waterproofing is required by the IRC where a high water table or severe soil-water conditions exist, which describes many Jersey City sites, and is a membrane system that bridges small cracks and resists hydrostatic pressure. Any basement that will be finished living space gets waterproofing from ESC, regardless of what the minimum allows.

ESC default: fluid-applied membrane plus drainage board and exterior footing drain on new basement walls; dampproofing only on crawlspaces and unfinished space. In rowhouse cellars, interior perimeter drain in stone at the base of the walls, dimple membrane up the masonry behind the new framing, and a sealed sump with a battery-backup pump. We add exterior membrane on the front and rear walls when they're excavated anyway.

Footing drain and sump

  • 4 in perforated pipe, holes down, on at least 2 in of washed stone and covered with at least 6 in, wrapped in filter fabric, alongside the footing (IRC foundation drainage provisions). Pipe top no higher than the bottom of the slab.
  • Outlet to daylight or a sump; never to the sanitary sewer unless the construction official and JCMUA allow it. Discharge to grade well away from the house or to a dry well, per local rules.
  • Sump: 18 to 24 in diameter liner, sealed lid (radon and moisture), check valve, primary pump on a dedicated circuit (Chapter 08), battery or water-powered backup.
  • Cleanouts at the high end of each run so the drain can be jetted later.

Backfill

Backfill only after waterproofing is inspected, drainage board and drain are in, and the wall is braced or framed (IRC backfill provisions). Place clean granular fill in 8 to 12 in lifts, compacted with a plate or jumping jack, not the excavator bucket. Keep heavy equipment at least the wall height away from an unbraced wall. Final grade slopes away at 6 in in the first 10 ft (IRC grading provisions) or swales where the lot is tight.

3.13 Inspection holds

Inspection hold

Footing / excavation. Bearing soil exposed and firm, dimensions and elevations right, forms set, rebar and dowels tied on supports, grounding electrode in place. No concrete is ordered for delivery until the inspection is passed or scheduled with the inspector's confirmation. Request it from the Jersey City building subcode as soon as the date firms up; NJ rules call for inspections within 3 business days of the request (typical; confirm current practice with the office).

Inspection hold

Underpinning, each bay. Special inspector checks bearing soil, pin dimensions and steel before the pour, and dry-pack before the next sequence opens. Monitoring readings reviewed by the engineer at the frequency in the design.

Inspection hold

Foundation wall steel where the construction official or engineer requires it, before the inside forms close. Under-slab: plumbing test (NSPC, plumbing subcode inspection), insulation, vapor retarder and radon stub before the slab pour.

Inspection hold

Waterproofing and drainage before backfill. The construction official may inspect this; ESC always does an internal walk and photographs every face, termination and penetration.

3.15 QC checklist

  • NJ One Call ticket current (dig started within 10 business days, markout under 45 business days old); JCMUA release and other prior approvals in the permit file.
  • Layout diagonals within 1/4 in; bearing soil probed, logged and soft spots replaced.
  • Footing size, steel, cover and dowels match drawings; photos with tape in frame.
  • Concrete tickets saved; no water added beyond the withheld amount; cylinders taken where required.
  • Wall lines, openings, sleeves, beam pockets and anchor bolts verified before pour.
  • Walls plumb and tops level within tolerance; tie holes and honeycombing patched.
  • Underpinning: each bay signed off by special inspector; dry-pack complete; monitoring log current.
  • Waterproofing continuous from footing to above grade; drainage board and termination bar on; penetrations sealed.
  • Footing drain wrapped and flow-tested; sump with sealed lid, check valve and backup pump.
  • Under-slab plumbing tested and inspected; insulation, vapor retarder lapped and taped; radon stub up and labeled.
  • Slab joints cut the same day; curing in place 7 days; finish flat to the finish trade's spec.
  • As-built elevations of top of wall, top of slab and drain invert recorded for Chapter 04 and Chapter 06.

3.16 Handoff to framing

We hand the hole to the framing crew (Chapter 04) when it looks like this:

  • Foundation walls cured, stripped, patched, waterproofed, drained and backfilled, or braced with a written note of when backfill can happen.
  • Top of wall level within ±1/4 in over the house, with a marked-up plan of actual elevations and any shim locations.
  • Anchor bolts or straps in place and on the layout; beam pockets sized with steel bearing plates set if called for.
  • In rowhouses: underpinning complete and signed off, monitoring baseline recorded for the framing phase, and new pad footings for steel posts poured, cured and marked with post centerlines.
  • Slab poured, joints cut, covered or protected from framing traffic, and a stated date when it can be loaded with lumber.
  • Sump, drain cleanouts, radon stub and all sleeves labeled and protected.
  • Spoils gone, edges fenced or backfilled, and the job file complete: inspection cards, photos, tickets, cylinder results and monitoring logs.
