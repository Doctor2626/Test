Divisions 26 & 27 · Electrical and Communications

Electrical & Low Voltage

How ESC sizes services, lays out circuits, roughs, inspects and trims power, lighting, life-safety and low-voltage systems in new houses and gutted masonry rowhouses, with the different wiring and control methods and when we use each. Written for lead carpenters, licensed electrical and low-voltage subs, PMs and owners reviewing a lighting or device plan.

8.1 Where electrical sits in the job

Electrical work happens in four passes. Each one has an inspection or a PSE&G sign-off attached to it.

  1. Temporary power and service planning. During preconstruction (Chapter 01) we file the PSE&G service request, because PSE&G work orders and meter sets often take 6 to 12 weeks. On a rowhouse gut, the existing service usually feeds GFCI-protected temporary power during demolition (Chapter 02).
  2. Rough-in. Boxes, cable or conduit, recessed housings, low-voltage cabling and the panel can. Follows plumbing (Chapter 06) and HVAC (Chapter 07) rough, because cable can bend around pipe and duct and they can't bend around cable.
  3. Service. Meter socket, service conductors, grounding electrode system and main panel. Often inspected separately so PSE&G can set the meter early.
  4. Trim (finish). Devices, fixtures, breakers, labeling and testing, after paint (Chapter 09), tile (Chapter 10) and cabinets and counters (Chapter 11).

New Jersey has adopted the NEC 2023 (NFPA 70) with NJ amendments under the Uniform Construction Code, along with the IRC 2024 (NJ edition) for one- and two-family dwellings. During the grace period, a complete application submitted with all prior approvals by February 17, 2027 may still follow the NEC 2020, but the application must say which NEC edition the design follows. Confirm the edition on the permit before layout starts; where this chapter flags an NEC 2023 change, it matters on a job filed under NEC 2020. The electrical subcode of the Jersey City Division of Construction Code reviews the permit and makes the inspections.

New Jersey

All electrical work is done by an NJ licensed electrical contractor holding a business permit (N.J.S.A. 45:5A), even where an owner exemption might apply. NJ has no low-voltage exemption from the business permit, so Cat6, coax, AV, security and access control wiring is also done under a licensed electrical contractor's business permit. If a low-voltage or AV sub isn't itself a licensed electrical contractor, the PM confirms how that sub works under one before the contract is signed. PSE&G handles service work, meters and service upgrades.

8.2 Before electrical starts

The electrician should not walk into a rough-in that's still moving. These items come from earlier trades and must be complete or answered.

  • Permit drawings show the panel location, service size and a load calculation; the PSE&G service application is filed and the meter location is approved by PSE&G.
  • Framing (Chapter 04) is complete and punched: blocking for heavy fixtures, fans, TV mounts and grab bars installed; soffits and chases framed; beams and engineered joists marked with their no-bore zones.
  • Plumbing drains and vents (Chapter 06) and ductwork and line sets (Chapter 07) are roughed, so we route around fixed pipe rather than the reverse.
  • Kitchen and bath cabinet shop drawings approved (Chapter 11): appliance specs, hood, under-cabinet lighting, island depth and countertop height fixed.
  • Lighting plan, switching plan and low-voltage plan signed by the owner and architect, with fixture cut sheets for every recessed or specialty fixture.
  • Rowhouse only: existing wiring traced and labeled; live circuits identified; knob-and-tube located (Section 8.13); party-wall routing agreed (no chasing a shared wall without the structural and party-wall review in Chapter 02).
  • Nameplate data for the heat pump, EV charger, range, water heater and dryer in hand for the load calculation.

8.3 Step-by-step process

  1. Load calculation and service decision. Run NEC Article 220 (the optional dwelling method in 220.82 is typical) with real nameplate loads, including future EV and heat pump circuits. Choose 100, 200 or 400 A, or a smaller service with load management (Section 8.4).
  2. Layout walk. Electrician, PM and lead carpenter walk the framed house with the lighting plan. Mark every box, recessed light and low-voltage drop on the framing with paint or a lumber crayon. Owner attends or signs the marked plan.
  3. Panel can and service equipment. Set the panel enclosure, meter socket and service raceway. Drive ground rods or tie into the concrete-encased electrode left by the foundation crew (Chapter 03).
  4. Boxes and housings. Nail or screw boxes set to finished wall thickness (1/2 in drywall, 5/8 in drywall, plaster, or tile on backer). Install recessed housings, fan boxes and remodel-rated boxes where framing is existing.
  5. Bore and pull. Bore studs and joists within code limits (Fig. 8.3), install nail plates, and pull home runs to the panel. Label every home run at the panel with circuit number and room.
  6. Make up. Splice grounds, fold conductors into boxes, leave 6 in of free conductor and at least 3 in outside the box (NEC 300.14). Check box fill.
  7. Low-voltage rough. Data, coax, speaker, security and intercom cable pulled to the structured media enclosure, with separation from power (Section 8.12).
  8. Rough inspection. Inspection hold. Nothing gets insulated or closed until it passes.
  9. Trim. Devices, plates, fixtures, breakers and appliances connected after paint and tile. Torque lugs and breaker terminals to the listed values with a torque screwdriver.
  10. Test and label. Test every GFCI and AFCI, check polarity and ground with a plug-in tester, test smoke and CO interconnection, and type the panel directory.
  11. Final inspection and PSE&G release. Inspection hold. Final electrical sign-off is a prerequisite for the certificate of occupancy or final building sign-off.

Load calculation

The electrician owns the calc; the PM checks the inputs against nameplates: heat pump minimum circuit ampacity (MCA), strip heat size, range kW and the EV charger setting. A 48 A charger needs a 60 A circuit, because EV charging is a continuous load sized at 125% (NEC 625.41). A 40 A charger takes a 50 A circuit.

EV and heat pump readiness

Even when the owner has no EV, we leave the house ready. Where the owner declines a charger, we install a 1 in empty conduit or a #6 Cu circuit from the panel to the parking location, plus two reserved breaker spaces labeled. Check with the construction official whether EV-ready parking applies to the project. For heat pumps, reserve two-pole spaces for the outdoor unit and the air handler or strip heat even on a gas-heat job.

ESC default: 200 A service with a 40-space panel on every full gut and new house. We switch to 400 A when the calc exceeds about 160 A with EV and heat pump loads included, and to load management when PSE&G or the vault in a rowhouse front areaway can't support an upgrade on schedule.

Panel location

The main panel goes where the service enters, usually the cellar front wall in a rowhouse and the garage or utility room in a new house. NEC 110.26 sets the working space: 30 in wide (or the width of the equipment if wider), 36 in deep in front, and 6 ft 6 in of headroom, kept clear of pipes and ducts above the panel. The panel can't go in a bathroom (240.24(E)), a clothes closet (240.24(D)) or over the steps of a stair (240.24(F)). The top breaker handle can't be more than 6 ft 7 in above the floor (240.24(A)).

Rowhouse cellars are often damp. Mount the panel on a 3/4 in plywood or PVC backer spaced off the masonry, keep it well above any past water line, and never land it under a waste stack or cleanout.

Subpanels

A 3- or 4-story rowhouse loses a lot of copper and voltage running every circuit to the cellar. A subpanel on an upper floor (often a hall closet wall, which is allowed as long as it's outside the clothes closet itself) shortens home runs. Feeders to a subpanel are four-wire: two ungrounded conductors, an insulated neutral and an equipment grounding conductor. The neutral bar in the subpanel is isolated from the enclosure; the bonding screw or strap is removed. Neutral-to-ground bonding happens only at the service disconnect.

8.5 Wiring methods

The wiring method is set first by the code and building type, second by the framing, and third by cost. In New Jersey, NM cable is generally permitted in one- and two-family dwellings under the NEC as adopted. For larger buildings and mixed-use (a rowhouse with a ground-floor shop, or a conversion to three or more units), confirm with the electrical subcode official before pricing, because NM may be limited by construction type and MC or raceway may be needed. Out-of-state rules, such as conduit everywhere, don't carry over to NJ work.

ESC default: NM cable in wood-framed walls and ceilings in one- and two-family work, as the NEC adopted in NJ generally permits, MC cable for exposed cellar runs and anywhere NM is restricted, and EMT only where required or for unfinished mechanical rooms. On masonry party walls we fur out with 2x2 or 1-5/8 in steel channel and run inside the furring rather than chasing the brick.

Routing in rowhouses

Rowhouse joists usually span party wall to party wall, pocketed into the brick, so home runs to a front cellar panel cross every joist from back to front. Bore through the middle third of the joist depth within the limits in Fig. 8.3, and stack vertical runs in one chase per floor, usually beside the stair or plumbing stack. Never notch the bottom of a joist that's sagging or has rot at the pocket: flag it to the framing lead (Chapter 04).

8.6 Circuit protection: AFCI, GFCI and tamper-resistant receptacles

NEC 2023 requires arc-fault protection on 120 V, 15 and 20 A branch circuits supplying outlets in nearly every habitable room (210.12(A)) and ground-fault protection for receptacles in wet and service areas (210.8(A)). Because kitchens and laundry areas need both, we use dual-function (DF) breakers that provide AFCI and GFCI in one device. On renovations, a modified, replaced or extended branch circuit needs AFCI protection (210.12(D)), except short extensions of 6 ft or less with no new outlets.

All 15 and 20 A, 125 V receptacles in a dwelling must be tamper-resistant (406.12), with limited exceptions such as receptacles more than 5-1/2 ft above the floor or dedicated to an appliance that isn't easily moved. ESC installs TR receptacles everywhere so nobody has to remember the exceptions.

Common failure

Some inverter-driven heat pumps and older appliances nuisance-trip GFCI and AFCI devices. Before ordering, check the outdoor unit manufacturer's guidance on GFCI compatibility and the electrical subcode official's current position on 210.8(F). A nuisance trip in January means no heat.

8.7 Receptacle layout

General wall receptacles

In habitable rooms, NEC 210.52(A) requires receptacles so that no point along the floor line of any wall is more than 6 ft from a receptacle. In practice that means receptacles no more than 12 ft apart along a wall, and one within 6 ft of each door opening or wall end. Any wall space 2 ft or wider counts, including the wall beside a door. Fixed glass panels count as wall; sliding door panels don't.

Kitchen countertops

  • At least two 20 A small-appliance branch circuits serve the countertop and dining-area receptacles (210.11(C)(1)). We alternate them along the counter so two high-wattage appliances side by side don't share a circuit.
  • Every countertop space 12 in or wider gets a receptacle, and no point along the wall line of the counter may be more than 24 in from a receptacle (210.52(C)(1)). That works out to 48 in maximum between receptacles and 24 in maximum from each end.
  • Range tops, sinks and refrigerators split the counter into separate spaces, each measured on its own.
  • Receptacles mount on or above the counter, not more than 20 in above it (NEC 2023 210.52(C)(3)), and may not face up in the countertop surface unless listed for it.
  • Islands and peninsulas: NEC 2023 no longer requires a receptacle, but if one is provided it must follow the location rules. A job filed under NEC 2020 during the NJ grace period still requires at least one. Confirm the edition on the permit during design so the island can be detailed for a side-mounted or pop-up listed assembly.
  • Dedicated circuits: refrigerator, dishwasher, disposal, microwave, hood, range or cooktop and wall oven.

Bathrooms, laundry and outdoors

Each bathroom needs at least one receptacle within 3 ft of the outside edge of each basin, on a wall or partition adjacent to the basin or on the countertop side of the vanity (210.52(D)). It can't be more than 12 in below the countertop. Bathroom receptacles need a 20 A circuit (210.11(C)(3)). ESC runs a dedicated 20 A circuit per primary bath because of hair tools and heated towel bars. Exhaust fans and lights go on a separate circuit so a tripped GFCI doesn't leave the room dark.

Laundry gets its own 20 A circuit (210.11(C)(2)) plus a 30 A, 240 V circuit for an electric dryer. Outdoors, a one-family house needs a GFCI receptacle at the front and back, accessible from grade and not more than 6 ft 6 in above grade (210.52(E)), plus one at each balcony, deck or porch accessible from inside. In a rowhouse, the "front" receptacle often lands at the stoop or areaway.

8.8 Box heights and placement

Most box heights are convention, not code. Mark heights to center of box from finished floor, and tell the electrician the finished floor thickness. Leveling an old rowhouse floor can move a box 2 in.

8.9 Lighting

Recessed lighting goes in at rough, so the fixture type has to be chosen before the layout walk. In rowhouses, the ceiling below a roof or a top-floor attic is part of the air barrier and thermal envelope (Chapter 05), and every hole in it leaks. IRC N1102.4.5 (IECC R402.4.5) requires recessed fixtures in the building envelope to be IC-rated, sealed to the drywall with a gasket or caulk, and labeled for no more than 2.0 cfm air leakage when tested to ASTM E283.

8.10 Smoke and CO alarms

IRC 2024 (NJ edition) R314 requires smoke alarms in each sleeping room, outside each separate sleeping area in the immediate vicinity of the bedrooms, and on every story including basements. In new construction and in alterations that require a permit, alarms are hardwired with battery backup and interconnected so that one sounds all (R314.4, R314.6). Where finishes aren't removed, listed wireless interconnected alarms are allowed. CO alarms are required outside each sleeping area where the house has a fuel-burning appliance or an attached garage (R315). An all-electric house with no attached garage may not require them; ESC installs combination smoke/CO units anyway.

  • Ceiling mount at least 4 in from the wall; wall mount with the top 4 in to 12 in below the ceiling.
  • At least 3 ft horizontally from a bathroom door with a tub or shower and from supply registers (NFPA 72 spacing referenced by the IRC).
  • Keep every alarm at least 10 ft from a cooking appliance, and use photoelectric alarms within 20 ft of one (NFPA 72).

8.11 Grounding and bonding

Grounding connects the system to earth; bonding ties metal parts together so a fault trips the breaker fast. The grounding electrode system (NEC 250.50) must use every electrode present at the building:

  • Concrete-encased electrode (Ufer). At least 20 ft of 1/2 in or larger rebar, or 20 ft of #4 bare copper, in a footing in direct contact with earth (250.52(A)(3)). On new houses we coordinate with the foundation crew (Chapter 03) to leave a rebar stub or copper tail accessible above the slab. It's the best electrode available and costs nothing if planned.
  • Ground rods. 8 ft long, driven full length. A single rod must test at 25 ohms or less or be supplemented by a second rod at least 6 ft away (250.53(A)(2) and (A)(3)). We drive two rather than test. In a rowhouse cellar, drive through the slab or in the rear yard and run the GEC exposed and protected.
  • Metal water pipe. If 10 ft or more of metal water pipe is in contact with earth, it's an electrode and must be supplemented (250.53(D)). The connection is within the first 5 ft of where the pipe enters the building. Install a jumper around the water meter.

Bond interior metal water piping (250.104(A)) and metal gas piping (250.104(B)). CSST gas piping follows the manufacturer's bonding instructions, typically a #6 Cu jumper from the gas service near the meter to the grounding electrode system. When a rowhouse replaces a copper or lead water service with plastic, the old water-pipe ground is gone; add rods or another electrode during the same job.

ESC standard

Two ground rods on every service, plus the concrete-encased electrode on every new foundation. Water and gas bonds are photographed with the clamp label visible before any finish covers them, and the photos go in the project file.

8.12 Low voltage and communications (Division 27)

Low-voltage cable is pulled with or after the power rough, before insulation. In NJ it's electrical work under the business permit: it's installed by or under a licensed electrical contractor, and the PM confirms with the electrical subcode whether it's carried on the electrical permit or needs its own.

Structured wiring

  • Head end. A structured media enclosure in a ventilated, accessible spot with a dedicated 20 A receptacle. In rowhouses the cellar works if it stays dry; otherwise use a second-floor closet.
  • Data. Cat6 home runs to every TV location, office, Wi-Fi access point and fixed device; two drops at offices and TV walls. Keep each run under 90 m (295 ft) of permanent link. Terminate to T568B at both ends.
  • Coax. RG6 quad-shield to TV locations and the service entry, if the owner wants cable or an antenna.
  • Separation. Keep low-voltage cable at least 12 in from parallel power runs where practical and cross at 90 degrees. Never share a box with power unless it has a listed barrier (NEC 800.133).
  • Conduit. A 1-1/4 in or larger smurf tube or EMT from the head end to the attic or roof, and from each TV wall down to the equipment location, so future cable can be pulled.

Wi-Fi, AV, security and entry

Masonry party walls and metal lath block Wi-Fi, so plan one ceiling-mounted, PoE-powered access point per floor in a rowhouse. TV and speaker locations get pre-wire and blocking, with speaker cable home-run to the AV location. Security pre-wire covers door and window contacts, keypads and camera locations (Cat6 to each camera). For rowhouses, the front door entry is often a multi-story stoop: plan a video intercom or doorbell at the vestibule with Cat6 back to the head end, an electric strike or smart lock with a low-voltage power supply, and a release station on each main floor.

8.13 Knob-and-tube and old wiring in rowhouses

Pre-1940 rowhouses often still have knob-and-tube (K&T) in the walls and floors, sometimes still energized and spliced into later circuits. It has no ground, its insulation is brittle, and NEC 394.12 prohibits it in hollow spaces where insulation surrounds the conductors. Many insurers won't cover a house until it's removed.

Before demolition, the electrician traces every live circuit and kills K&T at the panel.

Common failure

A "dead" K&T run that's still live because it was tapped from a later circuit or fed from the neighbor's side of a shared wall. Test every conductor with a non-contact tester and a meter before cutting. In attached houses, never assume a cable in the party wall belongs to our building.

8.15 Inspection holds

Inspection hold

Rough electrical. All boxes, cable or raceway, recessed housings, grounds spliced and made up, nail plates in place, panel can set with home runs labeled. No insulation, drywall or plaster until passed. Low-voltage rough is usually inspected or photographed at the same time.

Inspection hold

Service. Meter socket, service conductors, main disconnect, grounding electrode conductor, rods or Ufer connection, water and gas bonds. Passing this releases the job to PSE&G for the meter set. The PSE&G queue starts only after release.

Inspection hold

Final electrical. Devices and fixtures installed, covers on, panel directory typed, AFCI and GFCI tested, smoke/CO interconnection working, equipment disconnects labeled. Required before final building sign-off.

The electrical subcode may add an underground or slab inspection for conduit below a slab, and a separate inspection of the concrete-encased electrode before the footing pour (Chapter 03). Ask at permit issuance.

8.16 Common failures and how to avoid them

  • Boxes set for the wrong wall thickness. Tile on backer, plaster, or two layers of drywall leaves boxes buried. Write the finish on the framing at each box during the layout walk; use adjustable boxes in tiled areas.
  • Counter receptacles that collide with backsplashes or tile layout. Get the backsplash height and tile layout (Chapter 10) before setting kitchen boxes; center boxes on a grout line or a full tile where the owner cares.
  • Recessed lights that hit joists, ducts or sprinkler pipe. Lay out cans on the actual framing, not only from the reflected ceiling plan.
  • Drywall screws through cable. Missing nail plates at shallow bores, and cable run on the face of studs at corners. The QC walk before insulation catches this.
  • Lost water-pipe ground after plumbing replaces the service with plastic. Coordinate with Chapter 06 and add electrodes.
  • PSE&G delays. File the service request during preconstruction. Rowhouse vault and sidewalk work can need a street opening permit from Jersey City's Division of Engineering, Traffic and Transportation on top of the PSE&G order.

8.17 QC checklist

Before rough inspection

  • Every box matches the signed layout, at the correct height and set to finished wall thickness.
  • Receptacle spacing checked with a tape against the 6/12 ft and 24/48 in rules.
  • Bores within limits; nail plates at every bore within 1-1/4 in of a face; no notches in joists' middle third.
  • Cable supported and stapled; no staples crushing cable; grommets or bushings at metal studs.
  • Home runs labeled at the panel; low-voltage cables labeled at both ends.
  • Recessed housings are IC and airtight where in the envelope; gaskets present.
  • Fire-blocking at cable penetrations through top plates and floors restored with listed sealant (Chapter 04).
  • Photos of every wall and ceiling before insulation, uploaded to the project file.

Before final inspection

  • All devices tamper-resistant; plates straight and level; no gaps over 1/8 in at box edges.
  • Every GFCI and AFCI tested with its test button; plug-in tester shows correct wiring at every receptacle.
  • Smoke/CO alarms all sound when one is tested.
  • Panel directory typed and specific (room and use, not "lights"); spare breakers off and labeled.
  • Disconnects labeled; heat pump and EV circuits tested under load.
  • Low-voltage terminated, tested with a cable certifier or at minimum a continuity tester, and documented with a port map.

8.18 Handoff to the next trade

At rough, we hand off to insulation (Chapter 05) and drywall or plaster (Chapter 09). The space must have: a passed rough inspection posted on site; every box flush with the planned finish and stuffed or covered so mud and paint stay out; recessed housings capped; no loose cable in the cavities; nail plates installed; low-voltage cable coiled and labeled at each location, not hanging out of the wall; and a photo record of every wall.

At trim, we come back after paint (Chapter 09), tile (Chapter 10) and cabinets, counters and flooring (Chapter 11). We leave with a passed final, all covers and plates on, the panel directory and low-voltage port map in the closeout binder, alarms tested, and no paint or debris in any device.
