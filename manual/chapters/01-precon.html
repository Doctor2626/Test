Division 01 · General Requirements

Preconstruction, Permits & Sequencing

How an ESC job in Jersey City gets from signed contract to a Certificate of Occupancy or Certificate of Approval: the order of trades, the approvals, permits and inspections that gate them, site setup, neighbor protection on attached houses, long-lead buying, and the paperwork that keeps the job honest. Project managers and superintendents run from this chapter; subs and clients use it to see where their piece fits.

1.1 Overview and where this chapter sits

Other chapters each describe one trade. This one covers the order they come in and how the job passes between them. Most schedule losses come from a permit that wasn't filed, a window order placed four weeks late, an inspection that wasn't called, or a trade that showed up to a space the last trade didn't finish.

ESC builds two kinds of houses, and the sequence differs between them:

  • Gut renovation of attached masonry houses. Brownstones and brick rowhouses, 3 to 4 stories, wood joists pocketed into masonry party walls, low-slope roofs, no side yards. The building is already standing, the neighbors share our walls, and we don't know what's behind the plaster until demolition (Chapter 02) opens it up.
  • New single-family houses and additions. We control the hole in the ground, the structure goes up in a predictable order, and the main risks are weather, excavation and procurement.

ESC works under the New Jersey Uniform Construction Code (UCC, N.J.A.C. 5:23), enforced in Jersey City by the Division of Construction Code. Since August 17, 2026 the UCC adopts the IRC 2024 (NJ edition) for one- and two-family dwellings, IBC 2024 for larger buildings, NEC 2023 with NJ amendments, the 2024 National Standard Plumbing Code (NSPC, NJ edition), IECC 2024 for energy, and IMC and IFGC 2024 for mechanical and fuel gas. In NJ the plumbing code is the NSPC, not the IRC plumbing chapters, so the manual never cites IRC plumbing sections. OSHA 29 CFR 1926 governs site safety.

Grace period. A complete permit application, with all prior approvals, submitted by February 17, 2027 may still be reviewed under the previous editions (2021 I-codes, 2020 NEC, 2021 NSPC). Electrical applications filed in that window must state which NEC edition the design follows. The PM confirms with the architect which editions each job is filed under, and the field works to those.

Existing buildings fall under the Rehabilitation Subcode (N.J.A.C. 5:23-6), not the IEBC (section 1.4). The construction official and the subcode officials have the last word on how the code applies to a given job.

1.2 Before anything starts: what must be in hand

Preconstruction is the trade that comes before demolition. Its "prerequisites" are documents and decisions, not built work. We do not mobilize a crew until the following are complete or have a dated plan.

  • Signed written contract with ESC's Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration number, scope, allowances, exclusions and the change order procedure (section 1.8), and the owner's 3-business-day cancellation period run out (section 1.4).
  • Prior approvals the city requires before a permit issues: zoning, Historic Preservation Commission (HPC) approval in a historic district, and a JCMUA release for footing and foundation work.
  • Permit set from the architect, stamped where required, with structural drawings from the engineer of record.
  • Existing-conditions survey: as-built measurements, floor levels, joist sizes and bearing where visible, and probes at suspect areas (joist pockets, rear wall, roof).
  • Hazardous materials survey: asbestos on any building built before about 1980 (removal only by an NJ Department of Labor-licensed contractor), and lead paint assumed on any pre-1978 house (EPA RRP rule, 40 CFR 745). If the client rents a unit in a pre-1978 building, note the NJ lead-safe certificate requirement (Chapter 02).
  • Land survey and zoning check for new builds and additions: setbacks, height, lot coverage, flood zone.
  • Utility information: existing service size, meter locations, PSE&G gas and electric service, JCMUA water and sewer lateral size and material, and whether an upgrade is needed.
  • Owner selections schedule with decision dates tied to the build schedule (fixtures, tile, cabinets, appliances, windows).
  • Insurance certificates from every sub naming ESC and the owner, and for attached houses, the neighbors where an access agreement requires it. Plumbing, electrical and HVAC subs hold the NJ licenses listed in section 1.4.
  • Neighbor access agreements and condition surveys on attached houses (section 1.6).

ESC standard

No mobilization without a preconstruction meeting on site with the PM, superintendent, architect, and the demo, structural and MEP subs. We walk every floor with the drawings, mark what stays, what goes, and what needs protection, and photograph it all before anyone touches a wall.

1.3 The whole-house sequence, contract to CO

Fig. 1.1 shows a typical gut renovation of a 3-story rowhouse with a rear extension, about 44 weeks from contract to CO. A new house of similar size runs longer at the front (excavation, foundation, full frame) and shorter in the middle (no surprises behind the plaster). The bars overlap on purpose. Plumbing, HVAC and electrical rough-in run together floor by floor; tile starts on the first bathroom while drywall finishes the upper floors.

Contract to CO, in order

  1. Contract and budget lock. Written HIC contract with scope, allowances and selections deadlines signed. Long-lead items identified (section 1.7).
  2. Prior approvals, then permit filing. Zoning, HPC and JCMUA approvals come first. The architect files the construction permit application with the Jersey City Division of Construction Code; ESC supplies its HIC registration, the licensed trades' credentials and insurance. The asbestos survey and any abatement permit go in first. Right-of-way permits follow at least 2 weeks before mobilization (section 1.4).
  3. Neighbor work. Access agreements, pre-construction condition surveys, monitoring plan (attached houses).
  4. Release long-lead orders. Windows, steel, HVAC equipment, cabinets and the electrical service upgrade application go out as soon as dimensions are reliable, which for gut renos may mean after selective demo.
  5. Mobilize and set up site. Protection, fence or covered walkway, dumpster, temp power and water, toilet, permit board (section 1.5).
  6. Abatement, then demolition (Chapter 02). Abatement clears before general demo starts.
  7. Foundations and underpinning (Chapter 03). NJ One Call markout before any digging, then footings, underpinning pits, new cellar slab, drainage.
  8. Structure (Chapter 04). Steel, joist repairs and sistering, new framing, stairs openings, shear and hold-downs.
  9. Envelope (Chapter 05). Roof, windows, rear wall, masonry repointing; building made weathertight as early as possible.
  10. MEP rough-in (Chapters 06, 07, 08). Plumbing first (largest, least flexible pipes), then ducts, then electrical.
  11. Rough inspections, insulation, close-in. Subcode rough inspections (building, plumbing, electrical, fire), insulation inspection, then board.
  12. Finishes (Chapters 09, 10, 11). Drywall or plaster, tile, cabinets, stairs, flooring, paint.
  13. Trim-out and finals. Fixtures, devices, equipment start-up, final inspections, punch list, CO, TCO or Certificate of Approval, and closeout binder.

Gut renovation versus new build

The same trades appear in both, but the order of risk is different.

  • Discovery comes early in a gut reno. Schedule float goes right after demolition, because that's when we find rotted joist ends, missing lintels, failed rear walls and old knob-and-tube still live. Budget 1 to 2 weeks for engineer review after demo before structural work is fully released.
  • Measure after demo, not before. Window, stair and cabinet dimensions in an old masonry house are only reliable once the plaster is off. We do selective demo at window openings early so the window order can go out.
  • Weathertight comes late on new builds, early on renos. A rowhouse is usually tight except for the roof and rear wall. A new house isn't dry until the roof, wrap and windows are done, and nothing inside can start until it is.
  • Logistics. Rowhouses have one front door and a stoop. All material goes in and all debris comes out through the same opening or a rear yard, which limits how many trades can work at once. New builds have laydown space and crane access.
  • Top-down finishes. On a rowhouse we often finish top-down so traffic moves away from finished floors.

1.4 Permits and the inspection map

In Jersey City, construction permits come from the Division of Construction Code. Under the UCC the permit is reviewed and inspected by subcode: building, plumbing, electrical and fire, each with its own technical section and inspector. A typical ESC gut needs all four. Several approvals and permits sit outside the construction permit and have their own lead times:

  • Prior approvals. The city lists approvals that must be in hand before a construction permit issues, including zoning, Historic Preservation where it applies, and a JCMUA release for footing and foundation work.
  • Historic districts. In a historic district (Van Vorst Park, Hamilton Park, Harsimus Cove, Paulus Hook and others), the owner needs a Certificate of No Effect (CoNE) or Certificate of Appropriateness (CoA) from the HPC before work starts, whether or not a construction permit is required. Review applies Zoning Ordinance §345-30 (procedures), §345-71 (Historic Design Standards) and the Secretary of the Interior's Standards. Facade, stoop, window, door, cornice, rear addition, rooftop and deck work is typically reviewed. A CoA is valid for 1 year (§345-30.B.2). HPC review can run longer than the construction review, so it goes in first.
  • Asbestos. Abatement permits and clearance fall under the DCA Asbestos Hazard Abatement Subcode (N.J.A.C. 5:23-8), and removal is by an NJ Department of Labor-licensed contractor (Chapter 02).
  • Right-of-way. Street opening, sidewalk occupancy and dumpster permits come from the Department of Infrastructure, Division of Engineering, Traffic and Transportation. Apply at least 2 weeks before the start date. Water and sewer connection changes are coordinated with JCMUA; gas and electric service with PSE&G.
  • NJ One Call (811). Required before any digging, by everyone including homeowners: call at least 3 full business days ahead (weekends and NJ holidays excluded), start digging within 10 business days of the call, and dig while the markout is valid, 45 business days (N.J.A.C. 14:2-3.1). New-house sites, rear-extension footings, yard drains and lateral work all need it.

Jersey City permit path, contract to CO

  1. HIC contract. Written contract with ESC's HIC number signed; the owner's 3-business-day cancellation period runs before we spend the owner's money on the job.
  2. Prior approvals. Zoning approval, HPC CoNE or CoA in a historic district (before any work, permit or not), and the JCMUA release if the job has footing or foundation work.
  3. Construction permit by subcode. The architect files the permit set; building, plumbing, electrical and fire technical sections are reviewed, along with any asbestos abatement permit. Permit placard posted on site once issued.
  4. Right-of-way permits. Street opening, sidewalk occupancy and dumpster permits from the Department of Infrastructure, applied for at least 2 weeks before they're needed.
  5. One Call. 811 at least 3 full business days before the first dig; dig within 10 business days; markout good for 45 business days.
  6. Inspections. Each subcode inspects at its holds (Fig. 1.2). Nothing is covered until the hold is signed.
  7. Final and certificate. Subcode finals pass, then the Certificate of Occupancy, Temporary CO or Certificate of Approval, whichever the construction official issues for the job.

Classifying the work: the Rehabilitation Subcode

Work on an existing NJ building is classified under the Rehabilitation Subcode (N.J.A.C. 5:23-6) as repair, renovation, alteration, reconstruction, change of use or addition (definitions in 5:23-6.3). A rowhouse gut is usually reconstruction (5:23-6.7), which must involve an entire use, primary function space or tenancy. The residential reconstruction provisions (5:23-6.26 and 6.27) were amended in January 2022. New work within a rehab project must meet the subcode's materials and methods requirements (5:23-6.8). The classification sets which upgrades the job must carry, so the PM confirms it with the architect before the price is final, and again if the scope grows during design.

Certificates at the end of the job

The UCC issues three completion documents: a Certificate of Occupancy (new construction, change of use, and reconstruction where required), a Temporary CO, and a Certificate of Approval for completed work that doesn't need a CO. Which one Jersey City issues for a given ESC job type should be confirmed with the construction official at permit filing, so the closeout schedule and the owner's move-in date are built around the right document.

Contracts and licensed trades

  • HIC registration. ESC holds an annual Home Improvement Contractor registration with the Division of Consumer Affairs; the HIC number goes on contracts and vehicles. Any home improvement contract over $500 must be in writing and include the HIC number. The owner may cancel until midnight of the third business day after receiving the contract, with any refund due within 30 days. A 2024 law (P.L. 2024, c.110) moves HICs toward licensing; the office checks the current status before each contract form update.
  • Licensed trades. Plumbing by an NJ licensed master plumber (N.J.S.A. 45:14C); electrical by an NJ licensed electrical contractor holding a business permit (N.J.S.A. 45:5A; NJ has no low-voltage exemption from the business permit); HVAC by an NJ Master HVACR licensee. A homeowner exemption exists for some owner-occupied work; ESC uses licensed trades regardless.

Inspections

Each subcode inspector checks their trade at set points. The construction official sets the list for each permit; a typical ESC gut sees footing and foundation, rough plumbing, gas, electrical and fire, framing after the rough-ins, insulation before cover, and finals. Fig. 1.2 maps where each hold sits in the work.

Inspection hold

Nothing gets covered until the hold is signed: no concrete over unchecked rebar, no backfill over unchecked damproofing, no insulation over uninspected rough-ins, no drywall over uninspected insulation. The superintendent photographs the signed card and the work it covers and files both in the daily log the same day.

Inspection hold

Utility cut-off before demolition. Gas and electric must be confirmed dead and capped, in writing from the utility or the licensed trade, before demo crews open walls. Treat every wire in an old rowhouse as live until a licensed electrician has traced and tested it.

Running inspections

  1. Book ahead. The UCC typically has inspections performed within 3 business days of the request (N.J.A.C. 5:23-2.18); confirm current practice with the Jersey City construction official. Request the inspection when the work is two to three business days from done, not after.
  2. Pre-inspect. The super and the trade foreman walk the work with the permit drawings the day before. Fix what we find; we don't use the inspector as our QC.
  3. Be there. The super or the licensed trade meets the inspector with the approved plans, the permit card, test gauges (plumbing and gas), and the engineer's letters if special inspections apply.
  4. Record it. Pass, partial or fail, with the inspector's notes, goes into the daily log with photos.
  5. Correct and re-call. Failed items are corrected and re-inspected before anything covers them, even if the rest of the floor passed.

1.5 Site setup

Site setup happens in the week before demolition. On rowhouses it's tight and public: the sidewalk is a public right-of-way, anything placed on the street or sidewalk needs a Department of Infrastructure permit applied for at least 2 weeks ahead, and the neighbors walk past every day. NYC-style mandatory sidewalk sheds don't apply in Jersey City; pedestrian protection follows IBC Chapter 33 as adopted and the conditions of the sidewalk occupancy permit. Fig. 1.3 shows a typical front-of-house setup where scaffold work over the sidewalk calls for a covered walkway.

Protection inside and out

  • Retained finishes. On a brownstone we often keep plaster cornices, ceiling medallions, original stair rails, doors, mantels and wood floors. Each one gets a tag, a photo and a protection method in the preconstruction walk: plywood boxes over mantels, foam and plywood over stair treads and rails, ram board plus plywood on floors we're keeping, and nothing taped to finished surfaces with anything but low-tack tape.
  • Zipwall and negative air. Where an occupied floor or a neighbor's open wall is involved, we build dust walls and run a HEPA negative air machine exhausting outside.
  • Street and sidewalk. Fence, barricades or covered walkway per the sidewalk occupancy permit, signage, lighting, swept sidewalk at the end of each day.
  • Roof. Rowhouse roofs are shared low-slope roofs with parapets. Protect the neighbor's roof where we stage or walk, and never pile material on a roof over existing joists without engineer sign-off.

1.6 Attached houses: party walls and neighbors

On a rowhouse the party walls belong to both owners. Our joists sit in pockets in the same brick that holds up the neighbor's joists. Underpinning, joist pocket repair, new steel bearing, roof tie-ins and even heavy demo vibration all affect the house next door. The neighbor relationship is a construction task with a schedule like any other.

Access agreements and NJ party-wall rules

New Jersey has no party-wall statute that gives a builder a right or license to enter the neighbor's property. Where we need the neighbor's property (to place monitors, protect their roof, underpin from their side, or flash a shared parapet), access is by written agreement. Under N.J.A.C. 5:23-2.34, where protecting adjoining property requires entering it and the neighbor refuses, the work may not proceed unless a court grants access. The owner's attorney handles the agreement and any court application; ESC supplies the scope, schedule, protection plan and insurance.

Excavation next to a party wall carries its own duty. Under N.J.S.A. 46:10-1, an owner excavating 8 ft or deeper next to a party wall or adjoining wall must protect that wall at their own expense. Cellar lowering and deep underpinning can reach that depth; the owner's attorney should confirm the statute's current text before the job is priced.

Start the agreement early: a refusal that ends in court can hold the job for months. A workable agreement covers: description of work affecting the wall, protection and monitoring, working hours, access dates and notice periods, insurance naming the neighbor, how damage is documented and repaired, and who pays the neighbor's engineer to review our drawings if requested.

Pre-construction condition survey

  1. Request access in writing. At least 3 to 4 weeks before demo, offer the survey to each adjoining owner (both sides, and the house behind if we're working the rear wall).
  2. Walk and record. A third-party surveyor or the engineer photographs and videos every room sharing a wall, plus the roof, cellar, facade and rear. Existing cracks get measured, marked with pencil end-ticks and dated.
  3. Install monitors. Tell-tale crack gauges on existing cracks and party walls; vibration monitors where required; optical targets on facades.
  4. Take baselines. Read every gauge and target before demo, and record the readings in the survey report.
  5. Distribute. Copies to the neighbor, the owner, the engineer and our insurer. A neighbor who refuses the survey gets a dated letter recording the offer, and we photograph what we can from public space and our side.

Vibration and movement monitoring

The engineer sets the limits for each project. For historic and fragile masonry, a peak particle velocity (PPV) threshold around 0.5 in/s is common practice, with a lower warning level that triggers a review. Monitors log continuously and send alerts by text. Crack gauges are read weekly and after any heavy work; facade targets are read at set stages (after demo, after underpinning, after steel).

Inspection hold

No work that needs the neighbor's property starts until there is a signed access agreement or a court order granting access (N.J.A.C. 5:23-2.34). The PM files the signed agreement or order in the job file before the work is scheduled.

Common failure

Skipping the neighbor survey because "they seem friendly." Six months later a crack in their parlor ceiling gets blamed on us and we have no baseline. The survey protects us and them. Never start demo on an attached house without one, or a dated letter showing it was offered and refused.

1.8 Daily logs, photos, RFIs and change orders

Daily log

The superintendent completes the log every working day before leaving site. It records: weather and temperature (morning and afternoon, which matters for concrete, masonry, paint and tile), crews and headcount by trade, work done by area, deliveries, inspections and results, visitors, safety issues, delays and their cause, and any instruction given by the owner or architect. Write it so a stranger could rebuild the day from it a year later.

Photo documentation

  • Pre-construction: every room, all four walls, floor and ceiling; facade, roof, cellar, neighbors' shared surfaces.
  • Before cover: every wall and ceiling bay with rough-ins, with a tape measure in frame from a fixed reference (floor or corner). These are the photos that find a buried junction box or a blocking location after drywall.
  • Every inspection: the signed card and the work it covers.
  • Every hidden condition that triggers an RFI or change order.

Photos are tagged by project, floor, room and trade in the photo app so anyone can find them later. Phone camera rolls don't count as documentation.

RFIs and change orders

An RFI (request for information) asks the architect or engineer to answer a question the documents don't. A change order changes the contract scope, price or time. Many RFIs end in no change; many hidden conditions end in both.

  1. Number everything. RFIs and change orders get sequential numbers per project, a date, the person asking, drawing references and photos.
  2. Ask one question per RFI and propose an answer. "Joist pocket at grid 3 is rotted 4 in deep; we propose a steel angle ledger bolted to the party wall per attached sketch" gets a fast reply. "What do we do here?" doesn't.
  3. Set a response date. ESC asks for RFI answers within 5 working days, and flags any RFI on the critical path as urgent with the date work stops.
  4. Price changes before the work. The change order states scope, price, and schedule impact in days. The owner signs it before the work starts.
  5. Emergency exception. If a hidden condition is unsafe (failed joist ends, a bowing wall, a gas leak), we stabilize first, notify the owner and engineer the same day, and submit the change order within 2 working days.
  6. Keep the logs current. The RFI log and change order log are reviewed at every weekly owner meeting.

1.9 Trade handoff rules

Every trade leaves the space ready for the next. That means the work is complete, inspected where required, cleaned up, and the next trade can start without fixing anything. If a trade can't finish an area, the foreman marks it in writing for the super, and the super decides whether the next trade starts around it.

ESC standard

Handoffs are walked. The outgoing foreman and the incoming foreman walk the area with the super before the incoming trade starts. Anything the incoming trade accepts becomes theirs to protect. Anything they reject goes on a list with a date.

1.11 Common failures and how to avoid them

  • Ordering windows from the drawings. Old masonry openings vary by an inch or more and are rarely square. Measure after selective demo at each opening.
  • Losing the first two weeks after demo. Engineer site visit scheduled for the day demo finishes, not after. Expose all bearing and pockets before the visit.
  • Covering work without an inspection. Results in tearing out finished drywall. The super holds the cover until the card is signed.
  • Verbal changes. The owner tells a carpenter to move a wall. No change happens without the PM and a written change order or a signed field directive.
  • Overloading a rowhouse floor. Stacked drywall, tile or masonry on old joists. Spread loads over bearing walls, near the party walls, and ask the engineer for anything heavy.
  • Trade stacking in a small house. Five trades on one floor of a 20-ft wide rowhouse means nobody works. Schedule floor by floor and keep no more than two trades on one floor when finishes start.
  • Utility upgrade applied for late. The house is done and has no power. Apply to PSE&G at permit filing.
  • Starting work in a historic district before the HPC approves it. The CoNE or CoA is needed before work starts, even work that needs no construction permit. Check the district map at the bid.
  • Calling One Call the day before. It's 3 full business days, not counting weekends or NJ holidays. Call when the dig date is set, and re-call if digging won't start within 10 business days.

Common failure

Accepting a space with damage. The tile setter starts on a shower pan with a cut membrane, and the leak becomes the tile setter's problem. Walk it, write it down, and refuse the space until it's fixed.

1.12 Preconstruction QC checklist

  • Written contract signed with HIC number, scope, allowances, unit prices and change order procedure; cancellation period passed.
  • Prior approvals in hand: zoning, HPC CoNE or CoA where applicable, JCMUA release for foundation work.
  • Rehabilitation Subcode classification confirmed with the architect.
  • All permits filed and tracked with issue and expiry dates: construction permit by subcode (building, plumbing, electrical, fire), asbestos abatement, street opening, sidewalk occupancy and dumpster.
  • NJ One Call ticket number logged before any digging, with its 10- and 45-business-day dates on the schedule.
  • Hazardous materials survey done; abatement scheduled before demo.
  • Existing-conditions photos complete for every room, roof, cellar and facade.
  • Neighbor surveys done or offer letters on file; monitors installed and baselined.
  • Neighbor access agreements signed, or court access granted, where the work needs the neighbor's property.
  • Long-lead items listed with order triggers, lead times and need dates on the schedule.
  • Utility upgrade application submitted.
  • Selections schedule issued to the owner with decision deadlines.
  • Site setup complete: protection, fence or covered walkway, dumpster, temp power with GFCI, temp water, toilet, extinguishers, lighting, permit board.
  • Sub insurance certificates and NJ trade licenses on file.
  • Daily log, photo app project and RFI and change order logs set up.
  • Schedule issued to all subs with the first four weeks in detail.

1.13 Handoff to demolition

Preconstruction is done when Chapter 02 can start on day one without a phone call. The demolition crew should arrive to find:

  • Permit placard posted, HPC approval on site where applicable, and abatement clearance in hand.
  • Utilities confirmed off and capped, with temp power and water live at the panel and hose bibs.
  • Everything that stays tagged, photographed and protected; everything that goes marked on the drawings and in the field.
  • Dumpster in place under its permit, chute installed, fence or covered walkway up and lit.
  • Neighbor monitors installed, baselined, and the alert contacts set.
  • The engineer's walk-through scheduled for the day demo finishes.
