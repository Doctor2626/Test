Division 01 · General Requirements

Preconstruction, Permits & Sequencing

How an ESC job gets from signed contract to certificate of occupancy: the order of trades, the permits and inspections that gate them, site setup, neighbor protection on attached houses, long-lead buying, and the paperwork that keeps the job honest. Project managers and superintendents run from this chapter; subs and clients use it to see where their piece fits.

1.1 Overview and where this chapter sits

Other chapters each describe one trade. This one covers the order they come in and how the job passes between them. Most schedule losses come from a permit that wasn't filed, a window order placed four weeks late, an inspection that wasn't called, or a trade that showed up to a space the last trade didn't finish.

ESC builds two kinds of houses, and the sequence differs between them:

  • Gut renovation of attached masonry houses. Brownstones and brick rowhouses, 3 to 4 stories, wood joists pocketed into masonry party walls, low-slope roofs, no side yards. The building is already standing, the neighbors share our walls, and we don't know what's behind the plaster until demolition (Chapter 02) opens it up.
  • New single-family houses and additions. We control the hole in the ground, the structure goes up in a predictable order, and the main risks are weather, excavation and procurement.

Codes referenced here are the model codes (IRC 2021, NEC 2023, and OSHA 29 CFR 1926 for site safety). Local amendments and the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) govern on every job. Where a city has its own building code, as many rowhouse cities do, that code replaces the IRC references below.

1.2 Before anything starts: what must be in hand

Preconstruction is the trade that comes before demolition. Its "prerequisites" are documents and decisions, not built work. We do not mobilize a crew until the following are complete or have a dated plan.

  • Signed contract with scope, allowances, exclusions and the change order procedure (section 1.8).
  • Permit set from the architect, stamped where required, with structural drawings from the engineer of record.
  • Existing-conditions survey: as-built measurements, floor levels, joist sizes and bearing where visible, and probes at suspect areas (joist pockets, rear wall, roof).
  • Hazardous materials survey: asbestos on any building built before about 1980, and lead paint assumed on any pre-1978 house (EPA RRP rule, 40 CFR 745).
  • Land survey and zoning check for new builds and additions: setbacks, height, lot coverage, flood zone.
  • Utility information: existing service size, meter locations, gas service, water and sewer lateral size and material, and whether an upgrade is needed.
  • Owner selections schedule with decision dates tied to the build schedule (fixtures, tile, cabinets, appliances, windows).
  • Insurance certificates from every sub naming ESC and the owner, and for attached houses, the neighbors where a party wall agreement requires it.
  • Party wall and access agreements, and neighbor condition surveys, on attached houses (section 1.6).

ESC standard

No mobilization without a preconstruction meeting on site with the PM, superintendent, architect, and the demo, structural and MEP subs. We walk every floor with the drawings, mark what stays, what goes, and what needs protection, and photograph it all before anyone touches a wall.

1.3 The whole-house sequence, contract to CO

Fig. 1.1 shows a typical gut renovation of a 3-story rowhouse with a rear extension, about 44 weeks from contract to CO. A new house of similar size runs longer at the front (excavation, foundation, full frame) and shorter in the middle (no surprises behind the plaster). The bars overlap on purpose. Plumbing, HVAC and electrical rough-in run together floor by floor; tile starts on the first bathroom while drywall finishes the upper floors.

Contract to CO, in order

  1. Contract and budget lock. Scope, allowances and selections deadlines signed. Long-lead items identified (section 1.7).
  2. Permit filing. Architect files; ESC supplies contractor license, insurance and any demolition, sidewalk, dumpster or shed permits. Asbestos survey and any required abatement filing go in first.
  3. Neighbor work. Party wall and access agreements, pre-construction condition surveys, monitoring plan (attached houses).
  4. Release long-lead orders. Windows, steel, HVAC equipment, cabinets and the electrical service upgrade application go out as soon as dimensions are reliable, which for gut renos may mean after selective demo.
  5. Mobilize and set up site. Protection, shed or fence, dumpster, temp power and water, toilet, permit board (section 1.5).
  6. Abatement, then demolition (Chapter 02). Abatement clears before general demo starts.
  7. Foundations and underpinning (Chapter 03). Footings, underpinning pits, new cellar slab, drainage.
  8. Structure (Chapter 04). Steel, joist repairs and sistering, new framing, stairs openings, shear and hold-downs.
  9. Envelope (Chapter 05). Roof, windows, rear wall, masonry repointing; building made weathertight as early as possible.
  10. MEP rough-in (Chapters 06, 07, 08). Plumbing first (largest, least flexible pipes), then ducts, then electrical.
  11. Rough inspections, insulation, close-in. Framing and MEP inspections, insulation inspection, then board.
  12. Finishes (Chapters 09, 10, 11). Drywall or plaster, tile, cabinets, stairs, flooring, paint.
  13. Trim-out and finals. Fixtures, devices, equipment start-up, final inspections, punch list, CO or TCO, and closeout binder.

Gut renovation versus new build

The same trades appear in both, but the order of risk is different.

  • Discovery comes early in a gut reno. Schedule float goes right after demolition, because that's when we find rotted joist ends, missing lintels, failed rear walls and old knob-and-tube still live. Budget 1 to 2 weeks for engineer review after demo before structural work is fully released.
  • Measure after demo, not before. Window, stair and cabinet dimensions in an old masonry house are only reliable once the plaster is off. We do selective demo at window openings early so the window order can go out.
  • Weathertight comes late on new builds, early on renos. A rowhouse is usually tight except for the roof and rear wall. A new house isn't dry until the roof, wrap and windows are done, and nothing inside can start until it is.
  • Logistics. Rowhouses have one front door and a stoop. All material goes in and all debris comes out through the same opening or a rear yard, which limits how many trades can work at once. New builds have laydown space and crane access.
  • Top-down finishes. On a rowhouse we often finish top-down so traffic moves away from finished floors.

1.4 Permits and the inspection map

A typical ESC gut renovation carries several permits at once: the building or alteration permit, plumbing, mechanical, electrical, often separate demolition, sidewalk shed or scaffold, dumpster-in-street, and curb cut or sewer permits where we change utility connections. Some cities also require a separate permit for asbestos abatement or for work in a historic district, which needs landmark approval before the building permit issues. Historic review can run longer than the building review.

The IRC sets out the required inspection points in R109.1: foundation, plumbing/mechanical/gas/electrical rough, frame and masonry, other inspections the AHJ chooses, and final. Most AHJs also inspect insulation before cover under the energy provisions. Fig. 1.2 maps where each hold sits in the work.

Inspection hold

Nothing gets covered until the hold is signed: no concrete over unchecked rebar, no backfill over unchecked damproofing, no insulation over uninspected rough-ins, no drywall over uninspected insulation. The superintendent photographs the signed card and the work it covers and files both in the daily log the same day.

Inspection hold

Utility cut-off before demolition. Gas and electric must be confirmed dead and capped, in writing from the utility or the licensed trade, before demo crews open walls. Treat every wire in an old rowhouse as live until a licensed electrician has traced and tested it.

Running inspections

  1. Book ahead. Most AHJs need 24 to 72 hours notice. Book the inspection when the work is two days from done, not after.
  2. Pre-inspect. The super and the trade foreman walk the work with the permit drawings the day before. Fix what we find; we don't use the inspector as our QC.
  3. Be there. The super or the licensed trade meets the inspector with the approved plans, the permit card, test gauges (plumbing and gas), and the engineer's letters if special inspections apply.
  4. Record it. Pass, partial or fail, with the inspector's notes, goes into the daily log with photos.
  5. Correct and re-call. Failed items are corrected and re-inspected before anything covers them, even if the rest of the floor passed.

1.5 Site setup

Site setup happens in the week before demolition. On rowhouses it's tight and public: the sidewalk is a public right-of-way, the street needs permits for anything parked in it, and the neighbors walk past every day. Fig. 1.3 shows a typical front-of-house setup.

Protection inside and out

  • Retained finishes. On a brownstone we often keep plaster cornices, ceiling medallions, original stair rails, doors, mantels and wood floors. Each one gets a tag, a photo and a protection method in the preconstruction walk: plywood boxes over mantels, foam and plywood over stair treads and rails, ram board plus plywood on floors we're keeping, and nothing taped to finished surfaces with anything but low-tack tape.
  • Zipwall and negative air. Where an occupied floor or a neighbor's open wall is involved, we build dust walls and run a HEPA negative air machine exhausting outside.
  • Street and sidewalk. Shed or fence per permit, signage, lighting, swept sidewalk at the end of each day.
  • Roof. Rowhouse roofs are shared low-slope roofs with parapets. Protect the neighbor's roof where we stage or walk, and never pile material on a roof over existing joists without engineer sign-off.

1.6 Attached houses: party walls and neighbors

On a rowhouse the party walls belong to both owners. Our joists sit in pockets in the same brick that holds up the neighbor's joists. Underpinning, joist pocket repair, new steel bearing, roof tie-ins and even heavy demo vibration all affect the house next door. The neighbor relationship is a construction task with a schedule like any other.

Party wall and access agreements

Rules differ widely by state and city. Some places have a formal party wall process; many US cities rely on the deed, common law and a private agreement. Where we need the neighbor's property (to place monitors, protect their roof, underpin from their side, or flash a shared parapet) and they refuse, some states offer a court process for a license to enter; in New York this is RPAPL 881. The owner's attorney handles the agreement; ESC supplies the scope, schedule, protection plan and insurance.

A workable agreement covers: description of work affecting the wall, protection and monitoring, working hours, access dates and notice periods, insurance naming the neighbor, how damage is documented and repaired, and who pays the neighbor's engineer to review our drawings if requested.

Pre-construction condition survey

  1. Request access in writing. At least 3 to 4 weeks before demo, offer the survey to each adjoining owner (both sides, and the house behind if we're working the rear wall).
  2. Walk and record. A third-party surveyor or the engineer photographs and videos every room sharing a wall, plus the roof, cellar, facade and rear. Existing cracks get measured, marked with pencil end-ticks and dated.
  3. Install monitors. Tell-tale crack gauges on existing cracks and party walls; vibration monitors where required; optical targets on facades.
  4. Take baselines. Read every gauge and target before demo, and record the readings in the survey report.
  5. Distribute. Copies to the neighbor, the owner, the engineer and our insurer. A neighbor who refuses the survey gets a dated letter recording the offer, and we photograph what we can from public space and our side.

Vibration and movement monitoring

The engineer sets the limits for each project. For historic and fragile masonry, a peak particle velocity (PPV) threshold around 0.5 in/s is common practice, with a lower warning level that triggers a review. Monitors log continuously and send alerts by text. Crack gauges are read weekly and after any heavy work; facade targets are read at set stages (after demo, after underpinning, after steel).

Skipping the neighbor survey because "they seem friendly." Six months later a crack in their parlor ceiling gets blamed on us and we have no baseline. The survey protects us and them. Never start demo on an attached house without one, or a dated letter showing it was offered and refused.

1.8 Daily logs, photos, RFIs and change orders

Daily log

The superintendent completes the log every working day before leaving site. It records: weather and temperature (morning and afternoon, which matters for concrete, masonry, paint and tile), crews and headcount by trade, work done by area, deliveries, inspections and results, visitors, safety issues, delays and their cause, and any instruction given by the owner or architect. Write it so a stranger could rebuild the day from it a year later.

Photo documentation

  • Pre-construction: every room, all four walls, floor and ceiling; facade, roof, cellar, neighbors' shared surfaces.
  • Before cover: every wall and ceiling bay with rough-ins, with a tape measure in frame from a fixed reference (floor or corner). These are the photos that find a buried junction box or a blocking location after drywall.
  • Every inspection: the signed card and the work it covers.
  • Every hidden condition that triggers an RFI or change order.

Photos are tagged by project, floor, room and trade in the photo app so anyone can find them later. Phone camera rolls don't count as documentation.

RFIs and change orders

An RFI (request for information) asks the architect or engineer to answer a question the documents don't. A change order changes the contract scope, price or time. Many RFIs end in no change; many hidden conditions end in both.

  1. Number everything. RFIs and change orders get sequential numbers per project, a date, the person asking, drawing references and photos.
  2. Ask one question per RFI and propose an answer. "Joist pocket at grid 3 is rotted 4 in deep; we propose a steel angle ledger bolted to the party wall per attached sketch" gets a fast reply. "What do we do here?" doesn't.
  3. Set a response date. ESC asks for RFI answers within 5 working days, and flags any RFI on the critical path as urgent with the date work stops.
  4. Price changes before the work. The change order states scope, price, and schedule impact in days. The owner signs it before the work starts.
  5. Emergency exception. If a hidden condition is unsafe (failed joist ends, a bowing wall, a gas leak), we stabilize first, notify the owner and engineer the same day, and submit the change order within 2 working days.
  6. Keep the logs current. The RFI log and change order log are reviewed at every weekly owner meeting.

1.11 Common failures and how to avoid them

  • Ordering windows from the drawings. Old masonry openings vary by an inch or more and are rarely square. Measure after selective demo at each opening.
  • Losing the first two weeks after demo. Engineer site visit scheduled for the day demo finishes, not after. Expose all bearing and pockets before the visit.
  • Covering work without an inspection. Results in tearing out finished drywall. The super holds the cover until the card is signed.
  • Verbal changes. The owner tells a carpenter to move a wall. No change happens without the PM and a written change order or a signed field directive.
  • Overloading a rowhouse floor. Stacked drywall, tile or masonry on old joists. Spread loads over bearing walls, near the party walls, and ask the engineer for anything heavy.
  • Trade stacking in a small house. Five trades on one floor of a 20-ft wide rowhouse means nobody works. Schedule floor by floor and keep no more than two trades on one floor when finishes start.
  • Utility upgrade applied for late. The house is done and has no power. Apply at permit filing.

Common failure

Accepting a space with damage. The tile setter starts on a shower pan with a cut membrane, and the leak becomes the tile setter's problem. Walk it, write it down, and refuse the space until it's fixed.

Division 02 · Existing Conditions

Demolition & Abatement

How ESC surveys, abates, strips, shores and clears a building before new work starts, from a single-room tear-out to a full gut of a masonry rowhouse. Field leads, PMs, demo and abatement subs, and owners who want to know what happens in the first weeks of a job all use this chapter.

2.1 Where demolition sits in the job

Demolition is the first physical work on site and the last chance to learn what the building is really made of before design is locked. On a new house it's short: clear the lot, remove old structures, cap utilities. On a rowhouse it decides the rest of the schedule, because the house finally shows us its rot, old fires and bad framing.

Demo is two jobs: hazard removal (surveys, licensed abatement, lead-safe practice) and controlled removal of finishes, systems and the structure shown for removal on the drawings while the building stays up and the neighbors stay clean.

Permits and schedule are Chapter 01; footings and underpinning, Chapter 03; permanent beams and headers that replace what we shore here, Chapter 04. This chapter ends when the shell is clean, documented and shored.

Codes and regulations cited here are the federal and model-code baseline: OSHA 29 CFR 1926 (Subpart T for demolition), EPA 40 CFR Part 745 for lead (the RRP rule), EPA asbestos NESHAP at 40 CFR Part 61 Subpart M, and IRC 2021. State and city rules are often stricter, especially for asbestos and for work on attached buildings. Local amendments and the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) govern in every case.

2.2 Before demolition starts

Nothing gets pulled until these items are closed out. Most come from preconstruction (Chapter 01), but the field lead confirms each one on site.

  • Demo or alteration permit posted; required neighbor notices served.
  • Existing-conditions drawings with bearing walls, joist direction and known prior alterations marked.
  • Hazmat survey results in hand, with an abatement plan for any positives.
  • Engineer engaged and shoring direction in writing for any structural change.
  • Utilities disconnected (Section 2.4); temporary power and lighting installed.
  • Adjoining properties surveyed: dated photos and video of neighbors' party-wall faces and existing cracks, with crack monitors where needed.
  • Salvage list agreed, items tagged, storage arranged.
  • Container or chute permit, delivery booked, hauler weight limits known.

2.3 Hazardous materials surveys

Any house built before 1978 is presumed to have lead paint until testing says otherwise. Any house built before the 1980s may contain asbestos. Old rowhouses usually have both.

Asbestos

A certified asbestos inspector samples suspect materials and sends them for lab analysis, usually polarized light microscopy (PLM). A material is regulated as asbestos-containing when it contains more than 1 percent asbestos. Typical lab turnaround is 24 to 72 hours; plan one to two weeks from site visit to written report. Federal NESHAP rules exempt most houses of four or fewer units, but many states and cities require a survey before issuing a permit.

ESC crews don't disturb asbestos. A licensed abatement contractor removes it, an independent consultant monitors air and clears the area, and any required advance notice (up to 10 working days in some jurisdictions) goes on the schedule.

Lead paint and the EPA RRP rule

The RRP rule applies to paid work that disturbs painted surfaces in pre-1978 housing and child-occupied facilities. It's triggered by disturbing more than 6 sq ft of painted surface per room inside, more than 20 sq ft outside, or any window replacement. The firm must be EPA-certified (or certified by an authorized state), each job needs a certified renovator assigned, and the owner gets the Renovate Right pamphlet before work starts. Paint counts as lead-based at 1.0 mg/cm² or 0.5 percent by weight.

Testing is by XRF (certified inspector or risk assessor) or EPA-recognized test kits (certified renovator). On a full gut of an 1880s house we usually skip testing and treat all paint as lead. Testing pays off when we're keeping a lot of original trim.

RRP prohibits open-flame burning or torching of painted surfaces, heat guns at or above 1,100°F, and power sanding, grinding or planing without a HEPA shroud and vacuum. Interior containment covers the floor at least 6 ft beyond the work area; exterior ground cover extends at least 10 ft. The job closes with cleaning verification using the EPA verification card, and records are kept 3 years. Worker exposure is a separate OSHA rule (1926.62), which drives air monitoring and respirator use for the crew.

Mold and other materials

There's no federal mold standard. We follow EPA residential mold guidance and IICRC S520 practice: small areas (under about 10 sq ft, the EPA guidance threshold) our crew cleans with PPE and HEPA; larger growth goes to a remediation contractor, after the moisture source is fixed. Also watch for abandoned fuel-oil tanks (licensed tank contractor), mercury thermostats, PCB ballasts, and refrigerant in old AC units, which a certified tech must recover.

2.4 Utility disconnects and temporary services

  1. Gas. The utility pulls or locks the meter. Our plumber cuts and caps the house piping at the meter location and caps any line we'll cut through. Abandoned branch lines get traced and capped, never left open.
  2. Electric. On a full gut, the utility de-energizes the service and our electrician sets a temporary service or a temporary panel fed from the meter. On selective demo, the electrician identifies and locks out circuits feeding the work area and confirms dead with a tester at each box before we open walls. Lockout tags carry the electrician's name and date.
  3. Water. Keep one supply live at a temporary hose bib with a backflow preventer. We need water for wet-cutting and dust control. Everything else is shut and drained, and the meter is protected from freezing in winter.
  4. Sewer. Cap the house drain at the house trap or main cleanout once fixtures come out, to stop sewer gas and rodents. Keep one capped connection we can reuse for a temporary toilet if the AHJ allows.
  5. Heat and fuel. Boiler drained and isolated; oil tank handled as above.
  6. Telecom and alarms. Owner disconnects accounts; we coil and tag the lines at the entry point. Monitored alarm or fire panels are taken offline with the monitoring company notified so the fire department isn't called by dust.

Inspection hold

The field lead signs the utility disconnect sheet (gas, electric, water, sewer, fuel) before demo crews start. On selective jobs, every wall and ceiling we open must be checked dead by the electrician first. A non-contact tester alone is not enough; verify at the device with a two-pole tester.

2.7 The strip-out sequence

We demolish top-down and outside-in. Work starts on the top floor so debris always moves down and no crew works below another. Each floor is stripped to framing before the crew moves down. Structural removals come last, after everything non-structural is out and the engineer has seen the exposed framing.

  1. Protect and set up. Protect retained finishes, stairs and sash. Set the chute, container, temporary lighting and one fire extinguisher per floor near the stair.
  2. Salvage. Remove and tag listed items before general demo enters the room (Section 2.9).
  3. Fixtures and loose items. Doors, hardware, cabinets, appliances, plumbing fixtures, radiators. Cast-iron radiators can weigh several hundred pounds; plan the route and rig them down.
  4. Finishes. Trim, then plaster and lath, ceilings before walls in each room so falling ceiling material doesn't land on a crew working a wall. Plaster goes straight to bags or the chute hopper.
  5. Flooring. Down to subfloor or joists as drawn; pull nails from retained joists.
  6. Systems. After the electrician and plumber confirm everything is dead and capped, remove old piping, wiring, ductwork and boiler components. Knob-and-tube comes out entirely; none is reused or left in cavities.
  7. Non-bearing partitions. Only after the engineer or architect has confirmed in writing which walls are non-bearing. In rowhouses, stair walls and the walls at parlor pocket doors often carry floors above.
  8. Conditions walk. Once the floor is open, the PM, engineer and architect walk it. We photograph every bay and mark findings on the drawings (Section 2.11).
  9. Structural removals. With shoring installed and approved, remove bearing walls, joists, stairs or masonry as drawn, top floor first.
  10. Clean. HEPA vacuum all surfaces, framing and masonry; then the handoff walk (Section 2.16).

Common failure

Piling wet plaster on a floor. Three-coat plaster on wood lath weighs roughly 8 to 10 lb per square foot of wall. A room's worth on the floor can far exceed the 30 to 40 psf live loads houses are designed for (IRC Table R301.5), and old joists with notches or rot have less capacity than that. Keep debris moving to the chute every shift and never stage it over a span we've already cut.

2.8 Temporary shoring and needling

Before we cut anything that carries load, shoring takes that load. The engineer of record designs it or approves our layout in writing. OSHA 1926.850(a) requires an engineering survey of the structure by a competent person before demolition starts; on any structural removal, that survey is the engineer's walk plus our shoring plan.

Shoring must carry load through every floor to a sound slab, footing or pad. A shore landing mid-span on the floor below just moves the problem down a level.

Needle beams through masonry

For a new or widened masonry opening, we cut small holes above the future lintel, slide a steel or LVL needle through each, shore both ends, and pack the gap to the brick above with drypack or shims. Only then is the opening cut.

Needling a party wall needs access to the neighbor's side, which means a written agreement and often their engineer. Many party-wall openings aren't permitted at all.

2.9 Salvage of historic elements

Brownstones carry a lot of material that can't be bought new: carved or marble mantels, pier mirrors, plaster ceiling medallions, pocket doors with original hardware, interior shutters, newel posts and balusters, stained or etched glass, and wide-board flooring. What we keep is the owner's and architect's decision; how we remove it is ours.

  1. Inventory. Walk the house with the owner and architect. Tag each item with number, room and wall, and photograph it.
  2. Test for lead. Painted salvage is treated as lead-painted unless tested. Wrap it before it leaves the containment.
  3. Remove by hand. Score paint lines before prying and pull nails through the back with nippers. Marble mantels come apart as shelf and pilasters, two people per piece, supported as each comes free.
  4. Protect. Wrap in moving blankets or foam, label the wrap with the tag number, and stand flat panels vertically. Keep hardware in labeled bags with its door.
  5. Store. Off site or in a secured, dry, heated room on site that's out of the demolition path.
  6. Document for re-use. Measure and photograph plaster cornices and medallions that can't be removed intact so a plaster shop can run new profiles or cast replicas (Chapter 09).

ESC standard

Salvage comes out before general demo enters the room, not during. Plaster cornices and medallions are usually not salvageable once the ceiling comes down; we take a profile and a sample piece instead. Salvage that returns to the job is reinstalled by finish carpenters (Chapter 11), and the tag log goes with it.

2.10 Dust control and containment

In rowhouses, open joist pockets, old pipe chases and shared flues connect straight into the neighbor's rooms, and plaster dust finds every one.

The tools are containment, negative air (HEPA fans exhausting the dirty zone outdoors) and HEPA cleaning (filters rated 99.97 percent at 0.3 microns; a shop vac with a fine-dust bag isn't HEPA).

  1. Seal the building first. Close and tape HVAC registers and returns, shut down forced-air systems, seal fireplace openings and any party-wall holes (old pipe chases, open joist pockets, mail slots between cellars) with poly and tape or with foam board and sealant.
  2. Build the containment. 6-mil poly on spring-loaded pole systems, taped to floor, walls and ceiling. Airlock with two zipper doors (or overlapping flaps) where crews enter. Floor protection outside the zone: rosin paper or a breathable protection board, taped at seams, with a sticky mat at the airlock.
  3. Set negative air. Size the HEPA air unit for at least 4 air changes per hour in the zone. Example: a 20 ft by 25 ft room with a 10 ft ceiling is 5,000 cu ft; 4 ACH is 20,000 cu ft per hour, or about 335 CFM. Duct the exhaust outdoors through a sealed panel in a window, away from neighbors' windows and air intakes.
  4. Verify pressure. Target -0.02 in. w.c. (about -5 Pa) in the zone relative to the clean side, read on a manometer at the start of each shift. The poly should bow gently inward.
  5. Work wet, clean daily. Mist plaster before it comes down, never dry sweep, HEPA vacuum each day, and wipe bags before they leave the zone.
  6. Tear down last. After final HEPA cleaning and wipe-down, mist and fold the poly inward, bag it, and do a final HEPA pass on the area where it stood.

ESC standard

Poly containment with HEPA negative air for any selective demo in an occupied house or next to rooms we're keeping. On full guts, we seal party-wall openings and flues, run HEPA air scrubbers on each floor during plaster removal, and rely on source control and daily HEPA cleaning, because full negative pressure is hard to hold in an open shell.

2.11 Discovery protocol for hidden conditions

Every old house has surprises. The protocol makes the crew stop the same way every time and gets the right people looking quickly, with documentation for the owner conversation.

What we look for

  • Rotted joist ends at masonry, common under old leaders and at rear walls. Probe with an awl; more than about 1/4 in. of soft wood is a flag. Fixes are designed by the engineer (Chapter 04).
  • Earlier sistering that's short, unbolted or doesn't reach bearing; cut and notched joists from old plumbing runs. Map every one.
  • Undersized or missing headers at openings cut in past renovations. Shore at once if the wall is bearing.
  • Knob-and-tube. Live until the electrician proves it dead; old K&T is sometimes spliced into newer circuits.
  • Fire damage and masonry defects: charred framing, cracked lintels, bulging walls, loose brick at pockets. Masonry repair is Chapter 05; underpinning is Chapter 03.

ESC standard

Any field lead or worker can stop work on a discovered condition. The PM hears about it the same day, with photos in the job file. Structural or life-safety findings get temporary shoring or isolation right away, before anyone discusses cost. Nothing gets covered or removed until the PM releases it in writing.

Inspection hold

Post-strip conditions walk: once the shell is open, the PM, engineer and architect walk every floor. Structural design isn't final, and Chapter 03 and 04 work doesn't start, until findings are logged, answered or listed as open RFIs, and any AHJ demolition or structural inspection has passed.

2.12 Debris handling, containers and recycling

2.14 Common failures and how to avoid them

Common failure

Removing a "partition" that was bearing. Avoid it by getting written confirmation for every wall, checking joist direction from below and above, and assuming stair walls and walls in line with the floor above are bearing until proven otherwise.

Common failure

Dust in the neighbor's house. It usually travels through a shared flue, an open joist pocket or a cellar opening. Seal those before the first ceiling comes down and check the neighbor side if they'll let us.

Common failure

Cutting into the party wall past our half. In rowhouses, the neighbor's joists often pocket into the same wall from the other side, sometimes only 4 in. from ours. No coring, chasing or pocket enlargement in a party wall without engineer direction and a check of what's on the other side.

Common failure

Leaving the building open to weather. When roof, skylight or rear-wall work opens the shell, cover it at the end of every day with secured tarps or temporary framing and membrane. Wet plaster debris and soaked joists set back the schedule by weeks.

Divisions 03 & 31 · Concrete and Earthwork

Excavation, Footings & Foundations

How ESC lays out, digs, forms, reinforces, pours and waterproofs the part of the house that sits in the ground, from a new spread footing on open ground to underpinning a 120-year-old party wall. Field leads, PMs, excavation and concrete subs, and owners who want to understand the most expensive work they'll never see all use this chapter.

3.1 Where foundations sit in the job

Foundation work starts once the site is cleared and shored (Chapter 02) and ends when framing can set a sill plate on a straight, level, dry wall (Chapter 04). It is the least forgiving trade in the house. A footing poured an inch out of position or on soft soil can't be fixed with shims later.

On a new house or addition: layout, excavation, footing inspection and pour, walls, waterproofing and drainage, under-slab rough-ins, slab, backfill. On a rowhouse gut, the foundation exists and is usually shared: we underpin or bench the party walls to lower the cellar, add pads for new steel posts, and pour a new slab with the drainage and vapor barrier the house never had.

Code references are to the IRC 2021 and ACI 318, 117 and 306 unless noted. Local amendments and the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) govern, and the engineer's drawings govern over this chapter. Underpinning is always engineered work.

3.2 Before we dig: prerequisites

Nothing gets excavated until the following is in hand. Most of it comes out of preconstruction (Chapter 01) and demolition (Chapter 02).

  • Permit issued, with stamped foundation drawings and, for underpinning, the engineer's sequence and special inspection agreement.
  • Boundary and topographic survey with a benchmark elevation; for additions, the existing finished floor tied to that benchmark.
  • Geotechnical report or, for small additions where the AHJ allows it, the presumptive soil values from IRC Table R401.4.1 confirmed by the engineer.
  • Utility locate ticket (811) called in and marked; private lines (sewer lateral, old oil tanks, yard drains) located by hand.
  • For attached houses: neighbor notices, access or party-wall agreements, a dated photo condition survey of both neighbors, and crack monitors or survey targets where the engineer calls for them.
  • Demo complete to the depth needed; existing slab broken out and hauled; temporary shoring from Chapter 02 inspected and signed off.
  • Sewer invert, under-slab drain layout and sump location confirmed with the plumber (Chapter 06); mix designs, pump and rebar shop drawings approved.

3.3 Layout

Layout turns the drawings into string lines on the ground. We set batter boards outside the dig so the lines can be pulled again after the excavator has torn out every stake inside the hole.

  1. Establish control. Find the surveyor's pins and benchmark. Shoot the benchmark with a builder's level or rotary laser and mark a reference elevation (usually top of foundation wall or top of slab) on a fixed object off site of the dig.
  2. Rough-stake the corners. Measure from the property line or existing building per the site plan. Set hubs with tacks at each outside corner of the foundation wall.
  3. Square it. Check one corner with a 3-4-5 triangle (or 6-8-10, 9-12-15 for longer legs), then check both diagonals. Adjust until diagonals match within 1/4 in on a typical house footprint.
  4. Set batter boards. At each corner, drive three 2x4 stakes 4 to 8 ft outside the excavation line and screw two 2x ledgers in an L. Set every ledger top to the same elevation with the laser, usually top of wall.
  5. Transfer the lines. Pull mason's line over the ledgers so the lines cross directly over the hubs. Mark each ledger with a saw kerf or a screw so the line can be removed and reset exactly.
  6. Mark the dig. Drop a plumb bob or laser at each string intersection, then paint the excavation line on the ground: footing outside edge plus 2 ft of working room, more where we'll form, waterproof or set drainage.
  7. Recheck after the dig. Re-pull lines, re-check diagonals, and mark footing edges in the hole before forming.

On a rowhouse, layout is mostly elevations: footing bottoms shot at test pits, then pin bays painted and tagged along each wall.

  1. Dig to grade with a smooth bucket. The last 2 to 4 in come off with a toothless cleanout bucket or by hand so the bearing soil isn't churned up.
  2. Probe it. A 1/2 in probe rod or hand penetrometer every few feet. Mark soft spots, wet pockets, old foundations and debris.
  3. Over-excavate soft spots. Dig to firm soil, then backfill to footing bottom with lean concrete or with 3/4 in crushed stone compacted in 6 in lifts, as the engineer directs. Never with loose native soil.
  4. Step the footing where grade changes (R403.1.5): tops level, bottom slope no steeper than 1 in 10.

Common failure

Pouring on mud or frozen ground, which settles when it thaws or dries. If the bottom is a puddle the morning of the pour, cancel the truck. A one-day slip costs far less than a cracked foundation.

Excavation safety follows OSHA 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P: cuts 5 ft or deeper get sloping, benching, a trench box or shoring set by a competent person.

3.7 Underpinning existing rowhouse walls

Lowering a cellar in an attached house means the new floor ends up below the bottom of the existing party-wall footings, which are often a few courses of corbelled brick or rubble stone sitting 12 to 24 in below the old floor. Underpinning extends those walls down in short, alternating sections so the wall is never unsupported over more than a small length.

Underpinning is designed by a licensed engineer, almost always requires special inspection, and on an occupied neighbor's wall needs the agreements and monitoring listed in 3.2. The engineer sets the pin width, depth, sequence and the waiting time between sequences. The steps below are how ESC runs the work inside that design.

  1. Test pits and baseline. Dig two or three pits per wall to find footing bottom, width and soil for the engineer. Read crack gauges and survey targets before the first bay opens.
  2. Mark bays. Paint and tag bays along the wall, typically 3 to 4 ft wide, numbered by sequence. No two adjacent bays share a sequence number.
  3. Excavate sequence 1. Dig the approach pit on our side, then hand-dig under the existing footing to the new bearing elevation. Shore the pit faces as needed. Clean the underside of the old footing of loose soil and mortar.
  4. Inspect the bearing. The special inspector checks the bottom soil and dimensions before any concrete.
  5. Form and pour the pin. Set the form on our side, add rebar dowels into adjacent bays if the engineer calls for them, and pour the pin to within 2 to 3 in of the underside of the old footing.
  6. Dry-pack. After the pin has cured the specified time (often 24 to 48 hours), ram a stiff, low-water sand-cement mortar into the gap with a hardwood packing stick and hammer, in layers, until it's packed tight across the full width. Some engineers allow non-shrink grout in place of dry-pack. Follow the drawings.
  7. Next sequence. Open the next numbered bays only after the previous ones are dry-packed and have reached the strength or age the engineer specifies. Read the monitors daily and stop work if movement exceeds the engineer's trigger values.

Bench footings and basement lowering

Where the cellar only needs to drop a modest amount, or the neighbor won't agree to underpinning, a bench can lower the middle of the floor while leaving the soil wedge under the existing footing undisturbed. The new floor steps down at a line set back from the footing toe, typically no closer than a 1:1 (45 degree) line drawn down from the bottom edge of the existing footing on the excavation side. The engineer sets the actual line by soil type. A short retaining curb or concrete bench holds the step and gives a useful ledge for mechanical runs or a built-in seat.

The cost is floor area: at 1:1, each foot of drop below the footing costs about a foot of width along each wall. On a 16 to 20 ft wide rowhouse, deep lowering usually means underpinning.

Placing

  1. Check the ticket. Mix, time batched and water held back. ASTM C94 calls for discharge within 90 minutes or 300 drum revolutions unless the purchaser waives it. Reject old loads.
  2. Water control. Add water only up to the amount withheld on the ticket; use plasticizer for more flow. Extra water costs strength and adds shrinkage cracks.
  3. Test where required. When special inspection or the engineer requires it, the testing agency takes slump, air, temperature and cylinders (ASTM C31) at the truck.
  4. Place and consolidate. Pump or chute close to final position and vibrate each lift.
  5. Finish slabs in order: screed, bull float, let bleed water leave, then edge, joint and trowel.

Curing and weather

  • Curing. Keep concrete moist or covered for at least 7 days: wet curing blankets, poly sheeting, or a curing compound compatible with any later flooring adhesive (check Chapter 11 and Chapter 10 before using one on a slab that gets tile or wood).
  • Cold weather (ACI 306, air at or forecast below 40°F during the protection period). Never place on frozen ground or against frozen forms. Use heated water and accelerator from the plant (non-chloride where there's steel), cover with insulated blankets, and keep concrete at 55°F or above for the protection period in sections under 12 in thick (most footings and walls), 50°F for thicker sections. Concrete must not freeze before it reaches about 500 psi.
  • Hot weather (ACI 305). Pour early, wet the subgrade, use retarder, cure immediately.
  • Loading. Wall forms stripped at 24 to 48 hours; no backfill until the wall is braced or the floor framing and slab are in (R404.1.7); full design strength at 28 days.

Framing & Structure

How ESC frames floors, walls and roofs, sets beams and posts, and repairs the joists and pockets of old masonry rowhouses, with the different framing systems we use and when each one makes sense. Lead carpenters, framing subs, steel erectors, PMs and owners who want to understand the bones of their house all use this chapter.

4.1 Where framing sits in the job

Framing starts once the foundation can take load (Chapter 03) or, in a gut renovation, once demolition has left a clean, shored shell (Chapter 02). It ends when the structure is plumb, level, sheathed, strapped, fireblocked and has passed the framing inspection. Windows need square openings, tile needs stiff floors, cabinets need flat walls and stairs need correct floor-to-floor heights, so everything after depends on it.

On a new house, framing is one fast campaign: sills, deck, walls, next floor, roof, sheathing. On a rowhouse it runs floor by floor: set steel, rebuild pockets, sister and level joists, frame openings, then partitions, in the order the engineer's shoring plan allows.

Code references are to the IRC 2021 as the model baseline. Local amendments and the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) govern, and where the engineer's drawings ask for more than code, the drawings win. On rowhouses permitted under a local existing-building code, the engineer's repair details govern.

4.2 Before framing starts

The PM confirms all of these before the first lumber delivery.

  • Current stamped structural drawings on site, with beam, header, post and connector schedules.
  • Shop drawings for steel, floor trusses, roof trusses or SIP panels are approved, with delivery dates confirmed (lead times in 4.5 and 4.6).
  • New construction: foundation walls cured and backfilled or braced, anchor bolts and hold-down anchors set per plan and surveyed, foundation inspection passed (Chapter 03).
  • Rowhouse: demolition and abatement complete, shoring signed off by the engineer, party walls exposed and documented (Chapter 02).
  • Existing joists probed and mapped floor by floor: rot, notches, fire damage, sag.
  • Laser benchmark set on every floor, with a reference elevation tied to the finished floor height on the drawings.

  • Window rough openings confirmed against the window order (Chapter 05).

4.3 Framing systems compared

Most of what ESC builds is either platform framing on new work or a repair of whatever an old house was built with. The other systems show up when the design, energy goals or schedule ask for them.

4.4 Rowhouse structure: what we find and how we fix it

A typical brownstone or brick rowhouse has 12 in. to 16 in. brick party walls carrying the floors. Joists span party wall to party wall, usually 16 to 22 ft, and the originals are often rough-sawn, full-dimension 3x8 to 3x12 at 16 in. to 24 in. o.c., set in pockets in the brick. There's often a girder on piers in the cellar and sometimes a bearing partition near the stair. After a century these floors sag 1 in. to 2 in. at midspan and have been notched by every plumber who worked in the house. Our job is to make them level, stiff and sound without taking the house apart.

Options for existing joists

We can sister, level over, replace or supplement the old joists. A sister only carries end shear if it bears on the same supports as the original, which in a rowhouse means a new pocket or a hanger on a bolted ledger. We crown and set sisters to a laser line, not to the sagging original, so the sister does the leveling.

Joist pockets in masonry party walls

The IRC requires at least 3 in. of bearing on masonry (R502.6) and decay protection where wood enters masonry: treated or decay-resistant wood, or a 1/2 in. air space at top, sides and end (R317.1). Old pockets are often packed with damp mortar, and the joist end has rotted.

The fire cut bevels the top of the joist end so a burning joist rotates out of the pocket instead of prying the wall outward. We keep it on new joists set in masonry pockets unless the engineer details otherwise. Shims are steel or slate, never wood. Where the neighbor's joists pocket into the same wall, we check the solid brick left between pockets and fill old voids with mortar to keep the fire separation.

4.7 Wall framing

Walls are built flat on the deck and stood up, or in a rowhouse often built in place between floors. Layout drives everything.

  • Studs: 16 in. o.c. from one end so panel edges land on framing. A 92-5/8 in. precut stud with three plates gives a 97-1/8 in. wall for an 8 ft ceiling.
  • Openings: full-height king studs, jacks under each header end, header per IRC Table R602.7(1) or the engineer, flat rough sill, cripples on layout.
  • Double top plate: splices offset at least 24 in. (IRC R602.3.2), with the top plate lapped at corners and bearing-wall intersections to tie walls together.
  • Corners and partitions: three-stud corners, or two-stud corners with drywall clips (OVE); ladder or flat-stud backing at partitions.
  • Blocking: backing for cabinets, grab bars, valves, TV mounts, rails and heavy fixtures goes in now, from the backing plan.

Drilling and notching limits

The lead carpenter checks these limits after every rough-in. Studs in bearing walls may be bored up to 40 percent of the stud width (60 percent if the stud is doubled, with no more than two successive doubled studs bored), with the hole edge at least 5/8 in. from the face (IRC R602.6). Notches in bearing studs are limited to 25 percent of width. For sawn joists, holes are limited to one-third of the depth and kept 2 in. from the top and bottom edges; notches are limited to one-sixth of the depth and not allowed in the middle third of the span (IRC R502.8.1). I-joists, LVLs and trusses follow the manufacturer's hole chart only. If a member is cut past the limit, it gets an engineered repair or replacement, never a field guess.

4.8 Roof framing

A ridge board is only a nailing surface: rafters push the walls outward and ceiling joists or rafter ties resist that thrust (IRC R802.3, R802.5). Remove the ties without an engineered fix and the walls spread. A structural ridge beam carries half the rafter load on posts down to footings, so no ties are needed; it's the method for cathedral ceilings. Trusses span wall to wall, and the permanent bracing on the truss drawings is part of the install. Low-slope rowhouse roofs get slope from tapered sleepers, from joists stepped down toward the rear, or from tapered insulation on a level deck (Chapter 05). Most low-slope membranes need at least 1/4 in. per foot (IRC R905).

4.9 Sheathing, fasteners, hold-downs and fireblocking

Wall sheathing is 7/16 in. OSB or 15/32 in. plywood, edges on framing or blocking, gapped 1/8 in. Low-slope and walkable roofs get 5/8 in. or 3/4 in. sheathing, with panel clips where the span calls for them.

Lateral loads follow a chain too. Sill anchor bolts are 1/2 in. at 6 ft o.c. max., within 12 in. of plate ends, 7 in. embed (IRC R403.1.6), unless the engineer asks for more. Shear wall ends get hold-downs on cast-in anchors or threaded rod, straps tie wall to wall across the rim, and rafters get the ties the design calls for. Every connector gets every specified nail. In rowhouses the floors are diaphragms tying the party walls together, so any straps or anchors the engineer calls for into the masonry go in before the subfloor.

Fireblocking (IRC R302.11) closes concealed spaces at floor and ceiling lines, every 10 ft horizontally, at soffits, at stair stringers and around penetrations, using 2x lumber, 23/32 in. panel, 1/2 in. gypsum or secured mineral wool, with listed sealant at penetrations. Draftstopping (R302.12) limits concealed floor-ceiling spaces, such as open-web trusses, to 1,000 sq ft where there's usable space above and below. Balloon-framed walls and gaps at party walls are where we find the open paths.

4.10 The framing sequence, step by step

New construction

  1. Check the foundation. Shoot top of wall for level and diagonals for square; plan shims where it's more than 1/4 in. off.
  2. Set sills. Sill gasket, treated plate, washers and nuts tight; hold-down anchors protected.
  3. Frame the first deck. Beams, posts, rim and joists on layout, blocking at bearing and point loads, hangers fully nailed, subfloor glued.
  4. Snap and plate walls. Chalk lines from the plans; plates marked for studs, openings, partitions and blocking.
  5. Build and stand walls. Exterior walls first, sheathed flat where practical. Plumb and line them with braces before the next floor goes on.
  6. Repeat per floor. Keep posts over posts and bearing walls over bearing walls.
  7. Roof. Ridge beam or trusses with permanent bracing, sheathing, and underlayment the same day (Chapter 05).
  8. Connectors and straps. Hold-downs, straps, hurricane ties and remaining hangers. Walk every connector against the schedule.
  9. Interior partitions and backing. Frame partitions, soffits and chases; install all blocking from the backing plan.
  10. Punch for inspection. Walk with the plans and fix nailing, straps and plumb before the PM calls the inspection.

Rowhouse gut renovation

  1. Verify shoring. Confirm temporary shoring matches the engineer's shoring plan before any bearing member is removed or cut.
  2. Survey each floor. Laser grid at about 4 ft; record highs and lows to set the target plane.
  3. Set steel and new beams. Cut pockets, grout bearing plates, set steel, bolt nailers, in the engineer's sequence.
  4. Rebuild pockets. Cut out rot; new ends get fire cuts, steel or slate shims and decay protection.
  5. Sister and level. String or laser the target plane, crown sisters up, jack each sister to the line, fasten per the engineer.
  6. Frame openings and stairs. Headers, trimmers and hangers for new stairs, shafts and skylights.
  7. Roof. Sister or replace joists, build slope or a level deck, sheath, roofer in immediately (Chapter 05).
  8. Fireblock old paths. Party wall gaps, chimney chases, balloon-framed bays.
  9. Partitions, backing, punch, inspect. Same as new construction.

ESC standard

No subfloor goes down on a rowhouse floor until the PM has a laser survey showing the framing within tolerance. Planing a joist is cheap; fixing a floor through hardwood is not.

4.13 Common failures and how to avoid them

Common failure

Broken load path. A post lands on a slab with no footing, or blocking is missed under an upper-floor post. Trace every point load on the plans before framing and walk it floor by floor before subfloor.

Common failure

Sisters set to the old sag. The sister is clamped to the old joist and follows its curve, so the floor is stronger but still out of level. Set sisters to a laser line.

Common failure

Over-cut members. A joist notched under a toilet, an I-joist flange cut for a duct. Mark no-cut zones on engineered members and walk the house after every rough-in.

Common failure

Wet lumber closed in. Framing closed in at 22 percent moisture shrinks, pops nails, cracks tile and opens trim joints. Dry in fast and meter before insulation.

Common failure

Missing backing or connector nails. Backing is found missing when the cabinet installer arrives; hangers have short nails or empty holes. Keep a backing plan from the shop drawings and fixture specs, keep the right connector nails on site in labeled boxes, and check both on the pre-inspection walk.

Common failure

Open fire paths in old houses. Balloon-framed bays, party wall gaps and old chimney chases left open. Give fireblocking its own walk-through.

4.14 Framing QC checklist

  • Beams, headers, posts and connectors match the schedule and are photographed with stamps visible.
  • Every point load traced to a footing, blocked solid at every floor.
  • Joist bearing meets the minimums; pockets rebuilt with steel or slate shims, fire cuts and decay protection.
  • Floors surveyed with a laser grid and within tolerance; survey saved to the project file.
  • Walls plumb and straight within tolerance; tile and cabinet walls checked to the tighter number.
  • Rough openings match the window and door schedule, square within 1/8 in. on diagonals.
  • Sheathing nailing per schedule; no overdriven nails through the panel skin; shear wall edge nailing complete.
  • Hold-downs, straps, hurricane ties and hangers installed with the correct nails in every hole.
  • Holes and notches within code or manufacturer limits; any over-cut member repaired per engineer.
  • Fireblocking and draftstopping complete, including penetrations sealed with listed products.
  • Backing installed per plan and photographed with a tape in frame.
  • Framing moisture content at or below 15 percent before insulation.

4.15 Handoff to the next trades

Framing hands the house to the envelope crew (Chapter 05) and to the mechanical trades (Chapters 06, 07 and 08), who often work in parallel. When framing leaves a floor:

  • The structure is complete and inspected, or ready for the framing inspection as soon as rough-ins are in.
  • Roof is sheathed and dried in; window and door openings are sized, square and ready for flashing.
  • Mechanical chases, soffits and duct routes are framed where coordinated, with no-cut zones marked on engineered members.
  • Backing is in for every fixture, cabinet and accessory on the backing plan.
  • The laser benchmark and finished floor elevations are marked on each floor for the trades that follow.
  • The floor is broom clean and stacked materials are off the deck.
  • The PM has the photo set of every beam, connection, pocket, hold-down and backing location.

After rough-ins, the lead carpenter fixes anything the trades cut, closes out fireblocking and the PM calls the framing inspection. Insulation and drywall (Chapter 09) start only after it passes.

Building Envelope: Roofing, Windows, Insulation & Facade

How ESC keeps water out, holds heat in and stops air leaks: roofs, water-resistive barriers, windows and doors, insulation, air sealing and brownstone facade work. Lead carpenters, roofing and masonry subs, insulation contractors and PMs use this chapter to pick an assembly and build it in the right order.

5.1 Where the envelope sits in the job

Envelope work happens in two campaigns. Dry-in (roof, WRB, windows, exterior doors) follows framing and sheathing (Chapter 04) so the building stops taking water before the rough trades arrive. Insulation and air sealing follow plumbing, HVAC and electrical rough-in and their inspections (Chapters 06-08) and come right before drywall or plaster (Chapter 09). Cladding and facade masonry run in parallel with interior work once openings are flashed.

A rowhouse is different from a new house. The roof is usually a low-slope membrane behind brick parapets, the front and rear walls are solid brick or brownstone-faced brick, the party walls are shared with occupied neighbors, windows go into masonry openings, and the facade may sit in a landmarks district with its own approvals. A temporary roof often goes on during demolition (Chapter 02) and the permanent roof only after new steel and top-floor framing.

Code references are to the IRC 2021 as the model baseline. Local amendments, the local energy code and the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) govern, and in landmarks districts the preservation commission governs the facade. Manufacturer instructions are part of the code requirement for most of these products, so keep them on site.

5.2 Before envelope work starts

Before dry-in

  • Sheathing nailing and structural observation signed off where the AHJ or engineer requires them before the WRB covers the sheathing (Chapter 04). The full framing inspection comes later, after rough-ins.
  • Roof sheathing nailed off with edges supported; rowhouse roof joists leveled or tapered to the roof plan and parapets repaired.
  • Rough openings checked against approved shop drawings: size, diagonals within 1/8 in., sill level.
  • Windows and doors ordered with confirmed ship dates (custom wood units often 12-20 weeks; stock vinyl or fiberglass 6-12 weeks; see Fig. 1.5).
  • Roofing, flashing metals, WRB and tapes on site, all from compatible product families.
  • Rowhouse: neighbor roofs and party-wall caps photographed; access agreement signed for any work on an adjoining roof; landmarks approvals in hand.

Before insulation

  • Rough-in for Chapters 06-08 complete and inspected. Nothing gets insulated before the rough inspections.
  • Roof and windows watertight; framing at or below 15 percent moisture on a pin meter (ESC target, Chapter 04; 19 percent is the absolute ceiling); masonry dry, not darkened.
  • Every envelope penetration installed and flashed outside, ready for air sealing inside.
  • Blocking for cabinets, grab bars, TV mounts and rails installed per the backing plan (Chapter 04).

Flashing at walls, chimneys and parapets

Most roof leaks are flashing leaks. Each piece laps over the one below it, metal is never locked down at both ends, and sealant is never the water stop.

  • Step flashing: one L-piece per course, 4 in. min on the roof and up the wall (R905.2.8.3), lapping the piece below by at least 2 in. The wall WRB laps over the vertical legs.
  • Counterflashing at masonry: let into a mortar joint about 1 in. and lapping 4 in. over the base flashing. Through-wall flashing is better when the masonry is being rebuilt anyway.
  • Cricket on the uphill side of any chimney or penetration wider than 30 in. (R903.2.2).
  • Kickout wherever a roof edge dies into a wall that continues past it (R903.2.1).

  2. Parapets first. Repoint the inside face and top, rebuild loose top courses, set treated nailers anchored to the masonry.
  3. Drains and scuppers. Set drain bodies, scuppers and the overflow (typically 2 in. above the primary) before insulation.
  4. Insulation and cover board. Vapor retarder on plank decks or over humid rooms, then tapered polyiso with staggered joints and crickets between drains, then 1/2 in. cover board fastened per the uplift design.
  5. Membrane. Adhere, roll, and weld or tape seams. Probe every TPO weld at the end of each day.
  6. Base flashing and coping. Up the parapet 8 in. min above the finished roof and over the top. Coping on a continuous cleat, pitched to the roof, legs lapping 3-4 in. down each face with a drip.

Insulating solid masonry walls from inside

Rowhouse front and rear walls are two or three wythes of soft brick, often brownstone-faced, with no cavity. They absorb rain and dry in both directions. Insulating the inside cuts off the heat that used to dry them, so the brick runs colder and wetter and freeze-thaw damage and joist-end rot become more likely. Control the rain first, then match the insulation to the brick.

  1. Fix rain exposure. Sound coping, working gutters and leaders, pitched sills, flashed lintels, repointed joints (5.9).
  2. Assess risk. North-facing and exposed walls, soft or underfired brick and walls already spalling are high risk. Ask the architect for a hygrothermal review or brick testing before insulating them. Party walls between heated houses don't see freeze-thaw and only need air sealing.
  3. Keep it modest. Around 2-3 in. of closed-cell foam is the usual choice on exposed solid masonry, rather than thick insulation that drives the brick colder.
  4. Protect joist ends. Leave an air space around the joist end in the pocket and seal the foam to the joist at the masonry face. Where joists are being replaced, hangers on a ledger take the wood out of the wall (Chapter 04).
  5. Build the interior wall. Studs set off the brick, drywall over all foam, no polyethylene.

5.8 Air sealing and blower-door testing

On new work the air barrier is a continuous line around the house, usually taped sheathing, the roof deck or top ceiling and the foundation, with every transition detailed. On rowhouses the big leaks are wall-to-floor joints at the front and rear, party walls (gaps connect to the neighbor's joist bays), chimneys, the parapet line and old cornice cavities.

  1. Mark the air barrier on the drawings and walk it with the lead before insulation.
  2. Big holes first. Chases, flue surrounds (fire-rated sealant and metal), soffits, tub and stair openings.
  3. Penetrations and joints. Wires, pipes, ducts, sill plates, rim joists, top plates and window gaps.
  4. Pre-drywall blower door. Depressurize to 50 Pa, find leaks with smoke or infrared, and fix them while exposed.
  5. Final test. New dwellings must be tested under IRC Chapter 11 (N1102.4.1.2); the 2021 limit in climate zones 3-8 is 3.0 ACH50 unless the local energy code differs.

ESC standard

Every new house and addition gets a pre-drywall blower door, with a target of 2.0 ACH50 or better. On rowhouse gut renovations we test before and after, aim for 5.0 ACH50 or better, and report both numbers to the client, knowing that leakage through party walls counts against us.

  1. Match and mock up. Sample the mortar, match sand and color, and build an approval panel.
  2. Rake out. To 2 to 2-1/2 times the joint width (1/2 in. min), square at the back, without widening joints or chipping brick edges.
  3. Fill. Pre-wet, then pack in lifts about 1/4 in. thick, each thumbprint hard before the next. Tool to the original profile.
  4. Cure. Mist and cover for at least 3 days. No work below 40°F or when frost is forecast during the cure.

Brownstone patching and refacing

Brownstone is a soft sandstone that was often face-bedded, so it delaminates in sheets.

  • Patching: cut back to sound stone, undercut the edges, pin deep patches with stainless steel and wire armature, and build up mineral-based restoration mortar in lifts to the original profile and an approved color.
  • Dutchman: cut out and replace a section with new stone pinned and set in lime mortar. Best for sills, lintels and projecting moldings.
  • Refacing: full-face recarving in restoration mortar over stainless lath where the face has failed widely; needs architect details and landmarks approval.
  • Avoid Portland stucco skim coats and non-breathable coatings. They trap water and pop off in sheets with the stone face.

Stoop restoration

  1. Survey the structure under the stoop (brick piers, iron or concrete), any vault, the railings and treads.
  2. Rebuild the substrate per the engineer, with waterproofing and areaway drainage.
  3. Treads and risers. New stone or patch over stainless armature; pitch treads forward about 1/8 in. per foot; keep riser heights uniform within 3/8 in.
  4. Railings. Restore ironwork, set posts in stainless sleeves or with lead or anchoring cement, prime with rust-inhibiting primer.

5.12 Common failures and how to avoid them

Common failure

Reverse laps. Tape or WRB at a window head tucked under the layer below, sending water behind the WRB. Teach the shingle rule at every opening and photograph each one.

Common failure

Sealed sill flanges. A continuous bead under the sill flange traps water in the pan. Leave the sill open or gapped.

Common failure

Missing kickouts and short base flashing. Water runs into the wall below a roof edge, or over base flashing under 8 in. when snow drifts. Make kickouts part of the roofing scope.

Common failure

Batts and poly on brick. Fiberglass between furring on a solid brick wall with poly behind the drywall. Room air leaks around the poly, condenses on cold brick and rots furring and joist ends. Use closed-cell foam directly on the brick.

Common failure

Portland mortar on soft brick, and insulation over wet materials. Both trap water. Specify lime mortar, and meter framing and masonry before insulating.

5.13 QC checklist

  • Drip edge, ice barrier, underlayment, valleys, step and counterflashing, kickouts and crickets installed and photographed.
  • Low-slope roof hose-tested with no ponding; welds probed; base flashing 8 in. min; coping pitched to the roof; overflow in place.
  • WRB lapped shingle-fashion, seams taped and rolled, tears repaired.
  • Every opening: sill pan with end dams, sill left to drain, tapes in order, head flap closed, interior air seal.
  • Masonry openings: lintels sound, sills pitched out, perimeter sealant on backer rod.
  • Insulation full and uncompressed, density or thickness verified, vapor retarder per code, thermal barrier over all foam.
  • Pre-drywall blower-door result recorded and leaks fixed.
  • Facade mock-up approved, joints raked to depth, mortar cured, patches matched, stoop treads pitched.

5.14 Handoff to the next trade

After dry-in the building is watertight for the rough trades: roof complete or under a tight temporary membrane, every window and door set and flashed or the opening taped closed, and the WRB intact. Crews from Chapters 06-08 clear any new envelope hole with the lead, and ESC or the roofer flashes it.

After insulation, the space handed to drywall and plaster (Chapter 09) looks like this:

  • Insulation inspection passed and posted; pre-drywall blower door passed.
  • Cavities full, foam trimmed flush with the framing, nothing proud of the studs.
  • Vapor retarder taped where required; no polyethylene on masonry walls.
  • Window and door gaps air-sealed, ready for returns and casings (Chapter 11).
  • Foam waste removed, floors swept, and indoor humidity below about 60 percent before board or plaster goes on.

Plumbing

How ESC plans, roughs, tests and trims plumbing in new houses and gutted masonry rowhouses: water service, drain-waste-vent, supply piping, water heating, showers and gas, with the different ways to do each and when we choose them. Lead carpenters, licensed plumbing subs, PMs and owners reviewing a plumbing layout all use this chapter.

6.1 Where plumbing sits in the job

Plumbing happens in three passes, each with its own inspection.

  1. Underground (under-slab). Building drain, sewer connection, water service and drains below a slab. On a new house this follows the foundation (Chapter 03) and precedes the slab pour. In a rowhouse it follows demolition of the cellar slab (Chapter 02).
  2. Rough-in (top-out). Stacks, branch drains, roof vents, supply piping, valve bodies, shower drains, gas piping and stub-outs. This follows framing (Chapter 04) and leads HVAC (Chapter 07) and electrical (Chapter 08), because drains have fixed slopes and can't bend around ducts.
  3. Trim (finish). Fixtures, trim kits, stops and water heater startup, after tile (Chapter 10), cabinets and counters (Chapter 11) and paint (Chapter 09).

Code references are to the IRC 2021 (plumbing chapters P25 to P32, fuel gas chapter G24) as a model baseline. Local amendments, the local plumbing code where a city has adopted the IPC or its own code, the utilities and the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) govern. Many older rowhouse cities have their own rules on house traps, plastic pipe and test pressures, so read the local code before the first layout meeting.

6.2 Before plumbing starts

  • Fixture schedule final, with spec sheets for every toilet, valve, drain, tub and appliance. Rough-in dimensions come from these sheets.
  • Tile layouts for every shower and wet floor (Chapter 10): drain type and location, slope direction, niches and tile thickness.
  • Cabinet shop drawings (Chapter 11) for sink centerlines and drawer stacks under sinks.
  • Framing complete in plumbing areas (Chapter 04): curbless showers recessed, toilet bays headed off, wet walls built 2x6 where the stack or a carrier needs it.
  • Roof deck on and the roofer booked for vent flashings (Chapter 05).
  • Sewer lateral camera-scoped, existing water service size and material known, utility applications submitted.
  • Ducts and stacks drawn on one coordinated plan with HVAC (Chapter 07) so nobody claims the same joist bay.
  • Old gas and water lines capped, tagged or removed by demolition (Chapter 02).

6.3 Water service, meter and backflow

The service runs from the utility tap or curb stop to the main shutoff inside the house, usually through the front foundation wall in a rowhouse. Old rowhouse services are often lead or galvanized and undersized. On a gut renovation, plan to replace it and coordinate the street side with the utility, which may own part of the line.

Inside, in order: wall sleeve, main shutoff, meter (usually set by the utility), house-side valve, backflow device if the purveyor requires one, pressure-reducing valve (PRV) if needed, then a thermal expansion tank wherever a check, backflow device or PRV closes the system.

6.4 Underground and under-slab work

A wrong drain location under concrete costs a saw-cut and a patch, so this is the least forgiving pass. In a rowhouse cellar the building drain usually runs from the stack to the front wall, where the lateral heads to the street. Many older houses have a house trap and fresh-air inlet at that wall; some AHJs require keeping them, others require removal. Ask before demolition.

  1. Confirm inverts. Shoot the lateral invert at the front wall and the lowest fixture drain. Fixtures below the lateral need an ejector. Fixtures whose flood rims sit below the next upstream public sewer manhole rim need a backwater valve (IRC P3008).
  2. Lay out from the plan. Snap stack, drain and cleanout locations on the subgrade, tied to the foundation walls.
  3. Trench and bed. Remove rock and debris and bed pipe on 3 to 4 in of compacted sand or fine gravel so it bears along its full length. Never support pipe on blocks.
  4. Set pipe to slope. 1/4 in per ft wherever depth allows. Code permits 1/8 in per ft on 3 in and larger; we use it only when depth forces it and note it on the as-built.
  5. Bring up risers. Stub stacks, drains and cleanouts above the future slab with test caps, and sleeve or wrap pipe where it passes through the slab.
  6. Test and inspect. Water test (6.12), inspect, then photograph every run with a tape in the frame.
  7. Backfill. Bedding material over the pipe first, then fill in lifts. Hand off to concrete (Chapter 03).

ESC standard

We camera-scope every rowhouse lateral before final design. Clay with roots, offset joints or bellies, or heavily scaled cast iron gets priced for replacement or lining to the property line in the base scope.

6.8 Rough-in (top-out)

  1. Layout walk. PM, lead plumber and lead carpenter walk each room with spec sheets, tile layouts and cabinet drawings, and paint fixture centerlines on subfloor and studs.
  2. Confirm finished wall thickness. Add up backer, membrane, thinset and tile or plaster, and write it on the stud. A flange set 12 in from studs ends up about 11 in from tile.
  3. Frame for plumbing. Head off joists under toilet bends and shower drains, add blocking for valves, stubs and grab bars, and clear every cut with the lead carpenter (Chapter 04).
  4. Set stacks and DWV. Stack from the cellar up with riser clamps bearing at each floor, then branches, flanges at finished floor height, tub and shower drains, and vents.
  5. Roof penetration. Stack vent through the roof, flashed with the roofer (Chapter 05), at least 10 ft from operable windows and intakes, including the neighbor's, unless 2 ft above them (IRC P3103.5).
  6. Supply. Headers or manifolds, lines and stubs at the 6.9 dimensions. Valve bodies plumb, with plaster guards, at the depth the wall build-up needs.
  7. Gas. Pipe appliance locations per 6.11 and cap every outlet.
  8. Protect and label. Shield plates, capped and tagged stubs, hot and cold marked, manifold ports labeled.
  9. Test, photograph, inspect. Run the 6.12 tests and photograph every wall and floor with a tape in the frame before close-in.

A three-bath rowhouse with kitchen and laundry typically takes a two-person crew two to three weeks to rough, plus a day for testing. Order at contract: thermostatic valves, linear drains, wall-hung carriers and specialty tubs often take 2 to 6 weeks, and a utility meter set or new tap can take 4 to 12.

6.15 Common failures and how to avoid them

Common failure

Dimensions taken from studs. Tile pushes the wall out and the toilet tank hits it, or a valve trim won't reach after the tile changes from porcelain to thick stone. Measure from the finished face, lock tile thickness before valves go in, and keep plaster guards on until tile is done.

Common failure

Over-cut joists. A rowhouse joist notched for a toilet bend sags. Every cut beyond R502.8 goes through the lead carpenter, and the engineer if needed, and gets headed or reinforced.

Common failure

Curbless drain at the wrong height. Water ponds at the threshold or won't clear. Set the drain from the tile layout and a calculated bed thickness, with the tile setter present.

Common failure

Siphoned traps. A fixture moves after rough-in and its arm is now too long, or the vent lands below the weir. Any late fixture move triggers a vent check.

Common failure

Frozen pipes and dripping relief valves. Supply lines in a rowhouse's front or rear masonry wall freeze; a new PRV closes the system and the T&P weeps because no expansion tank was charged. Keep supply out of exterior walls and check tank charge at startup.

Common failure

Screws through lines and unbonded CSST. Missing shield plates and CSST the electrician never heard about. Walk every wall before close-in and put CSST bonding on the electrical rough checklist.

6.16 QC checklist

  • Installed valves, drains and carriers match the spec sheets on site.
  • Toilet flanges within 1/4 in of specified distance from finished wall, at finished floor height.
  • Valves plumb, square, at height and within plaster guard depth.
  • Shower drains on the tile layout and at agreed bed height.
  • Horizontal drains in continuous slope, no bellies or back-pitch.
  • Every trap vented within its arm limit; no vent below a weir.
  • Cleanouts at stack bases and the front wall, with clearance and access.
  • Riser clamps at each floor on cast iron stacks.
  • Shield plates wherever pipe is within 1-1/4 in of a framing face.
  • No supply in exterior masonry walls; nothing chased into party walls.
  • Rated penetrations firestopped; roof vents flashed and clear of neighbors' windows.
  • All tests passed with gauges photographed; CSST bonded; stubs and ports labeled.

6.17 Handoff

After rough-in, plumbing hands off to insulation and drywall (Chapter 09) and tile (Chapter 10). The space should look like this:

  • Rough inspections passed and posted; system holding pressure or drained and capped as the PM directs.
  • Stubs capped, protected and projecting correctly past the finished wall plane.
  • Plaster guards on; tub and shower drains plugged and covered.

HVAC & Ventilation

How ESC sizes, selects, routes, tests and starts up heating, cooling and ventilation systems in new houses and gut-renovated rowhouses, with the system types we use and when each one fits. Project managers, mechanical subs, lead carpenters coordinating chases and owners choosing a system all use this chapter.

7.1 Where HVAC sits in the job

HVAC design starts in preconstruction (Chapter 01), long before the sub shows up, because system choice decides chase sizes, soffit locations, roof loads and electrical service size. Field work starts after framing is substantially complete and the building is dried in (Chapters 04 and 05). It runs in two visits: rough-in, which ends at the mechanical rough inspection and duct leakage test, and trim-out and startup, which happens after drywall, paint and most finishes.

In the rough-in, the order inside the open framing is fixed: drains first, ducts second, everything else after. Gravity drains (Chapter 06) can't move. Ducts are the biggest and least flexible thing in the ceiling. Supply piping, gas, line sets and wiring (Chapter 08) bend around both.

On a new house the ducts usually live in a basement, between open-web floor trusses and in a few planned soffits. In a rowhouse there's no attic, no crawlspace and only a 16 to 20 ft wide floor plate between two masonry party walls. Every chase costs closet space and every horizontal run either fits the joist bays or becomes a soffit, so the rowhouse case drives most choices below.

Code references are to the IRC 2021 as the model baseline, and to the NEC 2023 for equipment electrical. Local amendments, the adopted energy code edition and the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) govern. Equipment installation instructions are part of the code (IRC M1401.1), so where the manufacturer asks for more, the manufacturer wins.

7.2 Before HVAC starts

The PM confirms these before the mechanical sub mobilizes for rough-in.

  • Manual J room-by-room load, Manual S equipment selection and Manual D duct design are complete, reviewed by ESC and the architect, and submitted with the permit where the AHJ requires it.
  • Equipment submittals approved and ordered, with delivery dates confirmed (lead times in 7.11).
  • Framing complete on the floors being roughed: walls, blocking, stair openings, beams and headers set (Chapter 04). No mechanical work in bays the engineer still has open for repair.
  • Building dried in: roof, windows and temporary closures in place (Chapter 05).
  • Roof curbs, pitch pockets or boots for line sets, flues and exhaust located and coordinated with the roofer before membrane goes on (Chapter 05).
  • Structural review of roof dunnage or condenser platforms, and of any joist notching or boring beyond IRC R502.8 limits, by the engineer.
  • Drains laid out or installed (Chapter 06) and the joint coordination walk held.
  • Electrical service size confirmed for heat pump and air-handler loads, including electric backup heat strips if used (Chapter 08).
  • Rowhouse: party-wall fire separations identified. Nothing penetrates a rated party wall without a listed firestop system and AHJ acceptance.

7.3 Loads and design: Manual J, S and D

The IRC requires heating and cooling equipment to be sized per ACCA Manual S from loads calculated per ACCA Manual J (M1401.3), and duct systems to be designed per Manual D (M1601.1). ESC treats these as the start of the design, not paperwork for the permit.

Manual J: what goes in

  • Design temperatures from the ACCA tables for the location: 99% heating and 1% cooling dry-bulb. Indoor design is 70°F heating and 75°F cooling. Don't pad them.
  • Envelope as drawn: actual wall, roof and slab R-values, window U-factor and SHGC, and orientation (Chapter 05).
  • Infiltration from the project's blower door target. A gut-renovated rowhouse built to ESC's air-sealing details will test far tighter than the "average" default in most software. Use the target, then confirm it at the test.
  • Party walls. Where a heated neighbor is on the other side, model the party wall as a no-load surface. Where the neighbor is vacant, unheated or a different use, model it as exposed to an unconditioned space. Ask; don't assume.
  • Room by room, never a block load alone. A top floor under a low-slope roof can carry several times the load per square foot of a middle-floor bedroom.

Manual S: picking equipment

Select equipment from the manufacturer's expanded performance data at the design conditions, not the nameplate. Under Manual S, cooling equipment and heat pumps are normally sized at 95 to 115% of the total cooling load, with up to 125% allowed for heat pumps where heating governs. For heat pumps, check heating capacity at the 99% design temperature and decide in writing whether backup heat is needed.

Manual D: ducts that deliver

Manual D works from the room airflows in Manual J and the blower's available external static pressure. After subtracting coil, filter and grille losses, each run is sized so its equivalent length fits the pressure left. Typical design targets: supply trunk velocity under 900 fpm, branch velocity under 600 fpm, return grille face velocity under 500 fpm so the house is quiet.

7.5 Ductwork

Layout: trunk and branch

Most ESC duct systems are trunk and branch. A rectangular or round trunk leaves the supply plenum and steps down in size as branches peel off. Each branch runs to one register, with a manual balancing damper at the takeoff where a tech can reach it. With good windows, registers can sit on inside walls to shorten runs; with large or old glass, supply near the exterior wall.

Keep trunks out of exterior walls and out of the joist bays drains need. In rowhouses, trunks belong in a hall soffit or the cellar ceiling and branches run in the joist bays toward the front and rear of the house, parallel to the joists, which usually span party wall to party wall. Branches that must cross joists go in a soffit or through holes the engineer has approved.

Return paths

A bedroom with a supply and a closed door pressurizes, pushes air out through the envelope and starves the system. Every room with a door gets a return path: a ducted return, a jump duct through the ceiling to the hall, or a transfer grille pair through the wall with a lined, offset cavity for sound. Target 3 Pa (0.012 in. w.c.) or less between a closed room and the hall with the air handler on high. A 1 in. door undercut alone is not enough for most bedrooms.

  • Kitchen hood: 100 cfm intermittent or 25 cfm continuous minimum. Hood ducts must be smooth metal (galvanized, stainless or copper), airtight, with a backdraft damper, and must discharge outdoors (M1503). Never into a joist bay, soffit or attic.
  • Makeup air: a hood exhausting more than 400 cfm needs makeup air at roughly the exhaust rate, through a motorized damper interlocked with the hood (M1503.6). Without it, a big hood in a tight rowhouse backdrafts appliances and whistles at every door.
  • Dryer exhaust: 4 in. smooth metal, 35 ft maximum developed length less fitting allowances (M1502.4), or the manufacturer's longer listing. Heat pump dryers need no duct at all.

ESC standard

We steer owners toward hoods at or under 400 cfm with good capture (a deep hood, sized wider than the cooktop). When the owner chooses a larger hood, the makeup air system is priced and designed with it.

7.7 Line sets, condensate and chases

Refrigerant line sets

  • Size per the manufacturer for the actual length and lift. Roof condensers feeding a cellar air handler can exceed some models' vertical lift limit; check before submittal.
  • Insulate the suction line (and both lines on heat pumps) with closed-cell insulation, 1/2 in. minimum, 3/4 in. where it's outside or in unconditioned space. UV-protect exterior insulation with a jacket or line-set cover.
  • Braze with a nitrogen purge flowing so the inside of the pipe doesn't scale. Torque mini-split flares to the manufacturer's value.
  • Since the 2025 refrigerant transition, new split equipment ships with A2L (mildly flammable) refrigerants such as R-454B or R-32. Follow the manufacturer's room-size, leak-detection and ventilation requirements for the charge, and make sure the tech is trained for A2L.
  • Pressure test with dry nitrogen to the manufacturer's test pressure and hold it, then evacuate to 500 microns or less and confirm a decay test before releasing the charge. Do this before the chase is closed.

Condensate

Every cooling coil and every ductless head makes water. Primary condensate lines are 3/4 in. minimum and slope at 1/8 in. per foot (1%) minimum to an approved receptor (IRC M1411.3). Where an overflow would damage the building, which in a closet or soffit is always, add a secondary drain pan with its own line or a float switch that shuts the unit off (M1411.3.1). Trap the line per the manufacturer. Condensate pumps are a last resort and need a safety switch wired to shut the unit down.

Chases and soffits

Fig. 7.1 shows the rowhouse pattern: one stacked chase beside the stair carries line sets and condensate from the roof to the cellar, with soffits on each floor in the hall where ceiling height matters least. Plumbing vents share the far side of the stair (Chapter 06). The coordination process:

  1. Draw it. The mechanical sub produces a coordination drawing showing every duct, pipe and line set in each soffit and chase with sizes and elevations. ESC overlays plumbing and electrical and resolves clashes on paper.
  2. Mark it. Chalk chase and soffit outlines on floors and ceilings at full size. Walk it with the architect for any soffit visible in finished rooms.
  3. Install by priority. Drains, then ducts, then line sets and condensate, then supply piping and gas, then electrical.
  4. Frame around it. The carpenter frames soffits and chase walls after the services are hung, leaving 1 in. clear for sealing and service (Fig. 7.3).
  5. Fireblock it. Fireblock chases at each floor line and seal penetrations with listed materials (IRC R302.11). Rated assemblies get a listed firestop system, and fire dampers wherever a duct passes through one where the design requires them.
  6. Leave access. Access panels for dampers, air handler filters, condensate traps and float switches, located before drywall.

7.8 Hydronic and radiant heat

Hydronic systems work well where ducts don't fit. A condensing (mod-con) boiler or an air-to-water heat pump makes hot water; circulators move it through zones to radiators, baseboard, fan coils or floor tubing. Outdoor reset control lowers the supply temperature as outdoor temperature rises, so a mod-con boiler spends most of the season with return water below about 130°F, where it condenses and earns its efficiency.

  1. Survey existing radiators. Rate each (EDR) against the new room load; mark which stay, go or move.
  2. Pull and protect. Remove and tag radiators before demolition (Chapter 02); pressure test before reset.
  3. Pipe new mains and branches. PEX-AL-PEX, oxygen-barrier PEX or copper, insulated where it runs through unconditioned space, kept out of exterior walls.
  4. Set the boiler or heat pump. Sealed combustion only for boilers, vented per its listing.
  5. Pressure test the system before cover, then fill, purge air and start up after finishes.
  6. Reset radiators after flooring is finished and coated (Chapter 11), with new valves and bleeders.

7.9 The installation process

  1. Design review. ESC, the architect and the mechanical sub review Manual J, S and D, the coordination drawing and the equipment submittals. Confirm roof loads, electrical loads and lift limits.
  2. Layout walk. Mark equipment, chases, soffits, registers, grilles and terminations. Keep registers clear of furniture walls and hardwood borders.
  3. Penetrations. Core or cut wall and roof openings. Roofer installs curbs and flashing (Chapter 05). Masonry openings at party walls are not allowed; rear and front wall openings get sleeved and sealed.
  4. Set equipment. Air handlers on vibration pads or hung on listed hangers with secondary pans; rooftop units on dunnage, leveled and anchored per the engineer; ERV hung with isolators.
  5. Trunks and plenums. Hung on strap or rod hangers at 8 to 10 ft max, sealed with mastic as they go.
  6. Branches, returns and exhaust. Rigid branches with dampers at the takeoff and boots fastened to framing; return paths framed; bath, hood, dryer and ERV ducts run to dampered terminations.
  7. Line sets and condensate. Run, support, braze or flare, nitrogen pressure test. Condensate lines sloped and trapped, secondary protection installed.
  8. Rough-in duct leakage test. Seal boots, pressurize the system to 25 Pa and measure (7.10). Fix and retest until it passes.
  9. Mechanical rough inspection. Inspection hold below. Then insulation (Chapter 05) and drywall (Chapter 09).
  10. Protect. Cap every boot and grille opening. No equipment runs during drywall sanding without filters and the owner's permission; ESC prefers temporary heat instead.
  11. Trim-out. After paint: registers, grilles, thermostats, condensers charged, ductless heads hung, radiators reset.
  12. Startup and commissioning. Per 7.10.

Inspection hold

Mechanical rough-in. No insulation or drywall over ducts, line sets, condensate, gas piping or exhaust ducts until the AHJ's mechanical rough inspection is signed and the rough-in duct leakage test report is in the project file. Photograph every soffit and chase before it's closed.

Inspection hold

Line set pressure and vacuum. The line set chase isn't closed until the tech has held a nitrogen pressure test and recorded the vacuum decay result on the startup sheet. Opening a finished chase to find a leaking braze joint costs far more than waiting a day.

Inspection hold

Firestopping. Penetrations through fire-rated assemblies and fireblocking at chase floor lines are inspected before cover where the AHJ requires it. Keep the listed system data sheets on site.

7.10 Testing, commissioning and startup

ESC doesn't call a system done when it turns on. It's done when it's measured.

  1. Duct leakage, rough-in. Duct pressurization test at 25 Pa with registers taped. The energy code limit is typically 4.0 cfm25 per 100 sq ft of conditioned floor area for total leakage with the air handler installed, or 3.0 cfm25 per 100 sq ft if tested before the air handler is set (IRC N1103.3.5). Depending on the edition the AHJ has adopted, ducts entirely inside the envelope may be exempt or held to a looser limit; ESC tests them anyway.
  2. Duct leakage, final. Retest after trim-out if the rough-in test was done without the air handler, or if the AHJ requires a post-construction test.
  3. Blower door. Coordinated with the envelope test (Chapter 05). Confirms the infiltration value used in Manual J.
  4. Equipment startup. Per the manufacturer's startup sheet: refrigerant charge verified by subcooling (TXV/EEV) or superheat (fixed orifice); combustion analysis on gas appliances; boiler pressure, purge and reset curve.
  5. Airflow. Total external static pressure measured and compared to the blower table; total airflow typically 350 to 400 cfm per ton in cooling unless the designer set otherwise. Room airflows measured with a flow hood and balanced to within 10% of Manual D, or 20% on small branches.
  6. Ventilation. ERV supply and exhaust measured and balanced within 10% of each other and of the design rate. Bath fans and the hood measured at the grille or cap.
  7. Controls. Thermostats, zoning, dampers, makeup air interlock, condensate safety switches and outdoor reset all function-tested.
  8. Owner turnover. Manuals, test reports, filter schedule, warranties and a controls walk-through.

7.12 Common failures and how to avoid them

Common failure

Oversized equipment. Rule-of-thumb sizing (a ton per 500 sq ft) on a tight house gives units two to three times too big. They short-cycle and leave the house clammy. Use a real Manual J with the blower door target, and pick variable-speed equipment that can turn down.

Common failure

Crushed or snaking flex. Long flex runs pulled around joists and pinched by soffit framing deliver half their design airflow. Rigid branches, flex only at the boot, and no framing until the ducts are inspected.

Common failure

No return path. Jump ducts or transfer grilles in every closable room, verified with a manometer.

Common failure

Condensate leaks in finished ceilings. Flat or back-pitched lines, missing traps and no secondary pan over a finished room. Slope checked with a level, traps per the manufacturer, a float switch on every air handler above finish, and a water test with a bucket before the ceiling closes.

Common failure

Roof penetrations that leak. Line sets run through a membrane with caulk and a hope. Use a curb with a hooded cap or a manufactured line-set boot, flashed by the roofer, with drip loops so water runs off before it reaches the opening.

7.13 QC checklist

  • Manual J, S and D on file, matching the equipment actually installed.
  • Equipment set level, accessible for service, with filters reachable without tools.
  • All ducts inside the conditioned space, or a written exception on file.
  • Every joint sealed with mastic; no cloth tape; boots fastened to framing.
  • Return path in every closable room.
  • Bath, hood, dryer and ERV ducts terminate outdoors with dampers; none end in a joist bay or soffit.
  • Makeup air installed and interlocked for any hood over 400 cfm.
  • Line sets insulated, supported, protected at penetrations; pressure and vacuum results recorded.
  • Condensate sloped, trapped, with secondary protection and a water test done.
  • Roof and wall penetrations flashed and inspected by ESC (Chapter 05).
  • Fireblocking at chase floor lines; listed firestop at rated penetrations.
  • Rough-in duct leakage test passed and report filed; rough inspection signed.
  • Startup sheets, airflow balance report, ERV balance and controls test complete.
  • Owner turnover packet and walk-through done.

7.14 Handoff to the next trade

At the end of rough-in, the space handed to insulation (Chapter 05) and drywall (Chapter 09) looks like this:

  • Boots and grille openings flush with the planned drywall face and fastened so the drywall crew can cut and seal to them. Every opening capped.
  • Soffits and chases framed around the services with 1 in. clearance and access panels located and blocked.

Electrical & Low Voltage

How ESC sizes services, lays out circuits, roughs, inspects and trims power, lighting, life-safety and low-voltage systems in new houses and gutted masonry rowhouses, with the different wiring and control methods and when we use each. Written for lead carpenters, licensed electrical and low-voltage subs, PMs and owners reviewing a lighting or device plan.

8.1 Where electrical sits in the job

Electrical work happens in four passes. Each one has an inspection or a utility sign-off attached to it.

  1. Temporary power and service planning. During preconstruction (Chapter 01) we file the utility service request, because utility work orders and meter sets often take 6 to 12 weeks. On a rowhouse gut, the existing service usually feeds GFCI-protected temporary power during demolition (Chapter 02).
  2. Rough-in. Boxes, cable or conduit, recessed housings, low-voltage cabling and the panel can. Follows plumbing (Chapter 06) and HVAC (Chapter 07) rough, because cable can bend around pipe and duct and they can't bend around cable.
  3. Service. Meter socket, service conductors, grounding electrode system and main panel. Often inspected separately so the utility can set the meter early.
  4. Trim (finish). Devices, fixtures, breakers, labeling and testing, after paint (Chapter 09), tile (Chapter 10) and cabinets and counters (Chapter 11).

Code references are to NEC 2023 (NFPA 70) and IRC 2021 as model baselines. Many jurisdictions are still on NEC 2017 or 2020, and several large cities write their own electrical codes. Local amendments and the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) govern every requirement in this chapter. Confirm the adopted edition on the permit before layout starts.

8.2 Before electrical starts

The electrician should not walk into a rough-in that's still moving. These items come from earlier trades and must be complete or answered.

  • Permit drawings show the panel location, service size and a load calculation; the utility service application is filed and the meter location is approved by the utility.
  • Framing (Chapter 04) is complete and punched: blocking for heavy fixtures, fans, TV mounts and grab bars installed; soffits and chases framed; beams and engineered joists marked with their no-bore zones.
  • Plumbing drains and vents (Chapter 06) and ductwork and line sets (Chapter 07) are roughed, so we route around fixed pipe rather than the reverse.
  • Kitchen and bath cabinet shop drawings approved (Chapter 11): appliance specs, hood, under-cabinet lighting, island depth and countertop height fixed.
  • Lighting plan, switching plan and low-voltage plan signed by the owner and architect, with fixture cut sheets for every recessed or specialty fixture.
  • Rowhouse only: existing wiring traced and labeled; live circuits identified; knob-and-tube located (Section 8.13); party-wall routing agreed (no chasing a shared wall without the structural and party-wall review in Chapter 02).
  • Nameplate data for the heat pump, EV charger, range, water heater and dryer in hand for the load calculation.

8.3 Step-by-step process

  1. Load calculation and service decision. Run NEC Article 220 (the optional dwelling method in 220.82 is typical) with real nameplate loads, including future EV and heat pump circuits. Choose 100, 200 or 400 A, or a smaller service with load management (Section 8.4).
  2. Layout walk. Electrician, PM and lead carpenter walk the framed house with the lighting plan. Mark every box, recessed light and low-voltage drop on the framing with paint or a lumber crayon. Owner attends or signs the marked plan.
  3. Panel can and service equipment. Set the panel enclosure, meter socket and service raceway. Drive ground rods or tie into the concrete-encased electrode left by the foundation crew (Chapter 03).
  4. Boxes and housings. Nail or screw boxes set to finished wall thickness (1/2 in drywall, 5/8 in drywall, plaster, or tile on backer). Install recessed housings, fan boxes and remodel-rated boxes where framing is existing.
  5. Bore and pull. Bore studs and joists within code limits (Fig. 8.3), install nail plates, and pull home runs to the panel. Label every home run at the panel with circuit number and room.
  6. Make up. Splice grounds, fold conductors into boxes, leave 6 in of free conductor and at least 3 in outside the box (NEC 300.14). Check box fill.
  7. Low-voltage rough. Data, coax, speaker, security and intercom cable pulled to the structured media enclosure, with separation from power (Section 8.12).
  8. Rough inspection. Inspection hold. Nothing gets insulated or closed until it passes.
  9. Trim. Devices, plates, fixtures, breakers and appliances connected after paint and tile. Torque lugs and breaker terminals to the listed values with a torque screwdriver.
  10. Test and label. Test every GFCI and AFCI, check polarity and ground with a plug-in tester, test smoke and CO interconnection, and type the panel directory.
  11. Final inspection and utility release. Inspection hold. Final electrical sign-off is a prerequisite for the certificate of occupancy or final building sign-off.

The main panel goes where the service enters, usually the cellar front wall in a rowhouse and the garage or utility room in a new house. Subpanels

A 3- or 4-story rowhouse loses a lot of copper and voltage running every circuit to the cellar. A subpanel on an upper floor (often a hall closet wall, which is allowed as long as it's outside the clothes closet itself) shortens home runs. Feeders to a subpanel are four-wire: two ungrounded conductors, an insulated neutral and an equipment grounding conductor. The neutral bar in the subpanel is isolated from the enclosure; the bonding screw or strap is removed. Neutral-to-ground bonding happens only at the service disconnect.

8.7 Receptacle layout

General wall receptacles

In habitable rooms, NEC 210.52(A) requires receptacles so that no point along the floor line of any wall is more than 6 ft from a receptacle. In practice that means receptacles no more than 12 ft apart along a wall, and one within 6 ft of each door opening or wall end. Any wall space 2 ft or wider counts, including the wall beside a door. Fixed glass panels count as wall; sliding door panels don't.

Kitchen countertops

  • At least two 20 A small-appliance branch circuits serve the countertop and dining-area receptacles (210.11(C)(1)). We alternate them along the counter so two high-wattage appliances side by side don't share a circuit.
  • Every countertop space 12 in or wider gets a receptacle, and no point along the wall line of the counter may be more than 24 in from a receptacle (210.52(C)(1)). That works out to 48 in maximum between receptacles and 24 in maximum from each end.
  • Range tops, sinks and refrigerators split the counter into separate spaces, each measured on its own.
  • Receptacles mount on or above the counter, not more than 20 in above it (NEC 2023 210.52(C)(3)), and may not face up in the countertop surface unless listed for it.
  • Islands and peninsulas: NEC 2023 no longer requires a receptacle, but if one is provided it must follow the location rules. Many jurisdictions on NEC 2017 or 2020 still require at least one. Verify with the AHJ during design so the island can be detailed for a side-mounted or pop-up listed assembly.
  • Dedicated circuits: refrigerator, dishwasher, disposal, microwave, hood, range or cooktop and wall oven.

Bathrooms, laundry and outdoors

Each bathroom needs at least one receptacle within 3 ft of the outside edge of each basin, on a wall or partition adjacent to the basin or on the countertop side of the vanity (210.52(D)). It can't be more than 12 in below the countertop. Bathroom receptacles need a 20 A circuit (210.11(C)(3)). ESC runs a dedicated 20 A circuit per primary bath because of hair tools and heated towel bars. Exhaust fans and lights go on a separate circuit so a tripped GFCI doesn't leave the room dark.

Laundry gets its own 20 A circuit (210.11(C)(2)) plus a 30 A, 240 V circuit for an electric dryer. Outdoors, a one-family house needs a GFCI receptacle at the front and back, accessible from grade and not more than 6 ft 6 in above grade (210.52(E)), plus one at each balcony, deck or porch accessible from inside. In a rowhouse, the "front" receptacle often lands at the stoop or areaway.

8.10 Smoke and CO alarms

IRC R314 requires smoke alarms in each sleeping room, outside each separate sleeping area in the immediate vicinity of the bedrooms, and on every story including basements. In new construction and in alterations that require a permit, alarms are hardwired with battery backup and interconnected so that one sounds all (R314.4, R314.6). Where finishes aren't removed, listed wireless interconnected alarms are allowed. CO alarms are required outside each sleeping area where the house has a fuel-burning appliance or an attached garage (R315). An all-electric house with no attached garage may not require them; ESC installs combination smoke/CO units anyway.

  • Ceiling mount at least 4 in from the wall; wall mount with the top 4 in to 12 in below the ceiling.
  • At least 3 ft horizontally from a bathroom door with a tub or shower and from supply registers (NFPA 72 spacing referenced by the IRC).
  • Keep every alarm at least 10 ft from a cooking appliance, and use photoelectric alarms within 20 ft of one (NFPA 72).

8.11 Grounding and bonding

Grounding connects the system to earth; bonding ties metal parts together so a fault trips the breaker fast. The grounding electrode system (NEC 250.50) must use every electrode present at the building:

  • Concrete-encased electrode (Ufer). At least 20 ft of 1/2 in or larger rebar, or 20 ft of #4 bare copper, in a footing in direct contact with earth (250.52(A)(3)). On new houses we coordinate with the foundation crew (Chapter 03) to leave a rebar stub or copper tail accessible above the slab. It's the best electrode available and costs nothing if planned.
  • Ground rods. 8 ft long, driven full length. A single rod must test at 25 ohms or less or be supplemented by a second rod at least 6 ft away (250.53(A)(2) and (A)(3)). We drive two rather than test. In a rowhouse cellar, drive through the slab or in the rear yard and run the GEC exposed and protected.
  • Metal water pipe. If 10 ft or more of metal water pipe is in contact with earth, it's an electrode and must be supplemented (250.53(D)). The connection is within the first 5 ft of where the pipe enters the building. Install a jumper around the water meter.

Bond interior metal water piping (250.104(A)) and metal gas piping (250.104(B)). CSST gas piping follows the manufacturer's bonding instructions, typically a #6 Cu jumper from the gas service near the meter to the grounding electrode system. When a rowhouse replaces a copper or lead water service with plastic, the old water-pipe ground is gone; add rods or another electrode during the same job.

ESC standard

Two ground rods on every service, plus the concrete-encased electrode on every new foundation. Water and gas bonds are photographed with the clamp label visible before any finish covers them, and the photos go in the project file.

8.12 Low voltage and communications (Division 27)

Low-voltage cable is pulled with or after the power rough, before insulation. Some jurisdictions require a separate low-voltage permit.

Structured wiring

  • Head end. A structured media enclosure in a ventilated, accessible spot with a dedicated 20 A receptacle. In rowhouses the cellar works if it stays dry; otherwise use a second-floor closet.
  • Data. Cat6 home runs to every TV location, office, Wi-Fi access point and fixed device; two drops at offices and TV walls. Keep each run under 90 m (295 ft) of permanent link. Terminate to T568B at both ends.
  • Coax. RG6 quad-shield to TV locations and the service entry, if the owner wants cable or an antenna.
  • Separation. Keep low-voltage cable at least 12 in from parallel power runs where practical and cross at 90 degrees. Never share a box with power unless it has a listed barrier (NEC 800.133).
  • Conduit. A 1-1/4 in or larger smurf tube or EMT from the head end to the attic or roof, and from each TV wall down to the equipment location, so future cable can be pulled.

Wi-Fi, AV, security and entry

Masonry party walls and metal lath block Wi-Fi, so plan one ceiling-mounted, PoE-powered access point per floor in a rowhouse. TV and speaker locations get pre-wire and blocking, with speaker cable home-run to the AV location. Security pre-wire covers door and window contacts, keypads and camera locations (Cat6 to each camera). For rowhouses, the front door entry is often a multi-story stoop: plan a video intercom or doorbell at the vestibule with Cat6 back to the head end, an electric strike or smart lock with a low-voltage power supply, and a release station on each main floor.

8.13 Knob-and-tube and old wiring in rowhouses

Pre-1940 rowhouses often still have knob-and-tube (K&T) in the walls and floors, sometimes still energized and spliced into later circuits. It has no ground, its insulation is brittle, and NEC 394.12 prohibits it in hollow spaces where insulation surrounds the conductors. Many insurers won't cover a house until it's removed.

Before demolition, the electrician traces every live circuit and kills K&T at the panel.

8.16 Common failures and how to avoid them

  • Boxes set for the wrong wall thickness. Tile on backer, plaster, or two layers of drywall leaves boxes buried. Write the finish on the framing at each box during the layout walk; use adjustable boxes in tiled areas.
  • Counter receptacles that collide with backsplashes or tile layout. Get the backsplash height and tile layout (Chapter 10) before setting kitchen boxes; center boxes on a grout line or a full tile where the owner cares.
  • Recessed lights that hit joists, ducts or sprinkler pipe. Lay out cans on the actual framing, not only from the reflected ceiling plan.
  • Drywall screws through cable. Missing nail plates at shallow bores, and cable run on the face of studs at corners. The QC walk before insulation catches this.
  • Lost water-pipe ground after plumbing replaces the service with plastic. Coordinate with Chapter 06 and add electrodes.
  • Utility delays. File the service request during preconstruction. Rowhouse vault and sidewalk work can need a street opening permit on top of the utility order.

Division 09 · Finishes (Gypsum Board, Plaster, Paint)

Drywall, Plaster & Paint

How ESC picks, hangs, finishes and paints wall and ceiling surfaces in new houses and gutted masonry rowhouses, including fire-rated and sound-isolated assemblies, veneer and traditional plaster, and the repair of historic plaster. Written for lead carpenters, drywall and plaster subs, painters, PMs and owners choosing a finish level.

9.1 Where this work sits in the job

Once board goes up, everything in the cavities is buried, so close-in is the last cheap chance to fix it. Drywall and plaster follow insulation (Chapter 05) and the passed rough-ins for plumbing, HVAC and electrical (Chapters 06, 07 and 08). They come before tile (Chapter 10), and before trim, cabinets and finished flooring (Chapter 11). Paint straddles those trades: primer and first coats before trim and cabinets, final coat and touch-up after. On a typical floor, hanging takes a few days and taping three or four coats over about a week.

Code references are to the 2021 International Residential Code (IRC) as a model baseline, and to Gypsum Association publications GA-214 (finish levels) and GA-216 (application). Local amendments and the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) govern every requirement in this chapter. Rated assemblies must match their tested listing (UL, GA File or manufacturer report) in every detail.

9.2 Before board goes up

The building must be closed, dry, heated and finished behind the walls:

  • Framing, plumbing, mechanical, electrical and insulation inspections passed (Chapters 04 to 08).
  • Building weathertight, windows and flashing complete (Chapter 05). No wet board, ever.
  • Framing at or below 19 percent moisture, and planar: adjacent members within 1/8 in. Rowhouse joists sistered or furred (Chapter 04).
  • Blocking in for cabinets, grab bars, TV mounts, handrails and shower doors.
  • Boxes flush with the finished face, allowing for two layers on rated or sound walls. Nail plates on pipes and cable within 1-1/4 in. of the stud face.
  • Heat on: at least 50 °F for 48 hours before taping and until compound is dry. Dehumidifiers in summer and basements.
  • Every wall and ceiling photographed open, with a tape in frame.

ESC standard

No board goes on until the PM has walked every room with the close-in checklist and the photo record is uploaded.

A 12-ft sheet rarely turns a rowhouse stair. Boom stock in through window openings before sash goes in, or buy 8- and 10-ft lengths for upper floors and accept more butt joints. Stack board flat near bearing walls, never in one pile at mid-span on old joists.

9.5 Hanging board

  1. Mark framing. Mark stud, joist and blocking locations on the floor and plates so hangers can find them once covered.

  3. Walls, top row first. Hang horizontally on walls up to about 9 ft, tight to the ceiling board.
  4. Bottom row. Hold the bottom sheet 3/8 to 1/2 in. off the subfloor with a foot lifter.

9.7 Rowhouse walls: masonry party walls and existing plaster

Each masonry wall in a gut gets one of four treatments, decided per wall in preconstruction (Chapter 01):

  • Exposed brick. Cleaned, repointed with a lime mortar, and sealed or left raw. Wiring runs surface-mounted, in the floor or in a furred chase.
  • Plaster direct to masonry. Traditional brown and finish coats right on the brick, or a modern gypsum or lime basecoat. Thin, durable, and lets the masonry dry inward.
  • Furred board. 1x or 2x furring, or steel studs, held off the brick with at least 1/2 in. air space, then glass-mat board. Leaves a service cavity for wiring.
  • Keep the existing plaster. Covered next.

Common failure

Poly vapor barrier or paper-faced board tight against damp brick. Masonry takes on moisture, and trapped against paper it grows mold. Leave an air space, use glass-mat board, and follow Chapter 05 on exterior masonry.

Existing plaster: restore, overlay or replace

Historic plaster is usually three coats on wood lath, about 7/8 in. thick. It is heavy, hard and quiet. Test before deciding: a hollow knock means delamination, movement under hand pressure means broken keys.

Any work on painted plaster or woodwork in a house built before 1978 falls under the EPA Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) Rule once it disturbs more than 6 sq ft per interior room. That means a certified firm and renovator, containment, HEPA dust control and cleaning verification. Test for lead before pricing.

9.14 Common failures and how to avoid them

Common failure

Cracks at openings, pops and ridges. Seams at head corners, wet lumber, nails, or board not held tight. Wrap openings, check moisture, screw from the center out.

Common failure

Joint flashing and shrink cracks. Joints show through sheen paint, and thick premixed fills crack. Use Level 5 and a quality primer under sheen; prefill deep gaps with setting compound.

Common failure

Shorted sound wall. A screw into a stud, a cabinet bolted to framing, back-to-back boxes or open perimeter gaps. Hold the pre-board inspection and brief every trade who fastens to that wall.

Common failure

Peeling and bleed-through. Latex over unscuffed oil paint peels; stains and tannins bleed through latex. Test with alcohol, degloss and use a bonding primer; spot prime stains with shellac or oil.

Tile & Stone

How ESC preps substrates, waterproofs showers, sets, grouts and protects ceramic, porcelain and natural stone in new houses and gutted masonry rowhouses, with the different systems for each step and when we pick them. Lead carpenters, tile subs, PMs and owners choosing between shower systems all use this chapter.

10.1 Where tile sits in the job

Tile depends on everything under it. A tile floor is only as good as the joists and subfloor it sits on, and a shower is only as dry as the waterproofing behind it. Most tile failures we're called to fix are substrate or waterproofing failures, not bad setting.

Tile work happens in three passes:

  1. Substrate and waterproofing. Flattening, backer, membranes, shower pans, curbs, niches, benches and the flood test. Follows plumbing rough (Chapter 06) and drywall hang in adjacent rooms (Chapter 09).
  2. Setting and grouting. Walls, then floors, in most rooms.
  3. Sealant and protection. Movement joints, stone sealer and floor protection, ideally after paint (Chapter 09) and before cabinets and trim (Chapter 11).

In a rowhouse gut, tile lands in the last third of the schedule, often on two or three stacked baths at once; keep one tile crew on all of them so details match. New construction follows the same sequence with flatter substrates, so prep goes faster.

Codes and standards cited here are the IRC 2021, NEC 2023, the TCNA Handbook and ANSI A108/A118/A137. Local amendments and the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) govern; where a manufacturer's written instructions are stricter, follow them, because the warranty depends on it.

10.2 Before tile starts

The tile foreman walks every tiled room with the PM before mobilizing. Anything missing here is a reason to delay.

  • Floor framing meets the deflection target for the tile chosen (Table 10.1); sistering and leveling of old joists signed off (Chapter 04). Subfloor glued and screwed, no soft spots.
  • Drain bodies of the right type (clamping ring for a liner, bonding flange for a sheet membrane) set at the right height. Valve bodies set to finished wall depth, with heads and sprays located from the tile layout. Rough inspection passed (Chapter 06).
  • Blocking for grab bars, glass hardware, niches, benches and wall-hung fixtures.
  • Electrical rough done for heated floors and thermostats (Chapter 08).
  • Envelope closed, building at 50°F or more, party walls dry (Chapter 05).
  • Tile, setting materials and trims on site, checked for lot, shade and quantity. Approved layout drawing for every tiled room.

ESC standard

Order 10% overage for straight lay, 15% for diagonal, herringbone or large-format, 20% for heavily veined stone. Special-order tile and stone run 4 to 12 weeks, so selections lock before framing. Set from three cartons at a time to blend shade.

10.3 Substrates: stiffness and flatness

Deflection

Tile and grout don't bend; a floor that deflects too much cracks grout first, then tile. The TCNA Handbook uses a live-load deflection limit of L/360 for ceramic and porcelain and L/720 for natural stone. L/720 often means deeper joists, closer spacing or a mid-span beam, so check it before stone is promised to the owner.

The subfloor between joists also has to be stiff. Over wood joists at 16 in on center, ceramic typically goes on a single layer of 23/32 in tongue-and-groove plywood (some uncoupling membranes list thinner panels in their tables). Stone needs two layers, with the second layer's joints offset from the first and screwed to it, not to the joists.

Rowhouse floors are the hard case: rough-sawn 3x8 or 3x10 joists pocketed into the party walls, often with 1 to 2 in of sag. Tile can't fix that; Chapter 04 does. A mortar bed or stone floor adds roughly 15 to 20 psf of dead load, so tell the engineer early.

Flatness

Flatness is measured from the required plane, not from level. Large-format tile (any edge 15 in or longer) needs a flatter substrate because it can't follow dips.

Traditional mortar bed, done right

  4. Flood test. Plug the drain and fill to at least 2 in deep at the threshold, per IRC P2503.6. Mark the level and hold it for 24 hours.

  6. Bed. Pack a 4:1 to 5:1 sand-cement dry-pack bed with galvanized wire reinforcing at mid-depth, 1-1/4 in minimum at the drain, sloped 1/4 in per foot. Screed to the strainer height minus the tile and thinset.

Bonded membranes, done right

Sheet membrane: bond it with the mortar and notch the maker lists, embed it with a flat trowel so no air is trapped, lap seams 2 in, use preformed corners and pipe seals, and lap onto the drain's bonding flange.

Liquid membrane: bed the maker's fabric in the first coat at seams and corners, then apply two coats in opposite directions to the maker's thickness (most land near 20 mils dry). Check with a wet-film gauge and log the reading.

Foam board system: use the system's washers, spacing and seam sealant. Never mix brands at seams or drains.

10.5 Curbless showers, linear drains, niches and benches

A curbless shower has no step at the entry, so the shower floor has to start below the bathroom subfloor to get its slope. That recess is framing work decided at design time. A linear drain helps: the floor slopes one direction as a single plane, so large-format tile works without the four-way cuts a center drain needs.

Ways to get the recess, in order of preference:

  • Dropped subfloor between joists on sistered cleats, so joists stay whole.
  • Lowered joists framed with hangers, in new construction.
  • Build the rest of the room up when the floor below can't be touched, at the cost of a threshold at the door.
  • Ripping joist tops only with an engineer's detail. Pocketed rowhouse joists are hard to sister to full bearing, so this is last.

On curbless showers, waterproof the whole bathroom floor or at least 3 to 4 ft past the entry, and give the glass a sweep or drip stop.

Niches go on interior walls, never exterior or masonry party walls. Size them to whole tiles, slope the sill into the shower about 1/4 in per foot, and waterproof them continuous with the wall. Benches need solid framing or a foam bench, a sloped seat and waterproofing over top and front; a floating bench needs blocking designed for a person sitting on its front edge.

Common failure

Flat niche sills and bench seats. Water sits, soaks the grout, and finds any pinhole in the membrane. Check every sill and seat with a level before tile. If the bubble is centered, it's wrong.

  2. Dry-lay or calculate. If an end cut is under half a tile, shift half a tile so a tile, not a joint, is centered on the line.
  3. Check focal points. Door threshold, shower entry, vanity front, niches and valves get full tiles or balanced cuts.
  4. Set control lines. Snap working lines every 3 to 4 ft. On walls, set a level ledger for the first full course; the bottom cut goes in last.

Lippage and offset

Lippage is the height difference between adjacent tile edges. ANSI A108.02 allows 1/32 in for joints under 1/4 in wide and 1/16 in for joints 1/4 in and wider, plus the tile's own allowable warpage. Long tile is slightly crowned, so a half-offset puts the high center of one tile next to the low end of the next.

That's why ANSI A108.02 limits the offset to no more than 1/3 of the tile length for running bond when the tile has any side longer than 18 in, unless the tile maker approves otherwise. Leveling clips help with large-format tile but don't fix a substrate that's out of flat.

ESC standard

Any running bond with tile longer than 18 in goes in at 1/3 offset or less, even if the owner's inspiration photo shows half-offset. Grout joints are 1/16 in minimum per ANSI A108.02; we run 1/8 in on rectified porcelain and 3/16 in on calibrated (non-rectified) tile.

10.8 Setting sequence

  1. Prep. Vacuum, prime where required, and confirm 50°F or warmer for 72 hours.
  2. Mix. Measured water, low-speed paddle, slake 5 to 10 minutes, remix. Never re-temper mortar that has started to set.
  3. Walls first. Ledger up, bottom course last, joint at floor or tub left open for sealant.
  4. Floors far wall to door. Trowel only what you can set while the mortar still transfers to a fingertip.
  5. Clean joints. Rake squeeze-out to two-thirds of tile depth before it hardens.
  6. Cure. Usually 24 hours before grout; longer over impervious membranes or in cold rooms.
  7. Grout and seal. Grout (10.9), leaving movement joints open; sealant after grout cures; stone and grout sealer after 48 to 72 hours.

10.10 Heated floors

Electric mats or cable warm bathroom floors for comfort, not primary heat, unless the HVAC design (Chapter 07) counts on them.

  1. Plan. Heat open floor only, never under toilets, vanities or tubs. Size the mat to the net area; cable can't be cut.
  2. Test. Record resistance and insulation resistance on the warranty card before install, after fastening, after embedding and after tile.
  3. Install. Fasten mat or cable to the subfloor or into a compatible uncoupling membrane. Never cross cable over itself.
  4. Sensor. Set the floor sensor between two runs in a conduit so it can be replaced, with a spare beside it.
  5. Embed. Cover with SLC or thinset using a plastic trowel.
  6. Power. The electrician connects a ground-fault-protected circuit per NEC Article 424 (Chapter 08). Don't use the heat to speed cure; wait the mortar maker's time, often 7 to 28 days.

Common failure

A cable nicked during setting and found months later. A continuity alarm clipped to the leads while setting beeps the moment a trowel cuts the wire, while it can still be repaired.

10.11 Natural stone

Stone is softer and more porous than porcelain, and many stones react to acids and to water in the mortar.

  • Stiffness first. L/720 and two layers of plywood (Table 10.1).
  • White mortar for light stone. Gray thinset can shadow through white marble and limestone. Use white thinset and grout that won't stain.
  • Moisture-sensitive stone. Green marble (serpentine) and some resin-backed tiles curl when set in water-based mortar. Use epoxy or a rapid-set mortar the stone supplier approves.
  • Seal before grout. Apply a grout release or the first coat of penetrating sealer before grouting porous stone so grout pigment doesn't lodge in the surface. Seal again after grout cures, and reseal every 1 to 3 years.
  • Cleaning. pH-neutral only on marble, limestone and travertine; note it in the closeout binder.
  • Blend. Dry-lay stone for the owner or architect to approve vein direction before setting.

ESC standard

Every natural stone selection gets a sealed sample board, kept on site, tested with water, oil and lemon juice for 10 minutes each so the owner sees how it stains and etches before it's installed. We note the result and the sealer used in the job file.

10.12 Rowhouse specifics

  • Out-of-level floors. Decide early whether to level the framing (Chapter 04), build up with SLC or mortar, or let tile follow the house. Tile can be flat without being level.
  • Party walls. Never tile on old brick or plaster on brick. Fur out with a gap and use foam board or backer with membrane; brick wicks moisture for years.
  • Stacked baths. A top-floor leak ruins two ceilings, so every pan is flood tested and photographed.
  • Thresholds. Plan saddles or transitions to old wood floors at design time (Chapter 11).

10.15 Common failures and how to avoid them

Common failure

Cracked tile on a bouncy floor. The floor met span tables but missed L/360 or L/720. Check deflection against the tile chosen and fix framing first.

Common failure

Hollow-sounding large tile. Poor coverage from swirled trowel ridges, no back-butter, or skinned mortar. Trowel straight, back-butter, set while the mortar is wet, and pull tiles to check.

Common failure

Leaking shower with a clogged weep system. Mortar packed into the weep holes on a traditional pan, so water backs up under the tile and finds the curb. Protect the weeps, or use a bonded membrane.

Common failure

Tented or popped floor tile. No perimeter or field movement joints, often on a sunny floor or over radiant heat. Follow EJ171 and leave the perimeter open under the base.

Common failure

Screws through the liner or membrane on curbs and the bottom of the wall, from the glass installer or the tile setter. Mark no-fastener zones on the wall and tell the glazing sub where blocking is.

Common failure

Mixed-brand systems. Voided warranties and leaks at the joints. One system per shower.

11.1 Where this work sits in the job

Finish carpentry shows every earlier mistake. A wall 1/2 in out of plumb, a rowhouse floor that dips 3/4 in toward the middle, a box set 1/4 in proud: drywall hides these, millwork exposes them. Our job is to absorb what's left with scribes, shims and reveals so nobody sees it.

The trade runs in two waves. The first follows prime coat on walls (Chapter 09) and tile in wet rooms (Chapter 10): nail-down flooring, doors, casing, cabinets, built-ins, base and stair parts. The second follows final paint: shoe, hardware, adjustments and punch. Floor sanding and finishing sits between the waves. A typical 3,000 sq ft gut renovation takes six to ten weeks of finish carpentry, cabinets and flooring combined.

Code references are to the 2021 International Residential Code (IRC) as a model baseline, and wood floor practice follows National Wood Flooring Association (NWFA) guidelines. Local amendments and the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) govern every requirement in this chapter, and manufacturer instructions govern their products.

Hanging a prehung door

  1. Check the opening. Rough opening is typically door width plus 2 in, and door height plus 2-1/2 in above finished floor. Check the floor for level across the opening and the hinge-side trimmer for plumb both ways.
  2. Set the hinge jamb. Shim behind every hinge with opposing pairs and plumb the jamb both ways with a 6 ft level.
  3. Fix the top hinge. Replace one hinge screw with a 3 in screw through the shim into the jack stud so the door can't sag.
  4. Set head and latch jamb. Shim at the strike, top and bottom until the slab gap is an even 1/8 in. Sight across the jambs for twist.
  5. Nail off and test. 15 or 16 gauge nails through the shims, behind the stop line. Snap shims flush. The door should stay put at any angle and latch without lifting; move the strike rather than forcing the slab.

Pocket and barn doors

Pocket frame rough openings run roughly twice the door width plus 1 in, by door height plus about 4-1/2 in; confirm on the frame maker's sheet before framing. Use a steel-reinforced split-stud frame, a full-width header and soft-close hardware, with 1 in drywall screws over the pocket. Mark the pocket on the as-built plan so nobody hangs a TV there. Original brownstone parlor pocket doors are often 8 to 10 ft tall, and many run on bottom rollers in a floor track. If the wall can be opened, we convert to an overhead track rated for the slab weight; if not, we rebuild the floor track and rollers in place.

Barn door tracks need continuous blocking or a 2x header plate across the full track, and the slab should lap the opening 1 to 2 in each side. Tell the owner up front that a barn door gives no acoustic privacy.

Coping versus mitering

A miter splits a corner into two 45 degree cuts. It looks right on day one, but walls are rarely square, and as wood dries an inside miter opens at the heel. A cope runs one piece square into the corner and cuts the second piece to the profile of the first, like a puzzle piece. When the wood shrinks, the coped piece slides along the face and the joint stays closed.

Outside corners are always mitered, glued and pinned with 23 gauge pins. Long runs join with a glued 45 degree scarf over a stud, never a butt joint. Cut casing miters at the measured half-angle: a head-to-leg joint on an out-of-square door may need 44 or 46 degrees.

Casing and base installation

  1. Mark the reveal. Tick the jamb edge at 3/16 in with a reveal gauge at the head corners and along the legs.
  2. Head first, then legs. Dry-fit and adjust until each miter closes front and back. Glue and pin the miters before nailing legs to the wall.
  3. Nail. 18 gauge 1-1/4 in into the jamb every 12 in; 15 or 16 gauge 2 to 2-1/2 in into framing every 16 in. Over plaster on masonry, use trim screws or adhesive into furring; nails into brick crack the plaster.
  4. Plane, don't shim. Where a jamb stands proud of the wall, plane the jamb edge or back-cut the casing.
  5. Base. Run base square into corners on the walls seen first from the entry and cope the returning pieces.
  6. Scribe to floors and walls. Where a gap exceeds 1/8 in, scribe with a compass and plane or sand to the line. On sloping rowhouse floors, either keep the base top level and scribe the bottom, or follow the floor; decide per room and keep it consistent across sight lines.
  7. Shoe last. After floor finish, nailed to the base only so the floor can move.

Historic brownstone millwork

Parlor-floor woodwork is often walnut, chestnut, mahogany or oak under layers of paint, with built-up casings, plinth blocks and folding shutters in the window reveals. Upper floors were usually painted pine. We number, photograph and profile every piece before demo (Chapter 02).

Common failure

Paint on pre-1978 trim is presumed to contain lead. Work follows the EPA Renovation, Repair and Painting Rule with a certified renovator: no open-flame burning, no heat guns at or above 1,100 °F, and no power sanding or planing without HEPA shroud exhaust. Plan it in preconstruction (Chapter 01).

  • Strip in place with chemical strippers (no methylene chloride) or a low-temperature infrared stripper when the trim can't come off without damage.
  • Strip off site in a controlled shop. Dip stripping is fast but raises grain and loosens glue joints; ask for a test piece.
  • Replicate profiles. A shop grinds custom knives from a profile gauge reading or a 2 in sample cut from a hidden end. Order 15 to 20 percent extra length; patching always uses more than measured.
  • Dutchmen of matching species and grain fix rot, burns and nail damage on stain-grade work. Filler is for paint grade only.

Restoring rowhouse stairs

The typical 19th-century rowhouse stair has a housed wall stringer let into the party wall, an open outside stringer with mitered risers, dovetailed balusters and a continuous rail ramping to a newel or volute at the parlor floor.   3. Repair treads and rail. Dutchman worn nosings in matching species or replace treads with the original profile. Re-glue loose balusters and tighten or replace the newel bolt; rails and guards must resist a 200 lb concentrated load.

ESC standard

Every stair, new or restored, gets a riser and tread survey sheet signed by the lead before finish parts go on: each riser and tread measured, maximum variation noted, headroom checked at the header.

Moisture and acclimation

Wood floors fail from moisture far more often than from installation. Measure; don't count days.

  • Wood subfloor: 12 percent or less, about 20 readings per 1,000 sq ft. Subfloor and flooring within 4 points for strip under 3 in wide, within 2 points for plank 3 in and wider (NWFA).
  • Flooring: acclimate in the rooms it goes in until readings stabilize near expected in-service moisture, often 6 to 9 percent; typically 3 to 7 days. Engineered and LVP follow the manufacturer.
  • Concrete: in-situ RH test to ASTM F2170 or calcium chloride to ASTM F1869, read against the product limit (glue-down limits are commonly 75 to 85 percent RH). New slabs often need 60 to 90 days to test dry.
  • Below grade and crawlspaces: vapor retarder on the slab or crawl floor (Chapter 05). No solid strip on slabs below grade.

Nail-down installation

  1. Flatten the subfloor. Within 1/4 in in 10 ft or 3/16 in in 6 ft. Plane high seams, fill lows, screw down squeaks.
  2. Underlayment. #15 asphalt felt or the maker's vapor retarder, lapped 4 in. Rosin paper keeps a floor clean but isn't a retarder.
  3. Layout. Run strip perpendicular to joists where possible; parallel runs need added plywood or blocking per NWFA. Start off a straight line so the last row isn't a sliver.
  4. Field. Face-nail starter rows 1/2 to 3/4 in off the wall, then blind-nail at 45 degrees through the tongue. Rack out rows ahead to stagger joints at least 6 in and avoid H-patterns.
  5. Transitions. Thresholds, reducers and flush vents are planned at layout. Wood flush to tile is set by adjusting the tile bed (Chapter 10).

Radiant floors

Use engineered flooring rated for radiant, or narrow (3 in or less) quartersawn solid in a stable species. 11.11 Common failures and how to avoid them

  • Cupped or gapped floors. Wood installed before the house was dry, or over a damp basement or crawlspace. Measure, run HVAC first, fix the source. Don't sand a cupped floor until moisture is back in range, or it crowns later.
  • Doors that drift or won't latch. Hinge jamb not plumb, or top hinge not screwed to framing. Use a 6 ft level, not a 2 ft one.
  • Open miters a month after paint. Unglued miters on trim that wasn't acclimated.
  • Shims showing under cabinets. Toe-kick not scribed. When the floor drops more than 1/2 in on a run, order taller toe-kicks or leveling legs.
  • Floating floor buckling at the kitchen. Cabinets set on top of LVP. Set cabinets first and float the floor to them.
  • Finish defects: bubbles, dust nibs, lap marks. Trades in the house during coats, HVAC blowing dust, or finish applied outside its temperature range. Close the house while finishing.

11.12 QC checklist and final punch

  • Every door swings, stays at any angle, latches without lift and clears rugs. Gaps even, stops tight.
  • Casing reveals even; miters closed and filled; colored putty on stain grade.
  • Base tight to wall and floor; copes closed; shoe nailed to base.
  • Cabinet doors and drawers aligned and adjusted after counters are on; spare hardware bagged and labeled.
  • Countertops level, seams flat and tight, overhangs supported.
  • Stair rail continuous and solid, balusters tight, no squeaks on a walk test, survey sheet in file.
  • Floor free of chatter and swirls under raking light; even sheen; vents and transitions set.
  • Owner gets the finish system name and care products, felt pads for furniture, 1 to 2 boxes of spare flooring and an 8 ft length of each trim profile.

Final punch is a walk with the owner and PM using painter's tape, one piece per item. Trades work the list in order of dust: carpentry adjustments, then paint touch-up, then a floor buff or maintenance coat if needed.

11.13 Handoff

This is the last trade in most rooms. When we leave, the room is ready to move into:

  • Doors hung and adjusted, hardware on, keys tagged.
  • Trim installed, filled, caulked and painted.
  • Cabinets, built-ins and counters complete, with sink and appliances connected by their trades (Chapters 06 and 08).
  • Stairs complete with handrails and guards, inspected.
  • Floors cured past light-use time, covered with breathable protection if trades still need access, and the rug and furniture schedule written down for the owner.
  • Room broom-clean, punch tape gone, care documents in the owner's binder.

Anything still open goes on the PM's closeout list (Chapter 01) with a date and a responsible trade.
